\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A lattice L\'evy walk moves on $\Z$ in runs of alternating direction. The
run lengths are independent, and their tails are regularly varying with an
index $\alpha$ between $1$ and $2$, so a run has a finite mean and an
infinite variance. Let $A_N$ be the number of right steps among the first
$N$. The position of the walk after $N$ steps is $2A_N-N$, so the law
$P_N(k)=\Prob(A_N=k)$ on $\{0,\dots,N\}$ is the law of the walk read on its
natural lattice. We describe $P_N(k)$ at every point $k$, with one exception
that we state below. In the bulk $P_N$ is a stable density, uniformly over the
range of the count (Theorem~\ref{thm:bulk}). On each side of the bulk and up
to the endpoint it is given by one kernel, which adds a complete giant run and
the run in progress at time $N$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:bigjump}). Read at $k=vN$ this
kernel is a rigorous lattice version of the infinite density of the L\'evy
walk~\cite{rdhb2014,rdhb2014b}, with its boundary layer and its endpoint
atoms. L\'evy walks are a standard model of superdiffusion~\cite{zdk2015}, and
the same walk describes heat transport in one-dimensional
lattices~\cite{dsd2013,wlh2016} and the occupation time of an alternating
renewal process~\cite{godrecheluck2001,lamperti1958}. In continuous time the
propagator of a L\'evy walk is a stable bulk between two ballistic peaks at the
fronts, which are formed by the walkers still in their first
flight~\cite[Section~II.A.2 and Fig.~2]{zdk2015} (see also~\cite{zdh2011,dzh2012}).
On the lattice these peaks are the endpoint atoms and the boundary layers. The
picture is known. What we add is its exact form at a fixed time.

\subsection{The picture}\label{sec:intro-picture}

Figure~\ref{fig:atlas} shows $P_N$ for $N=5000$ on a logarithmic scale.
Four pieces make up the law. We call a right step a plus step.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item The bulk. Near $pN$, where $p$ is the long-run fraction of plus
steps, the law is a stable density at the scale $B_N$ of the larger of the
two run-length fluctuations. Here $B_N$ grows like $N^{1/\alpha_*}$ up to a
slowly varying factor, with $\alpha_*$ the smaller index.
\item The big-jump zones. A count $k$ with $k-pN$ much larger than $B_N$
needs one plus run of macroscopic length, a giant run, and the law of that run
gives $P_N(k)$ its size, which is of order $T_\plus(N)$ on the ballistic range,
with $T_\plus$ the tail of a plus run. The giant run is either a complete run in
the interior or the run in progress at time $N$, and $P_N(k)$ is the sum of the
two contributions.
\item The boundary layers. At $k=N-j$ with $j$ fixed, the giant run is the one
in progress at time $N$, and the only question is where it started. It starts
right after the $j$-th minus step exactly when a minus run ends there, so the
layer is $T_\plus(N)$ times the renewal mass function $u_\minus(j)$ of the minus
runs.
\item The atoms. The point $k=N$ is the event that the first run covers all
$N$ steps, and its mass is the tail of the first run.
\end{itemize}
The first run of the walk is its initial run. It may be an ordinary run, a run
seen from a typical time, or anything else, and it enters each of the four
pieces through its own criterion.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:intro-results}

We state the hypotheses in Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}. They are regular
variation of the two tails with indices in $(1,2)$, written (RV), the local
form $f_\sgn(n)\sim\alpha_\sgn T_\sgn(n)/n$ of the mass functions, written
(LR), and aperiodicity, written (SPAN). Section~\ref{sec:extrema} also uses a bound
on the differences of the mass functions, written (LD). The run laws may differ between the
two signs, so the two stable scales $b_{\plus,N}$ and $b_{\minus,N}$ may
differ, and their ratio may oscillate. Every theorem below except
Theorem~\ref{thm:window} holds for every relation between the two scales. We write $s$ for the sum of the two mean run
lengths and $p$ for the fraction of the plus mean.

\emph{The bulk.} Theorem~\ref{thm:bulk} says that for a fresh start, under
(RV) and (SPAN),
\[
 \sup_{k\in\Z}\bigl|B_NP_N(k)-h_N\bigl((k-pN)/B_N\bigr)\bigr|\to0,
 \qquad \sup_kP_N(k)\le\frac C{B_N},
\]
where $h_N$ is the density of $(1-p)\theta_{\plus,N}Z_\plus-p\,\theta_{\minus,N}Z_\minus$,
with $Z_\pm$ independent totally skewed stable variables. The supremum runs
over the whole range of the count. On a window $|k-pN|\le KB_N$ the local
limit also follows from a strong renewal theorem of Berger, and what is new is
the uniformity over every point under (RV) and (SPAN) alone. Outside the bulk
the theorem says only that $P_N(k)=o(1/B_N)$, and the next theorem says what
$P_N(k)$ is there.

\emph{Big jumps and the boundary layer.} Put $L_k=(k-pN)/(1-p)$, the length
of the plus run that carries a typical configuration to the count $k$.
Theorem~\ref{thm:bigjump} says that for a fresh plus start, under (RV) and
(SPAN) for both signs and (LR) for the plus sign,
\[
 P_N(k)=\Bigl[\gamma(N-k)\,f_\plus(\lfloor L_k\rfloor)+T_\plus(\lfloor L_k\rfloor)\,u_\minus(N-k)\Bigr](1+o(1))
\]
uniformly on $pN+K\lambda_NB_N\le k\le N$, in the sense that the error
tends to zero as $N\to\infty$ and then $K\to\infty$. Here $\gamma(j)$ is an
explicit finite sum with $\gamma(j)\sim js/\mu_\minus^2$, and
$\lambda_N=1+\log(B_N/b_{\plus,N})$, which equals $1$ when the plus scale is the
larger one. The first term is a complete giant run and the second the run in
progress at time $N$. At $k=N-j$ with $j$ fixed the theorem reduces to
$P_N(N-j)/T_\plus(N)\to u_\minus(j)$, which needs only (RV) for the plus sign.
As $j$ grows, $u_\minus(j)\to1/\mu_\minus$ and the layer joins the big-jump zone
continuously. When the plus scale is the larger one, the bulk profile up to a
slowly growing multiple of $B_N$ and the kernel beyond it give the whole plus
side in a single limit, while no fixed switching point does
(Remark~\ref{rem:no-single-formula}). When the plus scale is the smaller one, the
window $KB_N\le k-pN\le K\lambda_NB_N$ lies between the two theorems, and in general
we have no relative error there. This window is the exception announced above. For
a fresh start with $b_{\minus,N}/b_{\plus,N}\to\infty$, Theorem~\ref{thm:window}
narrows it. The kernel takes over at $pN+(1+\delta)\xi_NB_N$ and not at
$pN+(1-\delta)\xi_NB_N$, where $\xi_N=o(\lambda_N)$ comes from a Cram\'er transform of
the minus runs. So the start of the zone is sharp up to the factor $1\pm\delta$. Before
it the law is a moderate deviation of the minus runs, and the theorem describes it on
the logarithmic scale. For sums of independent variables with a heavy left tail of
index in $(1,2)$ and a lighter right tail, the same crossover at a logarithmic
multiple of the stable scale is due to Rozovski\u{\i}~\cite{rozovskii1997} for the
tail and to Denisov, Dieker and Shneer~\cite[Section~9.2]{dds2008} for local
probabilities. What Theorem~\ref{thm:window} adds is the count at a fixed time, where
the number of runs is random and the big jump may be made by the run in progress at
time $N$. The mirror window appears at the minus end when the minus scale is the
smaller one. (LR)
suffices for the theorem, and
Example~\ref{ex:rv-not-lr} shows that (RV) alone does not, since the kernel
reads $f_\plus$ at a single point. This parallels the optimality of the local
hypothesis for sums~\cite[Eq.~(2.4)]{berger2019ptrf}. Read at $k=vN$ the
theorem gives the infinite density
\[
 \frac{P_N(\lfloor vN\rfloor)}{T_\plus(N)}\to
 \frac1{\mu_\minus}\Bigl[\alpha_\plus(1-\ell)\ell^{-\alpha_\plus-1}+\ell^{-\alpha_\plus}\Bigr],
 \qquad \ell=\frac{v-p}{1-p},
\]
where the second term is the run in progress at time $N$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:infinite-density}).

\emph{The initial run.} Wang, Schulz, Deng and Barkai~\cite{wsdb2018} left
``the effect of initial preparation on the rare fluctuations'' for future work.
Their question covers all the observables of their paper and also the range
$0<\alpha<1$. Theorem~\ref{thm:preparation} answers it for the occupation count
of the lattice walk with $1<\alpha<2$, for every law of the initial run and on
every piece of the law. It rests on the first-run decomposition of
Proposition~\ref{prop:first-run}. Write $\nu_\sgn(n)$ for the probability
that the first run has sign $\sgn$ and length $n$, and $Q_\sgn(n)$ for the
probability that it has sign $\sgn$ and length at least $n$. On every
macroscopic interior of the range of the count the bulk approximation holds with no condition
on the initial run. It holds uniformly over $1\le k<N$ exactly when
$B_n(\nu_\plus(n)+\nu_\minus(n))\to0$, which we call the bulk criterion. The
initial run is invisible in the boundary layer at the plus end exactly when
$\nu_\plus(n)=o(T_\plus(n))$. On the
big-jump zone up to the end the same condition is sharp, while on its interior
only window averages of $\nu_\plus$ matter. The conditions are nested, and when
one fails the theorem gives the exact correction. The stationary initial run satisfies the bulk criterion and fails the
other two, and Corollary~\ref{cor:stationary} gives its corrections in closed
form. Corollary~\ref{cor:no-trim} shows that no trimming of the range
$\{0,\dots,N\}$ by $o(N)$ points removes the dependence on the initial run for
every initial run at once.

\emph{The shape.} Theorem~\ref{thm:mode} compares the endpoint atoms with
the bulk for an initial run that satisfies the bulk criterion. An endpoint is
the global mode when its atom exceeds $H_N/B_N$ by a fixed factor, with $H_N$
the height of the profile, and the bulk wins when both atoms are smaller by a
fixed factor. For the stationary initial run
with equal indices this is the golden ratio threshold. The endpoints win for
$\alpha<\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$, the bulk wins for $\alpha>\varphi$, and at
$\alpha=\varphi$ with pure power tails the bulk wins by the explicit factor
$1/\mathcal C(\varphi)=1.288\ldots$, with $\mathcal C$ as in~\eqref{eq:Calpha}.

Theorem~\ref{thm:antimode} locates the interior minimum between the bulk and
the plus end for a fresh plus start. If $u_\minus(j)>1/\mu_\minus$ for every
$j\ge1$, which holds for every strictly log-convex law of the minus runs, the
minimum sits at $N-j^*_N$ with $T_\minus(j^*_N)\sim2\alpha_\plus s/N$, so $j^*_N$
grows like the stable scale of the minus runs. It is asymptotic to the minimizer
of $u_\minus(j)+C(j)/N$, with $C(j)$ the exact second-order coefficient of
Theorem~\ref{thm:second-order}. When $u_\minus$ dips below its limit the
minimum stays at bounded distance from the end.

Section~\ref{sec:extrema} compares neighbouring values. A difference form of the renewal
theorem, $u_\minus(j)-u_\minus(j-1)\sim-T_\minus(j)/\mu_\minus^2$ under (RV) and (SPAN),
makes the end profile eventually monotone whenever the initial run has
$\nu_\minus(n)=(c+o(1))T_\minus(n)$ with $c\ne1/\mu_\minus$, in particular when
$\nu_\minus=o(T_\minus)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:prep-diff}). Each local maximum of the profile at an end is copied by $P_N$. So when
$\nu_\sgn=o(T_\sgn)$ for both signs, $P_N$ has at least one mode more than the two
profiles together (Theorem~\ref{thm:modes-lower}). Some prepared starts of a log-convex
law have at least four modes, for instance a fresh plus run or a minus run of fixed
length at least $2$, each with probability $\frac12$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:four-modes}). Under (LD) we prove a first-difference form of
Theorem~\ref{thm:second-order}. Up to distance $N^{1-\eta}$ from each end the local extrema
of $P_N$ are then those of the profile, followed by an odd number of extrema in a window of
width $2\eta'a_\minus(N)$ around the interior minimum, for every $\eta'>0$, and $P_N$ is
strictly monotone in between (Theorem~\ref{thm:ends}). We do not prove that the window
holds a single minimum, nor that $P_N$ is monotone from there to the bulk.
Conjecture~\ref{conj:three-modes} states the matching upper bound, so that for all
large $N$ the number of modes would be exactly one more than the two profiles have
together. Finally, the ends
identify both run laws (Remark~\ref{thm:inverse}).

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:intro-related}

\emph{Infinite densities and big jumps.} Rebenshtok, Denisov, H\"anggi and
Barkai~\cite{rdhb2014,rdhb2014b} derived the infinite density of a L\'evy
walk from the Montroll and Weiss equation, formally and in continuous time.
For the two-state velocity model their density
\cite[Eqs.~(8) and~(10)]{rdhb2014} has the shape
$|v|^{-1-\alpha}[\alpha-(\alpha-1)|v|]$. Schulz and Barkai~\cite[Eqs.~(25)
and~(26)]{sb2015} found the infinite density of the occupation time of a site
for an alternating process with two sojourn laws and matched it to the stable
bulk, and Wang, Schulz, Deng and Barkai~\cite[Eq.~(83)]{wsdb2018} found it for
one law. A complete giant run alone would give the linear term $-\alpha|v|$,
and the run in progress adds $+|v|$, so these formulas already contain the run
in progress. Vezzani, Barkai and Burioni~\cite[Eqs.~(12) and~(13)]{vbb2019}
(see also~\cite{vbb2020}) derived the tail from a single big jump as the sum of
exactly these two terms, a jump still in progress at the observation time and
a completed jump that starts at the renewal rate. So the shape of the infinite
density and its two-term mechanism are known formally. What
Theorem~\ref{thm:bigjump} and Corollary~\ref{cor:infinite-density} add is a proof
on the lattice at a fixed time, with the exact kernels $\gamma$ and $\kappa$ at
finite distance from the end, uniformly up to the endpoint, together with the
boundary layer and the role of the local hypothesis. In the physics model each
flight chooses its direction independently, and consecutive flights in the same
direction merge into one run. This gives the alternating walk with run laws
thinned geometrically with parameter $\frac12$, as in Remark~\ref{rem:thinning}.

The paper~\cite{wsdb2018} works in continuous time through a double Laplace
transform, with one law for both states and a start of fair random sign. It
finds a boundary layer of tail times renewal density for the backward
recurrence time when $1<\alpha<2$ \cite[Eq.~(52)]{wsdb2018} and an atom with a
boundary layer for the occupation time when $0<\alpha<1$
\cite[Section~VII.A]{wsdb2018}. Its authors write that ``the effect of initial
preparation on the rare fluctuations is left for a future
work''~\cite[Section~VIII]{wsdb2018}. Wang, Vezzani, Burioni and
Barkai~\cite{wvbb2019} then compared the ordinary and the equilibrated start
formally for a biased continuous-time random walk. They found that the rare
fluctuations remember the start, through an added term $\xi^{-\alpha}$ in the
infinite density and an atom at the origin \cite[Eqs.~(28), (46)
and~(47)]{wvbb2019}. This is the structure of
Corollary~\ref{cor:stationary}(a) and~(b), where the stationary initial run adds
the complete initial run to the kernel. Theorem~\ref{thm:preparation} treats an
arbitrary initial run at every point, with a sharp criterion in each zone for
when the start is invisible and the exact correction when it is not. Wang,
H\"oll and Barkai~\cite{whb2020} treat the infinite-mean case.
Akimoto~\cite{akimoto2023} computes the moments of the occupation time for the
ordinary and the equilibrium start and shows that they differ for
$1<\alpha<2$. The classical integral limit theorems for the sojourn time of an
alternating renewal process are due to Tak\'acs~\cite{takacs1957,takacs1959} and
Kesten~\cite{kesten1962}, and they are statements in distribution, while every
statement here concerns single points. The
golden ratio appears in Wang, Li and H\"anggi~\cite{wlh2016} as the exponent
at which the side peaks and the central peak decay at the same rate, with the
edge exponent of Cipriani, Denisov and Politi~\cite{cdp2005}, and
Lee-Dadswell, Nickel and Gray~\cite{ldng2005} found the conduction exponent
$(3-\sqrt5)/2$ by a hydrodynamic argument. The threshold itself is the
exponent identity $\alpha-1=1/\alpha$. What Theorem~\ref{thm:mode} adds is the
comparison of single points as a theorem and the constant $\mathcal C(\varphi)$.

\emph{Local limit theorems and renewal theory.} Doney~\cite{doney1991} proved
a bivariate local limit theorem in the stable domain, and
Berger~\cite{berger2019} proved multivariate local large deviations and strong
renewal theorems. His Theorem~3.3 applied to the walk of run lengths, together
with the fresh-plus identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:fresh-identity}, gives
the window $|k-pN|\le KB_N$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:bulk}, and Section~\ref{sec:bulk}
explains what the rest of the theorem adds and why it needs new work. Melbourne,
P\`ene and Terhesiu~\cite{mpt2026} prove mixing local limit theorems with
multivariate stable limits on Young towers under the invariant measure. For the
walk they give one point at the stable scale for the stationary initial run,
with the first and the last run restricted to fixed lengths. In the i.i.d.\
renewal setting Theorems~\ref{thm:bulk} and~\ref{thm:preparation} remove these
restrictions. We use the local large deviation bound of Caravenna and
Doney~\cite{caravennadoney2019} (see also~\cite{melbourneterhesiu2022}) for sums
of a fixed number of variables. The local one-big-jump theorems for
sums of independent variables are due to Denisov, Dieker and
Shneer~\cite{dds2008} and Berger, Birkner and Yuan~\cite{bby2023}, and the one
under exactly the local condition (LR) is~\cite[Theorem~2.4]{berger2019ptrf}.
That theorem gives the probability that a fixed number of plus runs adds up to a
given large value. It does not give Theorem~\ref{thm:bigjump} directly. The
number of runs before step $N$ is random and tied to the run lengths, the big
jump may be made by the run in progress at time $N$, which no statement about
complete sums sees, and the kernel must hold uniformly up to the endpoint, where
only boundedly many minus steps remain. The proof of
Theorem~\ref{thm:bigjump} does not use these theorems. What is new for the local
big jump is the count at a fixed time, the run in progress at time $N$, the
exact constants $\gamma(j)$ and $\kappa(j)$, and the uniformity up to the
endpoint. The second-order coefficient rests on the second-order renewal theorem of
Frenk~\cite{frenk1982}, see also Rogozin~\cite{rogozin1973} and
Doney~\cite{doney2023}, and the monotonicity of $u_\minus$ on de Bruijn and
Erd\H{o}s~\cite{debruijnerdos1953}.

\emph{The walk.} The walk is the directionally reinforced random walk of
Mauldin, Monticino and von Weizs\"acker~\cite{mmw1996}, with limit laws by
Horv\'ath and Shao~\cite{horvathshao1998}, and the persistent random walk of
C\'enac, Le Ny, de Loynes and Offret~\cite{clnlo2018,clnlo2019}. For geometric run
lengths its crossings and a related walk with a Landau profile are treated
in~\cite{paperA,paperD}. Magdziarz, Scheffler, Straka and
\.Zebrowski~\cite{mssz2015} prove limit theorems for L\'evy walks in continuous
space and time. Theorem~\ref{thm:bulk} is the local form of the marginal of these
limits at one time. Godr\`eche and Luck~\cite{godrecheluck2001} give the transform
of the occupation time, which is the generating function of the fresh-plus
identity, and Lamperti~\cite{lamperti1958} proved the arcsine type law for
infinite-mean runs.

\emph{Organization.} Everything rests on the exact identities of
Section~\ref{sec:setting}, which write $P_N(k)$ through products of renewal kernels
of the two signs and reduce every initial run to the two fresh laws.
Sections~\ref{sec:bulk} to~\ref{sec:shape} prove Theorems~\ref{thm:bulk}
to~\ref{thm:antimode} in order, Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the
computations, and Appendix~\ref{app:classical} collects classical inputs.
