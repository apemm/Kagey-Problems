\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A persistent random walk on the square lattice $\Z^2$ moves one step east,
north, west or south at a time. It starts in a uniform direction, and at each
step it keeps its direction, turns left or right, or reverses, with given
probabilities. Its continuous-time analogue is a planar random motion with
orthogonal directions~\cite{cinqueorsingher2023,orsingher2000,kolesnikorsingher2002}.
In physics such motions model run-and-tumble particles with four orthogonal
directions~\cite{tailleurcates2008,santra2020,angelani2022}. After $N$ steps the corner $(N,0)$
is reached only by the path that starts east and never switches, while the origin is reached
by many paths that switch. We ask at which switching rate the two are equally
likely. In the continuum the corners carry atoms and the origin does not (it
has a density in the plane, and with reversals also along the
axes~\cite{kolesnikorsingher2002}), so the comparison of a corner with the
origin is a lattice question.

On the line the same question compares an endpoint with the middle. The
persistent random walk on the line~\cite{goldstein1951,kac1974,renshawhenderson1981,paperA}, which we call the line walk,
steps right or left. Its first step is fair, and each later step repeats the
previous one with probability $p$ and switches with probability $q=1-p$. An
endpoint is reached by a single path, the one that never switches, so it has
probability $\frac12p^{N-1}$. When $p$ is close to $1$ this path wins, and when
$p$ is smaller the middle wins. For the line walk this question is part of
Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131}, posed for a
Galton board. It is answered at every $N$ in~\cite{paperA}. The middle and an
endpoint are equally likely when $Nq$, which is close to the expected number
$(N-1)q$ of switches, is about $\log N$ (the ratio of the two tends to $1$). More precisely, the crossing value $z_N$ of $Nq$ satisfies
$z_N+\frac12\log z_N=\log N+\frac12\log\frac\pi8+o(1)$.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:results}

Turns and reversals are rare. Fix $T>0$. A turn to each side has probability
$rT/N$ and a reversal has probability $sT/N$, where $r,s\ge0$ are fixed rates
with $2r+s>0$. Figure~\ref{fig:planarpaths} shows the paths to the corner and
to the origin. We show that for every even $N\ge4$ there is exactly one value
$T^*_N$ of $T$ at which the corner $(N,0)$ and the origin are equally likely
(Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp}). The corner is more likely below it and the
origin above it, and as $N\to\infty$ through even integers
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-limit}
 \frac{T^*_N}{\log N}\to\min\Bigl(\frac2{2r+s},\frac1s\Bigr)
\end{equation}
(Theorems~\ref{thm:planar-first} and~\ref{thm:planar-rev}, with $1/0=\infty$). The two values in
the minimum belong to two kinds of paths to the origin.

\emph{Why two values.} The face selection rule of~\cite{paperC} for slowly
switching Markov chains explains them. Call a set of directions a face, and
call the vector of the numbers of steps in each direction the composition of a
path. If $T=\tau\log N$, the most likely composition that uses exactly the
directions of a face $F$ has probability about
$N^{-\tau\lambda_F-(\lvert F\rvert-1)}$, where $\lambda_F$ is the rate at which
the direction leaves $F$. The first term is the cost of never leaving $F$, and
the second is the cost of sharing the mass of $F$ among about
$N^{\lvert F\rvert-1}$ compositions. The planar walk fits this picture. The
corner is the face of the single direction east, with exit rate $2r+s$, so it
has probability about $N^{-(2r+s)\tau}$. A path to the origin either stays on
one axis, a face of two directions with exit rate $2r$, or uses all four
directions, a face with exit rate $0$. On an axis the origin is a single
composition of that face, of probability about $N^{-(2r\tau+1)}$. With all four
directions a single composition of the face has probability about $N^{-3}$, but
about $N/2$ of them end at the origin, which gives $N^{-2}$. This suggests that
the origin beats the corner when $(2r+s)\tau>\min(2r\tau+1,2)$, which
is~\eqref{eq:intro-limit}. It is only a heuristic, since the origin is not a
single composition of the full face, and Section~\ref{sec:planar} proves the
limit directly.

\emph{Second order.} The mass at
the origin splits exactly into the paths that stay on one axis and the paths
that use all four directions (Proposition~\ref{prop:decomp}). When reversals
dominate ($s>2r$) the axis paths carry the origin mass at the crossing. Then
$sT^*_N=z_{N/2}+O(N^{-\gamma})$ for some $\gamma>0$, where $z_{N/2}$ is the
crossing of the line walk with $N/2$ steps (Theorem~\ref{thm:planar-rev}).
When turns dominate ($s<2r$) the paths with all four directions carry it.
Their mass is given by a local limit theorem in two dimensions
(Theorem~\ref{thm:fourdir}), and the crossing then satisfies
\[
 (2r+s)\,T^*_N+\log T^*_N=2\log N+\log\frac\pi{8(r+s)}+o(1)
\]
(Corollary~\ref{cor:planar-turn}). Here $\log T^*_N$ has the coefficient $1$,
while the line walk has $\frac12$, because the mass at the origin now comes from a local limit
theorem in two dimensions.

\emph{Two copies of the line walk.} There is one curve of switching probabilities on which
everything is exact. If
the walk turns to each side with probability $\rho(1-\rho)$ and reverses with
probability $\rho^2$, its two diagonal coordinates are independent copies of
the line walk with switching probability $\rho$. So on this curve the planar
crossing is the crossing of the line walk at every $N$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:product}).
With turns only ($s=0$) we show that $rT^*_N=z_N+O((\log N)^2/N)$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:pureturns}).

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

On the lattice the correlated random walk goes back to
Gillis~\cite{gillis1955}. Chen and Renshaw~\cite{chenrenshaw1992} treat the
Gillis--Domb--Fisher walk, and in~\cite{chenrenshaw1994} they show that the
characteristic function of any correlated random walk satisfies a
recurrence. So in principle the law of our planar walk is known. Marris and
Giuggioli~\cite{marris2024} give lattice propagators for walks with forward,
backward and lateral steps. Lenci~\cite{lenci2007} proves recurrence for a
large class of persistent walks on the square lattice.

In the continuum, the law of a run-and-tumble particle on the line has atoms at
the two ends of its support~\cite{malakar2018}. In the plane, Santra, Basu and
Sabhapandit~\cite{santra2020} find the marginals of a run-and-tumble particle
with four directions, and Angelani~\cite{angelani2022} treats switching among
four orthogonal directions. With turns only, the continuous motion is a
rotation of two independent telegraph processes (Cinque and
Orsingher~\cite[Theorem~2.2]{cinqueorsingher2023}, who credit
Orsingher~\cite{orsingher2000} for constant rates, and Smith, Le Doussal,
Majumdar and Schehr~\cite{sldms2022}). Marchione and
Orsingher~\cite{marchioneorsingher2024} study orthogonal directions that change
at Poisson times by clockwise or counterclockwise turns, write each coordinate
as a linear combination of two independent telegraph processes, and obtain the
exact distribution, including a singular part on the boundary. With reversals
the two processes of the rotation are no longer
independent~\cite[Section~3]{cinqueorsingher2023}. The curve of
Proposition~\ref{prop:product}, on which the planar walk splits into two
independent line walks, is the lattice analogue of these reductions. But on
the lattice the independent case has reversals.

Orsingher and Marchione~\cite{orsinghermarchione2025} study a continuous-time
planar motion with orthogonal directions that turns clockwise, turns
counterclockwise or reverses, each with its own probability. Our walk, in which
the two turns are equally likely, is a discrete-time lattice analogue of this
motion. They derive the singular distributions on the edges and diagonals of
the square that bounds the motion, and its hydrodynamic limit. We did not find
the planar crossing of the corner and the origin in the literature.

\subsection{Plan of the paper}\label{sec:plan}

Section~\ref{sec:notation} fixes the notation for the line walk and recalls its crossing equation.
Section~\ref{sec:planar} treats the planar walk for general rates. It splits the origin mass,
proves the first-order answer, and locates the crossing when reversals dominate and when turns
dominate. Section~\ref{sec:product} (within Section~\ref{sec:planar}) treats the product curve
and the walk with turns only.
Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the computer checks and the open questions, and
Appendix~\ref{app:planar} has the longer proofs. From~\cite{paperA} we use the
fact that the bin polynomials grow toward the middle, the uniform Bessel bound
(recalled in~\eqref{eq:planar-bessel}), the bound $z_N\le\log N$ and the expansion of $z_N$. The proofs do not
use~\cite{paperC}, which is the source of the heuristic above.
