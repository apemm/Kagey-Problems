\section{The exact law}\label{sec:exact}

This section answers Kagey's questions about the law of the bins, his
numerator tables and the normal limit. The results are classical in
substance~\cite{renshaw1981,dekkingkong2011}, and we prove them because the
later sections use the count of words by their number of switches. Recall \eqref{eq:wordprob}: a word with $j$ switches has
probability $\frac12p^{N-1-j}q^j$. We also write $\rho=2p-1=p-q$, the
correlation $\E\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1}$ of 2 consecutive steps.

\begin{theorem}[bin probabilities]\label{thm:pmf}
Let $N\ge1$. Then $P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\tfrac12p^{N-1}$, and for $1\le k\le N-1$
\[
P_N(k)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}W(N,k,j)\,p^{N-1-j}q^{\,j},
\]
where, writing $a=k$ and $b=N-k$,
\[
W(N,k,2i-1)=2\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i-1},\qquad
W(N,k,2i)=\binom{a-1}{i}\binom{b-1}{i-1}+\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i}
\]
is the number of words with $a$ letters $\sR$, $b$ letters $\sL$ and exactly
$j$ switches.
\end{theorem}

In words, we sort the words in bin $k$ by their number of switches, and count
each class by its run lengths.

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:wordprob} it suffices to count words. A word with $j$ switches
consists of $j+1$ maximal runs, alternately of $\sR$'s and of $\sL$'s. If
$k\in\{0,N\}$ there is 1 word and it has no switch. Let $a,b\ge1$. If $j+1=2i$
there are $i$ runs of each letter and the first letter is free. The run lengths
form a composition of $a$ into $i$ parts and a composition of $b$ into $i$
parts, and there are $\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i-1}$ such pairs. If
$j+1=2i+1$ the word starts and ends with the same letter. Starting with $\sR$
gives $i+1$ runs of $\sR$ and $i$ runs of $\sL$, so
$\binom{a-1}{i}\binom{b-1}{i-1}$ words, and starting with $\sL$ gives the
other summand.
\end{proof}

For instance $P_2=(\tfrac p2,\,q,\,\tfrac p2)$, $P_3(1)=\tfrac12(1-p^2)$ and
$P_4(2)=q(1-p+p^2)$. Each interior bin needs at least 1 switch, while the
endpoints need none. So the endpoints win when $p$ is close to $1$, and
Section~\ref{sec:middle} asks how close.

\begin{proposition}[recurrence and generating function]\label{prop:gf}
Let $P_N^{\sR}(k)$ and $P_N^{\sL}(k)$ be the probabilities of being in bin $k$
after $N$ steps with last step $\sR$, respectively $\sL$, and set them to $0$
outside $0\le k\le N$. Then $P_1^{\sR}(1)=P_1^{\sL}(0)=\tfrac12$,
$P_1^{\sR}(0)=P_1^{\sL}(1)=0$, and for $N\ge2$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp}
P_N^{\sR}(k)=p\,P_{N-1}^{\sR}(k-1)+q\,P_{N-1}^{\sL}(k-1),\qquad
P_N^{\sL}(k)=p\,P_{N-1}^{\sL}(k)+q\,P_{N-1}^{\sR}(k).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gf}
\sum_{N\ge0}\sum_{k=0}^{N}P_N(k)\,x^kt^N=\frac{1-(p-\tfrac12)(1+x)\,t}{1-p(1+x)\,t+(2p-1)\,x\,t^2},
\end{equation}
and for all $N\ge2$ and all $k$, with $P_M(k)=0$ outside $0\le k\le M$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
P_N(k)=p\bigl(P_{N-1}(k)+P_{N-1}(k-1)\bigr)-(2p-1)\,P_{N-2}(k-1).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The recursion \eqref{eq:dp} follows by conditioning on the last 2 steps. Let
$H^{\sR}=\sum_{N,k}P_N^{\sR}(k)x^kt^N$ and define $H^{\sL}$ alike. By
\eqref{eq:dp} and the initial values, $H^{\sR}=xt(\tfrac12+pH^{\sR}+qH^{\sL})$
and $H^{\sL}=t(\tfrac12+pH^{\sL}+qH^{\sR})$. The determinant of this linear
system is $\Delta=1-p(1+x)t+\rho xt^2$, since $p^2-q^2=\rho$. Cramer's rule
gives $H^{\sR}=\tfrac12xt(1-\rho t)/\Delta$ and
$H^{\sL}=\tfrac12t(1-\rho xt)/\Delta$, and $1+H^{\sR}+H^{\sL}$ simplifies to
\eqref{eq:gf}. Multiplying \eqref{eq:gf} by $\Delta$ and comparing the
coefficients of $x^kt^N$ with $N\ge2$ gives \eqref{eq:rec}.
\end{proof}

At $p=\tfrac12$ the last term of \eqref{eq:rec} vanishes, and \eqref{eq:rec}
is Pascal's rule with weights $\tfrac12$.

\begin{remark}[numerator tables]\label{rem:tables}
For $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ with integers $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, not both $0$,
Kagey's numerator tables are
\[
n_N(k)=2(\alpha+\beta)^{N-1}P_N(k)\quad(N\ge1),\qquad n_0(0)=1,
\]
not reduced. By \eqref{eq:wordprob}, $n_N(k)$ is the sum of
$\alpha^{\rep(w)}\beta^{\chg(w)}$ over the words with $k$ letters $\sR$ and
$N-k$ letters $\sL$, so it is an integer. By \eqref{eq:rec},
$n_N(k)=\alpha(n_{N-1}(k)+n_{N-1}(k-1))-(\alpha^2-\beta^2)n_{N-2}(k-1)$ for
$N\ge3$. Read a word as a monotone lattice path to $(k,N-k)$. Then $n_N(k)$
counts these paths with each straight continuation colored in 1 of $\alpha$
ways and each corner in 1 of $\beta$ ways. For $p=\tfrac23$ the table counts
rook paths (A035002 read by antidiagonals), and for $p=\tfrac13$ it counts
Mathar's blocked walks (A348595). The OEIS entries already record the
generating functions (by Kruchinin, Somos and
Mathar)~\cite{oeisA035002,oeisA348595}, and our bijections explain them.
Appendix~\ref{app:tables} has the proofs.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Variance and the normal limit}\label{sec:clt}

Recall the spins $\sigma_i$, the position $X_N=\sigma_1+\dots+\sigma_N$ and the
bin $K_N=(N+X_N)/2$. The step rule says that $\sigma_{i+1}=\sigma_is_i$, where
$s_1,s_2,\dots$ are independent of each other and of $\sigma_1$, with
$\Prob(s_i=1)=p$ and $\Prob(s_i=-1)=q$. So $\E s_i=\rho$.

\begin{proposition}[mean and variance]\label{prop:var}
$\E K_N=N/2$, and for $p<1$
\[
\Var K_N=\frac14\Bigl(N\,\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{2\rho\,(1-\rho^N)}{(1-\rho)^2}\Bigr)
=\frac{Np}{4(1-p)}-\frac{(2p-1)\bigl(1-(2p-1)^N\bigr)}{8(1-p)^2}.
\]
For $p=1$, $\Var K_N=N^2/4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Note that $\E\sigma_1=0$ and $\sigma_{i+m}=\sigma_is_i\cdots s_{i+m-1}$, so
$\E\sigma_i=0$ and $\E\sigma_i\sigma_{i+m}=\rho^m$. Hence
$\Var X_N=N+2\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}(N-m)\rho^m$. Summing the 2 geometric series
gives the first form, and the second follows from $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)=p/q$ and
$1-\rho=2q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[normal limit]\label{thm:clt}
Let $0<p<1$ be fixed. Then $(K_N-N/2)/\sqrt N$ converges in distribution, as
$N\to\infty$, to the normal law with mean $0$ and variance $p/(4(1-p))$.
\end{theorem}

So persistence keeps the bell shape but widens it by the factor $\sqrt{p/q}$
compared with the ordinary board. The idea of the proof is that the
characteristic function is a power of a $2\times2$ transfer matrix, whose
largest eigenvalue near $\theta=0$ is $1-\frac p{2q}\theta^2+O(\theta^4)$.

\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi_N(\theta)=\E e^{i\theta X_N}$, and let $v_N$ be the vector with
entries $\E[e^{i\theta X_N};\sigma_N=\pm1]$. By \eqref{eq:dp}, $v_{N+1}=Mv_N$
with
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}pe^{i\theta}&qe^{i\theta}\\qe^{-i\theta}&pe^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{tr}M=2p\cos\theta,\quad\det M=\rho .
\]
The eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm(\theta)=p\cos\theta\pm\sqrt{q^2-p^2\sin^2\theta}$.
For $|\sin\theta|<q/p$ they are real and distinct, so
$\varphi_N=c_+\lambda_+^{N-1}+c_-\lambda_-^{N-1}$ for $N\ge1$. The values
$\varphi_1=\cos\theta$ and $\varphi_2=p\cos2\theta+q$ give
$c_+=(\varphi_2-\lambda_-\cos\theta)/(\lambda_+-\lambda_-)$ and
$c_-=\cos\theta-c_+$. As $\theta\to0$ we have $\lambda_-\to\rho$, $c_+\to1$,
$c_-\to0$ and
\[
\lambda_+(\theta)=p\bigl(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}2\bigr)+q\Bigl(1-\frac{p^2\theta^2}{2q^2}\Bigr)+O(\theta^4)
=1-\frac{p}{2q}\,\theta^2+O(\theta^4).
\]
Put $\theta=s/\sqrt N$ with $s$ fixed. Since $|\rho|<1$, we get
$\varphi_N(s/\sqrt N)\to\exp(-\tfrac{p}{2q}s^2)$. By L\'evy's continuity
theorem \cite[Theorem~26.3]{billingsley1995}, $X_N/\sqrt N$ converges to the
normal law with variance $p/q$. Finally $K_N-N/2=X_N/2$.
\end{proof}

For $p=1$ the walk ends in bin $0$ or bin $N$, and for $p=0$ in a middle bin,
so these 2 cases have no normal limit.

\begin{remark}[zeros of the generating polynomial]\label{rem:bernoulli}
Let $N\ge2$ and $g_N(x)=\sum_kP_N(k)x^k$. The zeros of $g_N$ behave
differently on the 2 sides of $p=\frac12$. First let $0<p\le\tfrac12$. By \eqref{eq:rec},
$g_N=p(1+x)g_{N-1}+(1-2p)xg_{N-2}$ with a nonnegative last coefficient. Then
every $g_N$ has only real zeros, as in the proof
of~\cite[Proposition~4.2]{dekkingkong2011}, and they are negative since $g_N$
has positive coefficients. By a classical result recalled by
Pitman~\cite[Proposition~1]{pitman1997}, $K_N$ then has the law of a sum of
$N$ independent Bernoulli variables. So each row is log-concave and no bin is
less likely than the endpoints. This fits with Section~\ref{sec:allbin}, where
every endpoint crossing is at least $\tfrac23$.

Now let $\tfrac12\le p<1$, so that $u\le1$. Put $x_{i,i+1}=x_{i+1,i}=u$ for
$1\le i<N$ and $x_{ij}=1$ for the other pairs $i\ne j$. For
$S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ let $w$ be the word with $\sR$ exactly at the
positions in $S$. Each switch of $w$ is a pair of neighbors split by $S$, so
$\prod_{i\in S,\,j\notin S}x_{ij}=u^{\chg(w)}$, and \eqref{eq:wordprob} gives
\[
g_N(x)=\frac{p^{N-1}}2\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}}x^{|S|}\prod_{i\in S,\,j\notin S}x_{ij}.
\]
The $x_{ij}$ are real and symmetric with $|x_{ij}|\le1$, so by the Lee--Yang
circle theorem~\cite{leeyang1952}, in the form quoted by
Ghosh~\cite{ghosh2012}, every zero of $g_N$ lies on the circle $|x|=1$. It
applies because the boundaries are free and the first step is uniform, so
there is no boundary field, and because $u\le1$ (that is, $J\ge0$).

At $p=\tfrac12$ we have $g_N=\bigl((1+x)/2\bigr)^N$, and both statements hold.
For $\tfrac12<p<1$ the zeros are never all real. Suppose they were. Then $K_N$
would again be a sum of $N$ independent Bernoulli variables, with variance at
most $N/4$. But the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:var} gives
$\Var X_N=N+2\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}(N-m)\rho^m>N$, so $\Var K_N>N/4$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[cylinder]\label{rem:cyl}
Kagey also asks about a board wrapped into a cylinder. Suppose each row has $c$
pegs around the cylinder and the local rule is unchanged. Then the position is
$X_N$ modulo $2c$ in half-peg units. The Fourier coefficients of its law are
$\varphi_N(\pi l/c)$, $0\le l<2c$, and they equal $1$ and $(-1)^N$ at $l=0$
and $l=c$. Let $0<p<1$ and $\theta\notin\pi\Z$. Then both eigenvalues of $M$
have modulus less than $1$. Real ones satisfy
$|\lambda_\pm|\le p|\cos\theta|+q<1$, and complex ones have modulus
$\sqrt\rho<1$. So $\varphi_N(\theta)\to0$ geometrically. This also holds for a
double eigenvalue $\lambda=p\cos\theta$, since then $(M-\lambda I)^2=0$ by the
Cayley--Hamilton theorem and $M^n=\lambda^nI+n\lambda^{n-1}(M-\lambda I)$.
Hence the law tends geometrically fast to the uniform law on the $c$ positions
of the correct parity. In words, on a cylinder the ball forgets where it
started, and there is no normal shape in the limit.
\end{remark}
