\begin{abstract}
We observe a ball bouncing down a Galton board, where its first bounce is fair and each later
bounce repeats the previous one with probability $p$. We find the value of $p$
at which the middle bin is exactly as likely as the end bins, a question posed by Kagey.
For $N$ bounces this is the value at which the ball turns about $\log N$
times on average. The main tool compares the middle bin with a Bessel function along
with an explicit error at every $N$. It gives an explicit interval for the tie
for every $N\ge2$. Large $N$ is done analytically, and small $N$ by exact
integer arithmetic that we also checked in Lean. We also find the tie of every
other bin and the shift caused by periodic boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} persistent random walk; correlated random walk;
Galton board; Markov binomial distribution; Ising chain; modified Bessel
functions; computer-assisted proof.

\noindent\textbf{MSC2020:} 60J10; 60F05; 82B20; 60G50; 33C10.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Kagey's question}

Problem 131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131} is about a
Galton board where the ball has momentum. The first bounce is fair. After that,
each bounce goes the same way as the previous one with probability $p$. For
$p=\frac12$ this is the usual board. The bin is binomial and the end bins are
the least likely. For $p$ close to $1$ the ball rarely turns, so the end bins
are the most likely.

Kagey asks five questions. What is the law of the bins? What do the tables of
numerators for $p=\frac23$ and $p=\frac13$ count? At which $p$ is the middle bin
as likely as the end bins? Is there a normal limit? What changes on a cylinder?
Most of this paper is about the third question.

With two steps there are three bins. The ball
lands in the middle exactly when it turns once, so the bins have probabilities
$\frac p2$, $1-p$ and $\frac p2$, and they are level at $p=\frac23$. This is
why the third row of Kagey's table for $p=\frac23$ reads $2,2,2$. With three
steps the bins have probabilities $\frac{p^2}2$, $\frac{1-p^2}2$,
$\frac{1-p^2}2$ and $\frac{p^2}2$, and they are level at $p=1/\sqrt2$. In
general there is exactly one way to reach a given end bin, by never turning,
and very many ways to reach the middle by turning a few times, each turn
bringing a factor of about $1-p$.

The middle bin and the end bins are level when the ball turns about $\log N$
times on average, where $N$ is the number of steps. More precisely, the
crossing value $p_N$ of $p$ satisfies
$N(1-p_N)=\log N-\frac12\log\log N-0.467\ldots+o(1)$, and for every $N\ge2$
we trap $N(1-p_N)$ between two explicit functions of $N$ whose distance tends
to $0$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n}). For $p<p_N$ the
middle is more likely, and for $p>p_N$ the ends are.

The other questions have short answers. The law of the bins is a sum over the
number of turns (Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf}), with a rational generating function
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gf}), and this is classical~\cite{renshaw1981}. The
numerators of Kagey's tables are weighted counts of words
(Remark~\ref{rem:tables}). For each fixed $p\in(0,1)$ the bin has a normal
limit (Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}). We do not treat the cylinder. Instead we close
the sequence of steps into a loop, which gives the periodic Ising chain. This
moves the central crossing by about $(\log2)/N$ in the odds $(1-p)/p$
(Section~\ref{sec:periodicwindow}). Kagey
also asks about boards with more directions, such as a tetrahedron. The
companion paper~\cite{paperB} treats $d$ directions, and more general chains
and walks with memory are in preparation~\cite{paperC,paperD}.

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{The walk.} The ball makes $N\ge1$ steps. We record a run as a word
$w=w_1\cdots w_N$ in the letters $\sR$ (right) and $\sL$ (left). The letter $w_1$
is $\sR$ or $\sL$ with probability $\frac12$, and given $w_1,\dots,w_i$,
\[
\Prob(w_{i+1}=w_i)=p,\qquad \Prob(w_{i+1}\ne w_i)=q=1-p .
\]
An index $i<N$ with $w_i\ne w_{i+1}$ is a \emph{switch}. Let $\chg(w)$ be the
number of switches and $\rep(w)=N-1-\chg(w)$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wordprob}
\Prob(w)=\tfrac12\,p^{\rep(w)}q^{\chg(w)} .
\end{equation}
For $p>0$ we put $u=q/p$, the odds of a switch.

\paragraph{Bins.} The ball lands in bin $k$ if $w$ has $k$ letters $\sR$, for
$0\le k\le N$. We write $P_N(k)$ for the probability of bin $k$, and $P_0(0)=1$.
Bins $0$ and $N$ are the \emph{endpoints}. Only the constant word
$\sL\cdots\sL$ reaches bin $0$, and only $\sR\cdots\sR$ reaches bin $N$, so
\[
P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\tfrac12p^{N-1}\qquad(N\ge1).
\]
A middle bin is $m_N=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$. For odd $N$ the two middle bins
have equal probability by symmetry. Row $n$ of the tables of the problem has
$n$ bins, so it is our $N=n-1$. For $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ with integers
$\alpha,\beta\ge0$, not both $0$, the numerator of bin $k$ in those tables is
$n_N(k)=2(\alpha+\beta)^{N-1}P_N(k)$ (Remark~\ref{rem:tables}).

\paragraph{Spins.} Put $\sigma_i=+1$ if $w_i=\sR$ and $\sigma_i=-1$ if
$w_i=\sL$, and $X_N=\sigma_1+\dots+\sigma_N$. The bin is $K_N=(N+X_N)/2$. Since
$\chg(w)=\sum_{i<N}(1-\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1})/2$, \eqref{eq:wordprob} gives
$\Prob(w)\propto\exp(J\sum_{i<N}\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1})$ with $e^{-2J}=u$. So the
spins form the Ising chain in zero field with free boundaries, and $J>0$
exactly when $p>\frac12$.

\paragraph{Scales.} Let $L=\log N$ and $T=Nq$. Each of the $N-1$ steps after
the first switches with probability $q$, so the expected number of switches is
$(N-1)q=T-q$. We call $T$ the \emph{switching scale}. The central crossing has
$T$ of order $L$. Near the edges the crossings have larger $T$, up to order
$\sqrt N$ for the bins next to an edge (Section~\ref{sec:allbin}).

\paragraph{Ratios and crossings.} For a bin $k$ with $1\le k\le N-1$ we write
$a=k$ and $b=N-k$ for its numbers of letters $\sR$ and $\sL$. For $p>0$ the
ratio of bin $k$ to an endpoint depends on $p$ only through $u$, and it is a
polynomial in $u$ with nonnegative integer coefficients
(\eqref{eq:allbin-polynomial} below). We write
\[
S_{a,b}(u)=\frac{P_N(a)}{P_N(0)},\qquad S_N(u)=S_{m_N,\,N-m_N}(u)
\]
for this ratio and for its value at the middle. \emph{The crossing} of bin $k$
is the value of $p$ at which bin $k$ is exactly as likely as an endpoint, that
is, $S_{a,b}(u)=1$. It is unique (Section~\ref{sec:allbin}), and we write
$p_{a,b}$ for it and $u_{a,b}=(1-p_{a,b})/p_{a,b}$ for its odds. The
\emph{central crossing} is $p_N=p_{m_N,N-m_N}$, with odds $u_N$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zN}
z_N=N(1-p_N)=\frac{Nu_N}{1+u_N}
\end{equation}
is the value of $T$ there.

\paragraph{Special functions and asymptotic notation.} $I_0$ and $I_1$ are the
modified Bessel functions of the first kind. We use them through their power
series and their behavior at infinity~\cite[Eqs.~10.25.2, 10.40.1]{dlmf},
\[
I_0(2x)=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r}}{(r!)^2},\quad
I_1(2x)=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r+1}}{r!\,(r+1)!},\quad
I_\nu(z)=\frac{e^z}{\sqrt{2\pi z}}\Bigl(1-\frac{4\nu^2-1}{8z}+O(z^{-2})\Bigr)
\]
as $z\to\infty$, for $\nu=0,1$.
We write $f\sim g$ if $f/g\to1$. Each $O(\cdot)$ comes with the parameters it
is uniform in, and $O_a(\cdot)$ means that the constant may depend on $a$.

\subsection{Main results}

\paragraph{A Bessel bound at every $N$.} Put $h=N/2$ and
$F(x)=2x(I_0(2x)+I_1(2x))$. Theorem~\ref{thm:bessel} says that for every
$N\ge2$ and every $x>0$,
\[
0\le1-\frac{hS_N(x/h)}{F(x)}\le\frac{x(x+1)}h .
\]
Near the crossing $x=Nu/2$ is about $L/2$, so the relative error is
$O(L^2/N)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:allbin-bessel} gives the
same kind of bound for every bin, with relative error at most $Nu^2/2+u$.

\paragraph{The central crossing at every $N$.} Let $\zeta_N$ be the positive
root of $8\zeta e^{2\zeta}=\pi N^2$, that is, $\zeta_N=\frac12W_0(\pi N^2/4)$
with $W_0$ the principal branch of Lambert's function, and let
$\varepsilon_N=\zeta_N(\zeta_N+1)/N+1/(16\zeta_N^2)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n}
says that
\[
\zeta_N+\frac1{8\zeta_N}-\varepsilon_N<z_N<\zeta_N+\frac1{8\zeta_N}
\qquad\text{for every }N\ge2.
\]
Note that $\varepsilon_N\to0$. For $N\ge175$ the proof is analytic. For
$2\le N\le174$ we decide the sign of $S_N-1$ at two rationals, one on each side
of the root, in exact integer arithmetic, and the Lean kernel repeats this
check. The theorem has two simpler consequences. With
$c_0=\frac12\log(\pi/8)=-0.467\ldots$, the crossing satisfies
\[
z_N+\tfrac12\log z_N=L+c_0+\frac1{8z_N}+R_N,\qquad
|R_N|\le\frac1{4L^2}+\frac{L^2+L}N\quad(N\ge2)
\]
(Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}), and as $N\to\infty$
\[
z_N=L-\tfrac12\log L+c_0+\frac{\frac14\log L-\frac12c_0+\frac18}{L}
+O\Bigl(\frac{(\log L)^2}{L^2}\Bigr)
\]
(Corollary~\ref{cor:every-n}). The convergence is slow, and $z_N/L$ is still
only $0.84$ at $N=10^4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:crossing}).

\paragraph{Every bin.} Let bin $a$ be at distance $a$ from the nearer edge,
with $b=N-a\ge a$. Next to the edge the crossing is at
$p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}=(1+\sqrt{N-1})/(2+\sqrt{N-1})$, and at the central
crossing these two bins are the least likely of all
(Proposition~\ref{prop:adjacent}). As $a\to\infty$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:allbin-expansion} expands $2\sqrt{ab}\,u_{a,b}$ to the same
order as $z_N$, uniformly in $b\ge a$. For fixed $a$ the crossing comes
instead from a Laguerre polynomial. Then $u_{a,b}=c_a/\sqrt b-1/b+O_a(b^{-3/2})$,
where $c_a^2$ is the positive root of an explicit polynomial of degree $a$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-laguerre}). The two regimes match
(Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-matching}).

\paragraph{Periodic boundaries.} If the steps are closed into a loop, so that
the last step also interacts with the first, the central crossing moves by
$(\log2)/N$ in the odds to leading order (Theorem~\ref{thm:periodic-crossing}).
Under either boundary, at odds $u_N^X+t/N$, where $u_N^X$ is the central
crossing of that boundary, the ratio of bin $N/2+yN/\sqrt{\log N}$ to an
endpoint tends to $e^{t-2y^2}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:critical-window}).

Sections~\ref{sec:exact} to~\ref{sec:periodicwindow} prove these results in
this order, Section~\ref{sec:verify} lists what was computed and what is in
Lean, and the appendix holds the longer proofs of Section~\ref{sec:middle}.

\subsection{Related work}

The correlated random walk goes back to Goldstein~\cite{goldstein1951} and
Gillis~\cite{gillis1955}. Renshaw and Henderson~\cite{renshaw1981} study the
same symmetric model, motivated by a Galton machine. They give the exact law
from run counts, the generating function, the normal limit and the upturn at
the endpoints, and in their Section~4 a Bessel limit at a fixed persistence
scale, followed by a limit as that scale grows. Since the two limits are taken
one after the other, there is no error bound at finite $N$ when the scale
grows like $\log N$, which is where the crossing happens. Konno~\cite{konno2009}
writes the exact law with the hypergeometric function ${}_2F_1$ and proves the
telegraph limit when $p=1-\theta/N$ with $\theta$ fixed, again a fixed scale.
Herrmann and Vallois~\cite{herrmann2010} give the telegraph limit with its
Bessel density. Dekking and Kong~\cite{dekkingkong2011} study the Markov
binomial law, give its variance and find when it has one, two or three modes.
Their criterion already gives the crossing of the bins next to the edges
(Section~\ref{sec:allbin-order}). The OEIS
entries~\cite{oeisA035002,oeisA348595} already record both tables of the
problem.

For the Ising chain, Antal, Droz and R\'acz~\cite{antal2004} give the
magnetization law at a fixed scale $T$ under several boundary conditions, and
Dantchev and Rudnick~\cite{dantchev2022} give exact free and periodic
coefficients. Stepanyan et~al.\@~\cite[Section~III.B]{stepanyan2024} study the
periodic endpoint--adjacent and endpoint--center crossings directly, so the
crossing question itself is not new. The new results here are bounds at finite
$N$ when $T$ is of order $\log N$, also near the edges. The Markov binomial
estimates of
\v{C}ekanavi\v{c}ius and Mikalauskas~\cite{cekanavicius2001,cekanavicius2001ld}
assume ranges of the transition probabilities that exclude $p\to1$.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Apart from Remark~\ref{rem:sign}, which is computer-assisted throughout, the
proofs use two computations, each described where it is used. They are the
integer certificates for $N\le174$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n}
(Step~4) and Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}, and the evaluation at
$N=2774$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:every-n}(d). The scripts are standard-library
Python. They check the exact law, the orderings, the Bessel bounds and the
asymptotic formulas, and where possible each reported number is computed in
two independent ways.

Many of the results are formalized in Lean~4, in the folder
\nolinkurl{kagey-problems/problem131/lean/PaperA}. This includes
Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n} for all $N\ge2$. For $N\le174$ the Lean kernel checks
the same certificates as Step~4 of its proof, with rational bounds on $\zeta_N$
that Lean proves itself. Only the standard axioms are used. Not formalized are
Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}, the remarks, and the terms after the
leading one in the asymptotic results. The file \nolinkurl{MAP.md} in that
folder gives the Lean theorem for each result, or says that there is none.

\paragraph{Data and code availability.} Everything is in the folder
\nolinkurl{kagey-problems/problem131} of
