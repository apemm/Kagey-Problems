 in the persistent random walk}
\author{Arjun Pemmasani}

\begin{abstract}
A ball falls through a Galton board and makes $N$ steps, each to the left or to
the right. The first step is fair, and each later step repeats the one before
it with probability $p$. Kagey asked for which $p$ the middle bin is exactly as
likely as the 2 end bins. We answer this for every $N$. The steps form the
zero-field Ising chain with free boundaries, and the answer is governed by
$T=N(1-p)$, essentially the expected number of direction changes. We compare
the ratio of each bin to an end bin with an explicit Bessel function, with a
relative error bound that holds at every $N$. At the center this traps the
crossing value $z_N$ of $T$ in an explicit interval whose width tends to $0$,
for every $N\ge2$. The proof is analytic for $N\ge175$, uses exact integer
certificates below that, and is also checked in Lean. As a consequence
$z_N+\frac12\log z_N=\log N+\frac12\log(\pi/8)+1/(8z_N)+R_N$ with an explicit
bound on $R_N$. We also find the crossing of every other bin, compare free and
periodic boundaries, and prove a local limit theorem for the bin probabilities
whose relative error tends to $0$ uniformly for $1-p\le\frac12$, from the
diffusive to the ballistic regime.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} persistent random walk; correlated random walk;
Galton board; Markov binomial distribution; Ising chain; modified Bessel
functions; local limit theorem; computer-assisted proof.

\noindent\textbf{MSC2020:} 60J10; 60F05; 82B20; 60G50; 33C10; 05A15.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Kagey's question}

Suppose you drop a ball into a Galton board. At each peg it bounces left or
right. On an ordinary board the bounces are fair and independent, the bin is
binomial, and the 2 end bins are the least likely. Problem 131 of Kagey's Open
Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131} gives the ball some momentum. The first
bounce is fair, and after that each bounce goes the same way as the one before
it with probability $p$. At $p=\frac12$ this is the ordinary board. As $p$
grows the ball tends to keep going, and the end bins gain weight.

Kagey asks for the law of the bins, for the meaning of the tables of
numerators that this law produces for $p=\frac23$ and $p=\frac13$, for the
value of $p$ at which the middle bin and the end bins are equally likely,
whether there is a normal limit, and what changes on a cylinder. The third
question is the main one, and most of this paper is about it.

Here are the smallest cases. With 2 steps there are 3 bins. The ball lands in
the middle exactly when it turns once, so the bins have probabilities
$\frac p2$, $1-p$ and $\frac p2$, and they are level at $p=\frac23$. This is
why the third row of Kagey's table for $p=\frac23$ reads $2,2,2$. With 3
steps the bins have probabilities $\frac{p^2}2$, $\frac{1-p^2}2$,
$\frac{1-p^2}2$ and $\frac{p^2}2$, and they are level at $p=1/\sqrt2$. In
general there is exactly 1 way to reach a given end bin, by never turning, and
very many ways to reach the middle by turning a few times, each turn costing a
factor of about $1-p$.

The answer is that the middle and the ends are level when the ball turns about
$\log N$ times on average, where $N$ is the number of steps. More precisely,
the crossing value $p_N$ of $p$ satisfies
$N(1-p_N)=\log N-\frac12\log\log N-0.467\ldots+o(1)$, and we trap $N(1-p_N)$
between 2 explicit functions of $N$ whose distance tends to $0$, for every
$N\ge2$ and not only in the limit (Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n}). For $p<p_N$ the
middle is more likely, and for $p>p_N$ the ends are.

The other questions have short answers. The law of the bins is a sum over the
number of turns (Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf}), with a rational generating function
(Proposition~\ref{prop:gf}); this is classical~\cite{renshaw1981}. The
numerators count lattice paths: rook paths for $p=\frac23$ (OEIS A035002) and
Mathar's blocked walks for $p=\frac13$ (OEIS A348595), by the bijections of
Appendix~\ref{app:tables}. For each fixed $p\in(0,1)$ the bin has a normal
limit (Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}), and Section~\ref{sec:llt} gives a local form
that stays accurate as $p\to1$ with $N$. On a cylinder the bin forgets the
start geometrically fast and tends to the uniform law (Remark~\ref{rem:cyl}).
A different way to make the board periodic closes the sequence of steps into a
loop. That is the periodic Ising chain, and it moves the central crossing by
about $(\log2)/N$ in the odds $(1-p)/p$ (Section~\ref{sec:periodicwindow}).
Kagey also asks about boards with more directions, such as a tetrahedron;
Papers~B and~C of this series treat those (Section~\ref{sec:series}).

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:setting}

The other papers of the series use the same notation.

\paragraph{The walk.} The ball makes $N\ge1$ steps. A run of the experiment is
a word $w=w_1\cdots w_N$ in the letters $\sL$ (left) and $\sR$ (right). The
first letter is $\sL$ or $\sR$ with probability $\frac12$ each. Each later
letter equals the one before it with probability $p$ and differs from it with
probability $q=1-p$, independently of the past. When $p>0$ we write $u=q/p$
for the odds of a switch. A \emph{switch} of $w$ is an index $i<N$ with
$w_i\ne w_{i+1}$. We write $\chg(w)$ for the number of switches and
$\rep(w)=N-1-\chg(w)$ for the number of repeats, so that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wordprob}
\Prob(w)=\tfrac12\,p^{\rep(w)}q^{\chg(w)} .
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Bins.} The ball lands in bin $k$ if $w$ has $k$ letters $\sR$, for
$0\le k\le N$. We write $P_N(k)$ for the probability of bin $k$, and $P_0(0)=1$.
Bins $0$ and $N$ are the \emph{endpoints}. Only the constant word
$\sL\cdots\sL$ reaches bin $0$, and only $\sR\cdots\sR$ reaches bin $N$, so
\[
P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\tfrac12p^{N-1}\qquad(N\ge1).
\]
A middle bin is $m_N=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$. For odd $N$ the 2 middle bins have
equal probability by symmetry. Kagey's row $n$ has $n$ bins, so it is our
$N=n-1$. For $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ with integers $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, not
both $0$, his numerator of bin $k$ is $n_N(k)=2(\alpha+\beta)^{N-1}P_N(k)$
(Remark~\ref{rem:tables}).

\paragraph{Spins.} Put $\sigma_i=+1$ if $w_i=\sR$ and $\sigma_i=-1$ if
$w_i=\sL$, and let $X_N=\sigma_1+\dots+\sigma_N$ be the final position, so that
the bin is $K_N=(N+X_N)/2$. By \eqref{eq:wordprob} the spins have law
proportional to $\exp(J\sum_{i<N}\sigma_i\sigma_{i+1})$ with $e^{-2J}=u$: this
is the one-dimensional Ising chain in zero field with free boundaries. The
coupling $J$ is positive exactly when $p>\frac12$.

\paragraph{Scales.} We write $L=\log N$ and $T=Nq$. The walk makes $N-1$
choices to repeat or switch, so the expected number of switches is
$(N-1)q=T-q$. We call $T$ the \emph{switching scale}. All the crossings in
this paper happen at $T$ of order $L$ or smaller.

\paragraph{Ratios and crossings.} For a bin $k$ with $1\le k\le N-1$ we write
$a=k$ and $b=N-k$ for its numbers of letters $\sR$ and $\sL$. For $p>0$ the
ratio of bin $k$ to an endpoint depends on $p$ only through $u$, and it is a
polynomial in $u$ with nonnegative integer coefficients
(\eqref{eq:allbin-polynomial} below). We write
\[
S_{a,b}(u)=\frac{P_N(a)}{P_N(0)},\qquad S_N(u)=S_{m_N,\,N-m_N}(u)
\]
for this ratio and for its value at the middle. \emph{The crossing} of bin $k$
is the value of $p$ at which bin $k$ is exactly as likely as an endpoint, that
is, $S_{a,b}(u)=1$. It is unique (Section~\ref{sec:allbin}), and we write
$p_{a,b}$ for it and $u_{a,b}=(1-p_{a,b})/p_{a,b}$ for its odds. The
\emph{central crossing} is $p_N=p_{m_N,N-m_N}$, with odds $u_N$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zN}
z_N=N(1-p_N)=\frac{Nu_N}{1+u_N}
\end{equation}
is the value of $T$ there.

\paragraph{Special functions and asymptotic notation.} $I_0$ and $I_1$ are the
modified Bessel functions of the first kind. We use them through their power
series and their behavior at infinity~\cite[Eqs.~10.25.2, 10.40.1]{dlmf},
\[
I_0(2x)=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r}}{(r!)^2},\qquad
I_1(2x)=\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r+1}}{r!\,(r+1)!},\qquad
I_\nu(z)=\frac{e^z}{\sqrt{2\pi z}}\Bigl(1-\frac{4\nu^2-1}{8z}+O(z^{-2})\Bigr)
\quad(\nu=0,1).
\]
We write $f\sim g$ if $f/g\to1$. Each $O(\cdot)$ comes with the parameters it
is uniform in, and $O_a(\cdot)$ means that the constant may depend on $a$.

\subsection{Main results}

\paragraph{A Bessel bound at every $N$.} Put $h=N/2$ and
$F(x)=2x(I_0(2x)+I_1(2x))$. Theorem~\ref{thm:bessel} says that for every
$N\ge2$ and every $x>0$,
\[
0\le1-\frac{hS_N(x/h)}{F(x)}\le\frac{x(x+1)}h .
\]
In words, the middle ratio at odds $u=x/h$ is a Bessel function up to a
relative error of at most $x(x+1)/h$. Near the crossing $x=Nu/2$ is about
$L/2$, so the error is $O(L^2/N)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:allbin-bessel} gives the
same kind of bound for every bin, with relative error at most $Nu^2/2+u$.

\paragraph{The central crossing at every $N$.} Let $\zeta_N$ be the positive
root of $8\zeta e^{2\zeta}=\pi N^2$, that is, $\zeta_N=\frac12W_0(\pi N^2/4)$
with $W_0$ the principal branch of Lambert's function, and let
$\varepsilon_N=\zeta_N(\zeta_N+1)/N+1/(16\zeta_N^2)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n}
says that
\[
\zeta_N+\frac1{8\zeta_N}-\varepsilon_N<z_N<\zeta_N+\frac1{8\zeta_N}
\qquad\text{for every }N\ge2.
\]
The width $\varepsilon_N$ is $0.44$ at $N=10$ and $0.039$ at $N=1000$, and it
tends to $0$. The proof is analytic for $N\ge175$. For $2\le N\le174$ it rests
on exact integer certificates for the sign of $S_N-1$ at 2 points, one on each
side of the root, which the Lean kernel also checks. Two consequences are
easier to read. With
$c_0=\frac12\log(\pi/8)=-0.467\ldots$, the crossing satisfies
\[
z_N+\tfrac12\log z_N=L+c_0+\frac1{8z_N}+R_N,\qquad
|R_N|\le\frac1{4L^2}+\frac{L^2+L}N\quad(N\ge2)
\]
(Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}), and as $N\to\infty$
\[
z_N=L-\tfrac12\log L+c_0+\frac{\frac14\log L-\frac12c_0+\frac18}{L}
+O\Bigl(\frac{(\log L)^2}{L^2}\Bigr)
\]
(Corollary~\ref{cor:every-n}). The convergence is slow: $z_N/L$ is still only
$0.84$ at $N=10^4$ (Figure~\ref{fig:crossing}).

\paragraph{Every bin.} Let bin $a$ be at distance $a$ from the nearer edge,
with $b=N-a\ge a$. Next to the edge the crossing is at
$p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}=(1+\sqrt{N-1})/(2+\sqrt{N-1})$, and at the central
crossing these 2 bins are the least likely of all
(Proposition~\ref{prop:adjacent}). As $a\to\infty$,
Theorem~\ref{thm:allbin-expansion} expands $2\sqrt{ab}\,u_{a,b}$ to the same
order as $z_N$, uniformly in $b\ge a$. For fixed $a$ the crossing comes
instead from a Laguerre polynomial: $u_{a,b}=c_a/\sqrt b-1/b+O_a(b^{-3/2})$,
where $c_a^2$ is the positive root of an explicit polynomial of degree $a$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-laguerre}). The 2 regimes match
(Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-matching}).

\paragraph{Periodic boundaries.} If the steps are closed into a loop, so that
the last step also interacts with the first, the central crossing moves by
$(\log2)/N$ in the odds to leading order (Theorem~\ref{thm:periodic-crossing}).
Under both boundaries, at odds $u_N+t/N$ the ratio of bin
$N/2+yN/\sqrt{\log N}$ to an endpoint tends to $e^{t-2y^2}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:critical-window}).

\paragraph{A uniform local law.} We approximate $P_N(k)$ itself by a tilted
symmetric Skellam kernel, with an explicit relative error bound for every
$0<q<1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:llt}) that tends to $0$ uniformly in $q\le\frac12$
away from the edges (Corollary~\ref{cor:llt}). This covers the diffusive
regime ($q$ fixed) and the ballistic regime ($T$ bounded) at once.

Sections~\ref{sec:exact} to~\ref{sec:llt} prove these results in this order,
Section~\ref{sec:verify} lists what was computed and what is in Lean, and the
appendices hold the longer proofs and Kagey's tables.

This paper is Paper~A, part~I of the series \emph{The middle and the ends}.
Papers~B, C and~D are parts~II, III and~IV~\cite{paperB,paperC,paperD}. The 4
papers share the following notation.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=2pt]
\item Kagey's board: $N$ steps (Kagey's row $n$ has $N=n-1$). The first step
is left or right with probability $\frac12$, and each later step repeats the
previous one with probability $p$. Also $q=1-p$ and $u=q/p$, the odds of a
switch.
\item $P_N(k)$ is the probability of $k$ right steps (the ball lands in bin
$k$). The endpoints have $P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\frac12p^{N-1}$.
\item $L=\log N$ and $T=Nq$, the expected number of switches up to the first
step. The central crossing is $p_N$, and $z_N=N(1-p_N)$.
\item \emph{The crossing} is the value of $p$ (or $T$) where a bin is exactly
as likely as an endpoint.
\item With $d$ states (Papers~B and~C), a \emph{face} $F$ is a nonempty set of
states and the \emph{vertices} are the faces of size $1$. $\lambda_F$ is the
principal Dirichlet eigenvalue of $-Q$ on $F$ and $K_F$ is the second-order
constant of $F$.
\end{itemize}
This paper treats Kagey's board itself, with 2 directions, and the others
use its exact law, its Bessel bounds and its crossing $z_N$. Paper~B lets the
ball move in $d$ directions and switch to each other direction at the same
rate (the $d$-state Potts chain); there every face ties with the vertices at
first order, and Paper~B resolves the tie at second order. Paper~C treats a
general chain with transition matrix $I+(T/N)Q$ and $T=\tau L$, where to first
order the mode sits on a face that minimizes $\tau\lambda_F+|F|-1$; the
constant $c_0$ of this paper is one of its corollaries. Paper~D asks what
memory changes, and finds for the elephant random walk a crossing about twice
$z_N$. Figure~\ref{fig:program} shows the crossings of all 4 papers.

\subsection{Related work}

The correlated random walk goes back to Goldstein~\cite{goldstein1951} and
Gillis~\cite{gillis1955}. Renshaw and Henderson~\cite{renshaw1981} study the
same symmetric model, motivated by a Galton machine. They give the exact law
from run counts, the generating function, the normal limit and the upturn at
the endpoints, and in their Section~4 a Bessel limit at a fixed persistence
scale, followed by a limit as that scale grows. Since the 2 limits are taken
one after the other, there is no error bound at finite $N$ when the scale
grows like $\log N$, which is where the crossing happens. Konno~\cite{konno2009}
writes the exact law with the hypergeometric function ${}_2F_1$ and proves the
telegraph limit when $p=1-\theta/N$ with $\theta$ fixed, again a fixed scale.
Herrmann and Vallois~\cite{herrmann2010} give the telegraph limit with its
Bessel density. Dekking and Kong~\cite{dekkingkong2011} study the Markov
binomial law, give its variance and find when it has 1, 2 or 3 modes. The OEIS
entries~\cite{oeisA035002,oeisA348595} already record both of Kagey's tables.

For the Ising chain, Antal, Droz and R\'acz~\cite{antal2004} give the
magnetization law at a fixed scale $T$ under several boundary conditions, and
Dantchev and Rudnick~\cite{dantchev2022} give exact free and periodic
coefficients. Stepanyan et~al.\@~\cite[Section~III.B]{stepanyan2024} study the
periodic endpoint--adjacent and endpoint--center crossings directly, so the
crossing question itself is not new. What we add are bounds at finite $N$ when
$T$ is of order $\log N$, also near the edges. The Markov binomial estimates of
\v{C}ekanavi\v{c}ius and Mikalauskas~\cite{cekanavicius2001,cekanavicius2001ld}
assume ranges of the transition probabilities that exclude $p\to1$.
Section~\ref{sec:llt} discusses the work on local limits.

\section{Verification}\label{sec:verify}

Apart from Remark~\ref{rem:sign}, which is computer-assisted throughout, the
proofs rest on only 2 computations: the integer certificates for $N\le174$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n} and Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}, made by 2
exact methods and compared with $\zeta_N$ in 60-digit decimal arithmetic with
every margin above $0.03$ (\texttt{verify\_every\_n.py}, rechecked by
\texttt{verify\_every\_n\_guards.py}), and the evaluation at $N=2774$ in
Corollary~\ref{cor:every-n}(d), in the same arithmetic
(\texttt{verify\_every\_n\_guards.py}). The checks for all 4 papers are
described in \texttt{SUPPLEMENT.md}, in the Problem 131 folder of
\url{https://github.com/apemm/Kagey-Problems}, together with the manuscript
source and the recorded results. The scripts are standard-library Python, and
\texttt{python -B verify\_all.py --paper a} runs the ones for this paper
(\texttt{--full} adds 2 long runs of about 21 minutes). They check the exact
law, the lattice-path counts, the orderings, the Bessel bounds and the
asymptotic formulas, and where possible each reported number is computed in 2
independent ways. Before the main computations (the monotonicity and
Bessel-ratio checks, the every-$N$ certificates and the local law) we wrote
predictions and conditions that would refute them in \texttt{ledger.md},
\texttt{paperA/data/ledger\_every\_n.md} and
\texttt{paperA/data/ledger\_llt.md}, and we recorded the outcomes there. Some
small-$N$ predictions for Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n} were not blind, as the
ledger says.

The Lean~4 proofs are in \texttt{lean/PaperA}, and \texttt{lean/README.md}
lists them. They include the exact law, the numerator tables, the variance and
the normal limit, the orderings of the crossings, the uniform Bessel bounds and
root comparisons (\texttt{UniformBessel.lean}, \texttt{RootBounds.lean}), the
leading terms $1-p_N\sim(\log N)/N$ and $2\sqrt{ab}\,u_{a,b}\sim\log a$, the
finite parts of the edge and periodic results, Lemmas~\ref{lem:bracket}
and~\ref{lem:bessel-bounds} and Theorem~\ref{thm:every-n} for every $N\ge2$
(\texttt{BesselIntegral.lean}, \texttt{EveryN.lean},
\texttt{EveryNLarge.lean}). The finite parts are the polynomial $H_a$ and its
root $y_a$, the exact series \eqref{eq:edge-exact-series}, the cyclic count
\eqref{eq:periodicratio} and the adjacent periodic crossing. For
Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-laguerre} only the algebra of the coefficients is in
Lean. For $N\le174$ the Lean kernel checks the same certificates in exact
integer arithmetic, with its own rational bounds on $\zeta_N$, so the whole
theorem is proved in Lean. \texttt{lean/build-log.txt} shows that every
theorem uses only the standard axioms. Remarks~\ref{rem:bernoulli},
\ref{rem:cyl}, \ref{rem:jacobi} and~\ref{rem:sign},
Corollary~\ref{cor:every-n} beyond part~(a),
Proposition~\ref{prop:crossing-eq}, Lemma~\ref{lem:inversion},
Theorem~\ref{thm:allbin-expansion} beyond its leading term, the expansions of
Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-laguerre}, the expansion
\eqref{eq:edge-matching-expansion} of Theorem~\ref{thm:edge-matching},
Theorem~\ref{thm:periodic-crossing}, even its leading term,
Corollary~\ref{cor:boundary-layer}, Theorem~\ref{thm:critical-window} and
Section~\ref{sec:llt} are not in Lean.
