\section{The second-order theory}\label{sec:secondorder}

The first-order rule locates a crossing only up to a factor $1+o(1)$ in $T$. To locate
it up to $o(1)$ we need the constant in front of $N^{-(k-1)}e^{-T\lambda_F}$ for
every face that satisfies (H), together with the power of $T$. The plan is as
follows. Section~\ref{sec:tilt} defines the constant $K_F$.
Section~\ref{sec:locallaw} proves a local limit theorem inside a face and
deduces $M_F$ up to a factor $1+O(1/T+T^2/N)$. Section~\ref{sec:crossings}
compares two faces, Section~\ref{sec:papersAB} recovers the crossings of the
two-state walk and of the complete graph, and
Section~\ref{sec:reducible} shows what can change without (H).
Section~\ref{sec:nextterm} adds the next term, of relative order $1/T$,
Section~\ref{sec:forest} gives a tree formula for $K_F$ on every irreducible face, and
Section~\ref{sec:flows} writes the rate $I_F$ through flows. In
this section gradients and Hessians in $\alpha$ are taken in the coordinates
$\alpha'=(\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_k)$, $|\cdot|$ is the Euclidean norm, and
$q=(q_i)_{i\in F}$.

\subsection{The tilted chain and the constant}\label{sec:tilt}

To find the probability of a composition with fractions $\alpha$ we change the
rates so that $\alpha$ becomes typical. The optimal vector $r_\alpha$ in the
definition of $I_F(\alpha)$ gives a new generator $\widehat Q_\alpha$ on $F$, with
the same switching graph, whose stationary law is exactly $\alpha$. Under it the
visit fractions satisfy a central limit theorem with covariance
$\Gamma_\alpha/T$, and the price of the change of rates is $e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}$
times boundary factors for the start and the end of the path. The constant
$C_F(\alpha)$ collects these factors and the Gaussian normalization.

\begin{definition}[tilt and constants]\label{def:tilt}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k\ge2$. For
$\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$ let $r_\alpha$ attain the supremum defining
$I_F(\alpha)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(b)), and put
$\theta(\alpha)=A_Fr_\alpha/r_\alpha$ and $l_\alpha=\alpha/r_\alpha$
(entrywise). The \emph{tilted generator} is
$\widehat Q_\alpha=R^{-1}(A_F-\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha))R$ with
$R=\operatorname{diag}r_\alpha$. With $D=\operatorname{diag}\alpha$ let
\[
Z_\alpha=(\ones\alpha^\top-\widehat Q_\alpha)^{-1}-\ones\alpha^\top,
\qquad\Gamma_\alpha=DZ_\alpha+Z_\alpha^\top D,
\]
and let $\Sigma_\alpha$ be $\Gamma_\alpha$ with the row and column of state
$1$ deleted. Finally let
\[
C_F(\alpha)=\frac{(\mu\cdot r_\alpha)(l_\alpha\cdot\ones)}
{(2\pi)^{(k-1)/2}\sqrt{\det\Sigma_\alpha}},\qquad K_F=C_F(\alpha^*_F).
\]
\end{definition}

For a single state we put $K_{\{i\}}=\mu_i$.
Nothing in Definition~\ref{def:tilt} changes when $r_\alpha$ is scaled. Note that
$l_\alpha\cdot r_\alpha=1$ and $I_F(\alpha)=\langle q-\theta(\alpha),\alpha\rangle$. Since
$r_\alpha$ minimizes $u\mapsto\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_iq_{ij}u_j/u_i$, its derivative in $u_j$, which
is $\sum_il_{\alpha,i}q_{ij}-l_{\alpha,j}\theta_j(\alpha)$, vanishes at $r_\alpha$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
l_\alpha^\top\bigl(A_F-\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha)\bigr)=0 .
\end{equation}
Also $(A_F-\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha))r_\alpha=0$ with $r_\alpha>0$, so $\theta(\alpha)\in
V_F$~\cite{bermanplemmons1994}. So $\widehat Q_\alpha$ is an irreducible generator with the
same off-diagonal zero pattern as $A_F$, and by~\eqref{eq:stationary} its stationary law is
$\alpha$. If $(\ones\alpha^\top-\widehat Q_\alpha)v=0$, multiplying by $\alpha^\top$ gives
$\alpha^\top v=0$, so $\widehat Q_\alpha v=0$. Hence $v$ is a multiple of $\ones$, and
$\alpha^\top v=0$ gives $v=0$. Thus $Z_\alpha$ exists, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Zprops}
Z_\alpha\ones=0,\qquad\alpha^\top Z_\alpha=0,\qquad
-\widehat Q_\alpha Z_\alpha=I-\ones\alpha^\top .
\end{equation}
So $\Gamma_\alpha\ones=0$. As $\Gamma_\alpha$ is symmetric, its adjugate is then a multiple of
$\ones\ones^\top$, so deleting another state instead of state $1$ gives the same
$\det\Sigma_\alpha$. At $\alpha^*_F$ the vector $u=r_F$ gives the value $\lambda_F=I_F(\alpha^*_F)$,
so $r_{\alpha^*_F}$ is a multiple of $r_F$, $\theta(\alpha^*_F)=q-\lambda_F\ones$, and
$l_{\alpha^*_F}$ is the matching multiple of $l_F$. In the local law below, $\mu\cdot r_\alpha$
accounts for the start of the path, $l_\alpha\cdot\ones$ for its free end, and $\Sigma_\alpha$
for the Gaussian spread of the occupation fractions.

For conductances $c_{ij}=c_{ji}$ on $F$ let
$\tree(c)=\sum_{\mathcal T}\prod_{\{i,j\}\in\mathcal T}c_{ij}$, the sum over
the spanning trees $\mathcal T$ of the complete graph on $F$.

\begin{proposition}[the constant]\label{prop:constant}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The maps $\alpha\mapsto\theta(\alpha)$, $I_F(\alpha)$, $\Sigma_\alpha$,
$r_\alpha$ and $l_\alpha$ (with $\sum_ir_{\alpha,i}=1$) are real-analytic on
$\Delta_F^\circ$, $\Sigma_\alpha$ is positive definite, and
$\nabla^2I_F(\alpha)=\Sigma_\alpha^{-1}$. Hence
$\det\Sigma_\alpha\cdot\det\nabla^2I_F(\alpha)=1$ and
$C_F(\alpha)=(\mu\cdot r_\alpha)(l_\alpha\cdot\ones)
\sqrt{\det\nabla^2I_F(\alpha)/(2\pi)^{k-1}}$.
\item Suppose that $\nu_iq_{ij}=\nu_jq_{ji}$ for $i,j\in F$ and some
$\nu\in(0,\infty)^F$. Then $r_\alpha$ is a multiple of $\sqrt{\alpha/\nu}$
and $l_\alpha$ of $\sqrt{\alpha\nu}$, and $\widehat Q_\alpha$ is reversible
with conductances $c_{ij}(\alpha)=\sqrt{\alpha_i\alpha_j}\,q_{ij}\sqrt{\nu_i/\nu_j}$.
Also $I_F(\alpha)=\sum_iq_i\alpha_i-\sum_{i\ne j}c_{ij}(\alpha)$, and
\[
\det\Sigma_\alpha=\frac{2^{k-1}(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2}{\tree(c(\alpha))},\qquad
C_F(\alpha)=\frac{(\mu\cdot r_\alpha)(l_\alpha\cdot\ones)}{(4\pi)^{(k-1)/2}}
\,\frac{\sqrt{\tree(c(\alpha))}}{\prod_i\alpha_i}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In words, the Gaussian factor in $C_F$ is, up to a power of $2\pi$, the square root
of the determinant of the Hessian of the rate. For a reversible face this determinant
is a weighted count of spanning trees divided by $2^{k-1}(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2$.

\begin{proof}
The proof of (a) is in Appendix~\ref{app:torus} and uses Lemma~\ref{lem:perron}.

(b) We guess the vector $r_\alpha$ and check that the guess is a critical point. Put
$u=\sqrt{\alpha/\nu}$ and $l=\alpha/u=\sqrt{\alpha\nu}$ (entrywise). As we saw
before~\eqref{eq:stationary}, the derivative of $\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_iq_{ij}u_j/u_i$ in $u_j$ is
$\sum_il_iq_{ij}-l_j(A_Fu)_j/u_j$. Here
\[
\sum_il_iq_{ij}=\sum_i\sqrt{\alpha_i\nu_i}\,q_{ij}\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{l_j(A_Fu)_j}{u_j}=\nu_j\sum_iq_{ji}\sqrt{\alpha_i/\nu_i}
=\sum_i\sqrt{\alpha_i\nu_i}\,q_{ij},
\]
where the last step uses $\nu_jq_{ji}=\nu_iq_{ij}$. So the derivative vanishes at $u$. The
function is convex in $\log u$, so its critical point $u$ is a minimizer, and the minimizer is
unique up to a factor (Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(b)). Hence $r_\alpha$ is a multiple of $u$ and
$l_\alpha$ is the matching multiple of $l$. Then
$\alpha_i(\widehat Q_\alpha)_{ij}=l_iq_{ij}u_j=c_{ij}(\alpha)$ for $i\ne j$, and
$c_{ij}(\alpha)=c_{ji}(\alpha)$ because $q_{ij}\sqrt{\nu_i/\nu_j}=q_{ji}\sqrt{\nu_j/\nu_i}$.
So $\widehat Q_\alpha$ is reversible with these conductances. Also
$\alpha_i\theta_i(\alpha)=l_i(A_Fu)_i=\sum_{j\ne i}c_{ij}(\alpha)$, and
$I_F(\alpha)=\langle q-\theta(\alpha),\alpha\rangle$ gives the formula for $I_F$.

For the determinant we express the quadratic form of $\Sigma_\alpha$ through the Laplacian of the
conductances, namely $\mathcal L=-D\widehat Q_\alpha$. It is symmetric, its off-diagonal entries
are $-c_{ij}(\alpha)$, and $\mathcal L\ones=0$. Fix $\eta\in\R^F$ with $\eta_1=0$, and put
$w=Z_\alpha\eta$ and $\zeta=D\eta-(\alpha\cdot\eta)\alpha$. By~\eqref{eq:Zprops},
$\mathcal Lw=D(I-\ones\alpha^\top)\eta=\zeta$ and $\alpha\cdot w=0$. So
$\eta^\top\Gamma_\alpha\eta=2(D\eta)^\top w=2\zeta^\top w$, where the second step uses
$\alpha\cdot w=0$. Next we remove state $1$. Let $v=w-w_1\ones$,
so that $v_1=0$. Since $\zeta\cdot\ones=0$ and $\mathcal L\ones=0$, we have
$\zeta^\top w=\zeta^\top v$ and $\mathcal Lv=\zeta$. We write primes for the coordinates
$2,\dots,k$, $D'=\operatorname{diag}\alpha'$, and $\mathcal L_{(1)}$ for $\mathcal L$ without row
and column $1$. As $v_1=0$, the rows $2,\dots,k$ of $\mathcal Lv=\zeta$ say that
$\mathcal L_{(1)}v'=\zeta'$, and as $\eta_1=0$ we have $\zeta'=(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)\eta'$. The
matrix-tree theorem~\cite{bollobas1998} gives $\det\mathcal L_{(1)}=\tree(c(\alpha))$, which is
positive because $F$ is irreducible. So $\mathcal L_{(1)}$ is invertible, and
\[
\eta'^\top\Sigma_\alpha\eta'=2\zeta'^\top v'
=2\eta'^\top(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)\mathcal L_{(1)}^{-1}(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)\eta'
\]
for every $\eta'\in\R^{k-1}$. Both matrices are symmetric, hence
$\Sigma_\alpha=2(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)\mathcal L_{(1)}^{-1}(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)$. Now we take
determinants. The matrix determinant lemma gives
$\det(D'-\alpha'\alpha'^\top)=\prod_{i\ge2}\alpha_i\,(1-\sum_{i\ge2}\alpha_i)=\prod_i\alpha_i$.
So $\det\Sigma_\alpha=2^{k-1}(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2/\tree(c(\alpha))$, and putting this into
Definition~\ref{def:tilt} gives the formula for $C_F(\alpha)$.
\end{proof}

Part (b) needs a reversible face. Proposition~\ref{prop:forest} at the end of this section gives a
formula of the same kind for every irreducible face.

\begin{corollary}[symmetric faces]\label{cor:symface}
Let $F$ satisfy \textup{(H)} with $k\ge2$, and let $Q_F$ be symmetric with constant row sums.
Then $\lambda_F=q_i-\sum_{j\in F\setminus\{i\}}q_{ij}$ for every $i\in F$, $\alpha^*_F$ is uniform
on $F$, and
\[
K_F=\mu(F)\,k^{(k+1)/2}\sqrt{\tree(q_F)}\,(4\pi)^{-(k-1)/2},
\]
where $\tree(q_F)$ is $\tree(c)$ for the conductances $c_{ij}=q_{ij}$, $i,j\in F$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The positive vector $\ones$ is a right and left eigenvector of $Q_F$, so it belongs to the
Perron root. So $\lambda_F$ is minus the row sum, $r_F$ and $l_F$ are multiples of $\ones$, and
$\alpha^*_F=\ones/k$.
At $\alpha=\ones/k$, Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b) with $\nu=\ones$ gives
$r_\alpha=\ones$, $l_\alpha=\alpha$ and $(\mu\cdot r_\alpha)(l_\alpha\cdot\ones)=\mu(F)$.
Also $c_{ij}(\alpha)=q_{ij}/k$, so $\tree(c(\alpha))=k^{-(k-1)}\tree(q_F)$, and
$\prod_i\alpha_i=k^{-k}$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The local law and the face maxima}\label{sec:locallaw}

Inside the face the occupation fractions fluctuate on the scale $T^{-1/2}$ in $k-1$ directions. So
a single composition gets the factor $T^{(k-1)/2}$ times the volume $N^{-(k-1)}$ of a lattice cell.

\begin{theorem}[local law]\label{thm:local}
Let $F$ satisfy (H) with $k\ge2$, and let $\mathcal K\subset\Delta_F^\circ$ be
compact. Uniformly for $\alpha\in\mathcal K$ and $T\ge1$,
\[
f_T(\alpha)=T^{(k-1)/2}C_F(\alpha)e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}\bigl(1+O_{\mathcal K}(1/T)\bigr).
\]
For $F=\{i\}$, $f_T=\mu_ie^{-q_iT}$.
\end{theorem}

In words, the density of the visit fractions on the event that the chain stays
in $F$ is a Gaussian factor $T^{(k-1)/2}$ times the large-deviation factor
$e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}$ times the constant $C_F(\alpha)$, with a relative error
$O(1/T)$ that is uniform away from the boundary of the face.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:torus}. The
generating function of the sum over words that defines $H$ is a matrix resolvent, so
Cauchy's formula writes $H$ as an integral over a torus with the radii
$\theta_2(\alpha),\dots,\theta_k(\alpha)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:torus}).
All the coefficients are nonnegative, so the integrand is largest at the real
point, and a Laplace lemma with a parameter (Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace}) gives the
expansion. The Hessian at the real point is computed from the Perron root of a
perturbed generator (Lemma~\ref{lem:perron}), which brings in $\Sigma_\alpha$.
The hypothesis $\mu(F)>0$ is needed, since $f_T=C_F=0$ when
$\mu(F)=0$. Also $f_T(\alpha)$ is continuous and positive in $(T,\alpha)$, as
$H$ is a convergent power series with some $W(m)>0$ by (H).

\begin{lemma}[discrete and continuum]\label{lem:discrete}
Let $F$ satisfy (H) with $k\ge2$, and let $\mathcal K\subset\Delta_F^\circ$ be
compact. Uniformly for $\operatorname{supp}n=F$ with $n/N\in\mathcal K$ and
$1\le T\le N^{1/2}$,
$P_N(n)=N^{-(k-1)}f_T(n/N)(1+O_{\mathcal K}(T^2/N))$.
\end{lemma}

In words, the probability of a composition is the continuum density times the volume
$N^{-(k-1)}$ of a lattice cell. The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:torus}. With
Theorem~\ref{thm:local} the lemma replaces the term $O_{\mathcal K}(1+\log T)$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:first}(i) by $\frac{k-1}2\log T+\log C_F(n/N)+O_{\mathcal K}(1/T+T^2/N)$.

\begin{theorem}[face maxima]\label{thm:facemax}
Let $F$ satisfy (H). If $k\ge2$, then uniformly for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$,
\[
M_F=N^{-(k-1)}T^{(k-1)/2}K_Fe^{-T\lambda_F}\bigl(1+O(1/T+T^2/N)\bigr),
\]
and every maximizer $\hat n$ satisfies $\hat n/N=\alpha^*_F+O(1/T)$. For $k=1$,
$M_{\{i\}}$ is given by~\eqref{eq:vertexmax}. In both cases,
uniformly for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logmax}
\log M_F=-(k-1)L+\tfrac{k-1}2\log T+\log K_F-T\lambda_F+O(1/T+T^2/N).
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

In words, the best composition on $F$ sits within $O(N/T)$ of $N\alpha^*_F$,
and its probability is the Gaussian peak of the local law at $\alpha^*_F$. The
factor $N^{-(k-1)}$ is the volume of a lattice cell, and $T^{(k-1)/2}$ is the
height of a Gaussian density whose spread in each free direction is of order
$T^{-1/2}$.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} Near $\alpha^*_F$ we use the local law and
Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete}, and maximize a Gaussian with a small linear
perturbation. Away from $\alpha^*_F$ the rate exceeds $\lambda_F$ by a fixed
amount, and Lemma~\ref{lem:upper}, applied with finitely many vectors $\theta$,
shows that these compositions are exponentially less likely in $T$. For bounded
$T$ positivity and continuity suffice.

\begin{proof}
For $k=1$ this is~\eqref{eq:vertexmax}. Let $k\ge2$. Put
$X=N^{-(k-1)}T^{(k-1)/2}K_Fe^{-T\lambda_F}$, the value that the theorem claims for $M_F$. We
compare every $P_N(n)$ with $X$, and we write $x=\alpha'-\alpha^{*\prime}_F$ for the
displacement of $\alpha$ from $\alpha^*_F$. We fix a ball $U$ around $\alpha^*_F$, bound the
compositions outside $U$, then those inside $U$, and at the end we locate the maximizer.

\emph{The ball $U$.} By Propositions~\ref{prop:rate}(a) and~\ref{prop:constant}(a), $I_F$ and
$\log C_F$ are real-analytic near $\alpha^*_F$, and $I_F$ has its minimum $\lambda_F$ there with
a positive definite Hessian. So there are a closed ball
$U=\{|x|\le\rho_0\}\subset\Delta_F^\circ$ and positive constants $c_U$, $C_U$, $C_1$ and $C_3$
such that on $U$
\[
c_U|x|^2\le I_F-\lambda_F\le C_U|x|^2,\qquad
\bigl|\log(C_F/K_F)-\gamma\cdot x\bigr|\le C_1|x|^2,
\]
where $\gamma=\nabla\log C_F(\alpha^*_F)$, and such that $\log C_F$ is $C_3$-Lipschitz on $U$.

\emph{Outside $U$.} We show that a composition with $n/N\notin U^\circ$ has
$P_N(n)=X\cdot O(T^{(k-1)/2}e^{-2\eta T})$ for some $\eta>0$. The set $\Delta_F\setminus U^\circ$
is compact and does not contain $\alpha^*_F$, and $I_F$ is lower semicontinuous. So
Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(a) gives $\eta>0$ with $I_F\ge\lambda_F+3\eta$ on this set. Recall
that $I_F(\beta)=\sup_{u>0}\langle q-A_Fu/u,\beta\rangle$ and that $A_Fu/u\in V_F$. So every
$\beta$ in the set has a vector $\theta\in V_F$ with
$\langle q-\theta,\beta\rangle>\lambda_F+2\eta$, and the same $\theta$ works for all points near
$\beta$. By compactness finitely many vectors $\theta_j\in V_F$ are enough, that is,
$\max_j\langle q-\theta_j,\beta\rangle>\lambda_F+2\eta$ for every
$\beta\in\Delta_F\setminus U^\circ$. Now let $\supp n=F$ with $n/N\notin U^\circ$, and take $j$
that attains the maximum at $\beta=n/N$. Then $h\langle n,q-\theta_j\rangle>T(\lambda_F+2\eta)$,
and Lemma~\ref{lem:upper} with $\theta=\theta_j$ gives
\[
P_N(n)\le C(T/N)^{k-1}e^{-T(\lambda_F+2\eta)}=X\cdot O\bigl(T^{(k-1)/2}e^{-2\eta T}\bigr).
\]

\emph{Inside $U$.} By Theorem~\ref{thm:local} and Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete} with $\mathcal K=U$,
for $\alpha=n/N\in U$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:insideU}
P_N(n)=X\,\tfrac{C_F(\alpha)}{K_F}\,e^{-T(I_F(\alpha)-\lambda_F)}(1+E),
\qquad|E|\le C_2(1/T+T^2/N).
\end{equation}
For the upper bound we use the bounds on $U$. For $T\ge2C_1/c_U$ the logarithm of the two middle
factors is at most
\[
\gamma\cdot x+C_1|x|^2-c_UT|x|^2\le\gamma\cdot x-\tfrac{c_UT}2|x|^2\le\frac{|\gamma|^2}{2c_UT},
\]
where the last step is the maximum over $x$ of a concave quadratic. So
$P_N(n)\le X(1+O(1/T+T^2/N))$ for $n/N\in U$. The bound outside $U$ is smaller than this for
large $T$, and hence $M_F\le X(1+O(1/T+T^2/N))$.

For the lower bound we take a lattice point next to $\alpha^*_F$. Let $n^0$ have support $F$ and
$|n^0_i-N\alpha^*_{F,i}|<1$ for all $i$. It exists for $N\ge N_0$ since every $\alpha^*_{F,i}$ is
positive, and its displacement has $|x|\le k/N$. Then $C_F(n^0/N)\ge K_Fe^{-kC_3/N}$ and
$T(I_F(n^0/N)-\lambda_F)\le C_Uk^2T/N^2$, so~\eqref{eq:insideU} gives
$P_N(n^0)\ge X(1-O(1/T+T^2/N))$.

\emph{Bounded $T$.} Fix $T_2$ so large that the two bounds above give
$M_F/X\in[\frac12,\frac32]$ for $T\ge T_2$. For $1\le T\le T_2$ the error term $O(1/T)$ is of
order $1$, so it is enough to show that $M_F/X\in[c,C]$ with constants $0<c<C$. For such $T$,
$N^{k-1}X$ lies between two positive constants, and so does $f_T$ on $U$, because it is positive
and continuous on $[1,T_2]\times U$. So Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete} bounds $N^{k-1}P_N(n)$ above for
$n/N\in U$ and bounds $N^{k-1}P_N(n^0)$ below, and the bound outside $U$ is $O(N^{-(k-1)})$.
This gives the first display for all $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$, and~\eqref{eq:logmax} is its logarithm.

\emph{The maximizer.} Let $\hat n$ be a maximizer and let $\hat x$ be the displacement of
$\hat n/N$. For $T\ge T_3$ with $T_3$ large, the bound outside $U$ is below
$X/2\le P_N(n^0)$, so $\hat n/N\in U$, and the error in~\eqref{eq:insideU} has $|E|\le\frac12$.
We take logarithms in $P_N(\hat n)\ge P_N(n^0)$ and use~\eqref{eq:insideU} on the left. There
$\log(C_F/K_F)\le C_3|\hat x|$ and $T(I_F-\lambda_F)\ge c_UT|\hat x|^2$, while the right side is
at least $\log X-C(1/T+T^2/N)$. So
\[
c_UT|\hat x|^2\le C_3|\hat x|+C_4\Bigl(\frac1T+\frac{T^2}N\Bigr)\le C_3|\hat x|+\frac{2C_4}T .
\]
The last step holds because $T\le N^{1/3}$ gives $T^2/N\le1/T$, and this is the step that needs
the exponent $\frac13$. Solving the quadratic inequality gives
$|\hat x|\le\bigl(C_3+\sqrt{C_3^2+8c_UC_4}\bigr)/(2c_UT)=O(1/T)$. For $1\le T<T_3$ the claim
holds since $|\hat x|$ is bounded.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Crossings and tie windows}\label{sec:crossings}

We now compare two faces. By Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax}, the
difference $\log M_F-\log M_G$ is an explicit increasing function of $T$ up to
an error $O(1/T+T^2/N)$, and its zero is the crossing.

\begin{theorem}[the crossing equation]\label{thm:crossing}
Let $F$ and $G$ satisfy (H), with $\Delta=k_F-k_G\ge1$ and
$\delta=\lambda_G-\lambda_F>0$, and let $D_N(T)=\log M_F-\log M_G$.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item For large $N$, $D_N$ has a zero in
$[\frac{\Delta}{2\delta}L,\frac{2\Delta}{\delta}L]$.
\item For large $N$, every zero $T_N\in[1,N^{1/3}]$ of $D_N$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossing}
\delta T_N+\frac\Delta2\log T_N=\Delta L-\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}
+O\Bigl(\frac1L\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\item All such zeros lie in one interval of length $O(1/L)$, and
\[
T_N=\frac\Delta\delta\Bigl[L-\frac12\log L+b_{FG}\Bigr]
+O\Bigl(\frac{\log L}L\Bigr),\qquad
b_{FG}=-\frac12\log\frac\Delta\delta-\frac1\Delta\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
We subtract~\eqref{eq:logmax} for $G$ from~\eqref{eq:logmax} for $F$. Uniformly on
$[1,N^{1/3}]$,
\[
D_N(T)=-\Delta L+\Xi(T)+\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}+O\Bigl(\frac1T+\frac{T^2}N\Bigr),
\qquad\Xi(T)=\delta T+\frac\Delta2\log T .
\]
The function $\Xi$ is increasing, with $\Xi'\ge\delta$.

(i) At $T=\frac{\Delta}{2\delta}L$ the display gives $D_N=-\frac\Delta2L+O(\log L)<0$, and at
$T=\frac{2\Delta}\delta L$ it gives $D_N=\Delta L+O(\log L)>0$. For fixed $N$, $M_F$ is a maximum
of finitely many polynomials in $h$, and it is positive because $F$ is admissible. The same holds
for $G$. So $D_N$ is continuous and has a zero in between.

(ii) First we locate a zero $T_N\in[1,N^{1/3}]$ roughly. The error term is $O(1)$ on
$[1,N^{1/3}]$, so $\Xi(T_N)=\Delta L-\log(K_F/K_G)+O(1)$. This is larger than
$\Xi(\frac\Delta{2\delta}L)=\frac\Delta2L+O(\log L)$, so $T_N>\frac\Delta{2\delta}L$ because
$\Xi$ is increasing. Also $\delta T_N\le\Xi(T_N)$, so $T_N\le\frac{2\Delta}\delta L$. With $T_N$
of order $L$ the error term is $O(1/T_N+T_N^2/N)=O(1/L)$, and $D_N(T_N)=0$
is~\eqref{eq:crossing}.

(iii) Let $T^\circ$ be the solution of $\Xi(T^\circ)=\Delta L-\log(K_F/K_G)$. By (ii) every zero
has $\Xi(T_N)=\Xi(T^\circ)+O(1/L)$, and $\Xi'\ge\delta$, so every zero is within $O(1/L)$ of
$T^\circ$. To solve for $T^\circ$, note that $T^\circ=\frac\Delta\delta L+O(\log L)$, so
$\log T^\circ=\log L+\log\frac\Delta\delta+O(\log L/L)$. Putting this into
$\delta T^\circ=\Delta L-\frac\Delta2\log T^\circ-\log(K_F/K_G)$ and dividing by $\delta$ gives
the explicit form.
\end{proof}

In words, the larger face $F$ pays $N^{-\Delta}$ for spreading and gains
$e^{\delta T}$ for staying, and its Gaussian peak gives an extra factor
$T^{\Delta/2}$. The crossing balances the three. In the numerical test after
Corollary~\ref{cor:C4} the explicit form in (iii) is still off by $0.06$ at $N=10^{14}$, so
computations should solve~\eqref{eq:crossing}. We do not prove that the zero of $D_N$ is unique in
general.

At a switch value $\tau^*$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} several faces minimize
$\Phi_{\tau^*}$, sometimes more than two, as all faces do at $\tau^*=1$ for the
complete graph (Corollary~\ref{cor:paperB}). Then the competition plays out in a window $T=\tau^*(L-\frac12\log L+t)$ with $t$ of
order $1$. In this window each tied face has a line $\ell_F(t)$, and the mode
follows the highest line.

\begin{theorem}[tie windows]\label{thm:window}
Fix $\tau^*>0$. Let $m^*$ be the least value of $\Phi_{\tau^*}$ over the
admissible faces and $\mathcal F^*$ the set of minimizers. Assume that every
face in $\mathcal F^*$ is irreducible. By Theorem~\ref{thm:hull}(c) this holds
if $Q$ has symmetric support or $\mu$ has full support. Put $T=\tau^*(L-\frac12\log L+t)$, and for
$F\in\mathcal F^*$ let
$\ell_F(t)=\log K_F+\frac{k_F-1}2\log\tau^*-\tau^*\lambda_Ft$ and
$E(t)=\max_{G\in\mathcal F^*}\ell_G(t)$. The function $E$ is convex and piecewise linear. We call
the finitely many points where its slope changes the \emph{kinks} of $E$, and we write $t_j$ for
them.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Uniformly for $t$ in compact sets, for $F\in\mathcal F^*$,
$\log M_F=-m^*L+\frac{m^*}2\log L+\ell_F(t)+O(\log L/L)$.
\item There are $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for large $N$ and
$|T/L-\tau^*|\le\varepsilon_1$, every admissible $F\notin\mathcal F^*$ has
$M_F\le N^{-\varepsilon_0}\max_{G\in\mathcal F^*}M_G$.
\item There are $\varepsilon,t_0>0$ such that the following holds for large $N$. If
$t_0\le t\le\varepsilon L$, then every global mode lies on a face $F\in\mathcal F^*$ with
$\ell_F=E$ on $[t_0,\infty)$. If $-\varepsilon L\le t\le-t_0$, then every global mode lies on a
face $F\in\mathcal F^*$ with $\ell_F=E$ on $(-\infty,-t_0]$. A face of the first kind has the
least $\lambda_F$ in $\mathcal F^*$, and a face of the second kind has the largest.
\end{enumerate}
Consequently the following holds for every $\eta>0$ and large $N$. If $|t|\le\varepsilon L$ and
$t$ is at distance at least $\eta$ from every kink of $E$, then every global mode lies on a face
whose line equals $E$ near $t$. So for $|t|\le\varepsilon L$ the line of the face that carries
the global mode changes only at $T=\tau^*(L-\frac12\log L+t_j)+o(1)$, where $t_j$ runs over the
kinks of $E$, and it changes near each kink. Faces with identical lines (the same $k_F$,
$\lambda_F$ and $K_F$) tie at this order and give no crossing.
\end{theorem}

In words, inside the window the logarithm of every tied face maximum is the
same leading term plus the line $\ell_F(t)$, faces that do not tie are smaller by
a power of $N$, and far out in the window the face with the least (for $t>0$) or
largest (for $t<0$) exit rate wins. In between, the mode changes face only where the highest
line changes, namely near the kinks of $E$.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} In the window $T/L$ is close to $\tau^*$. For a tied face the terms
$-(k_F-1)L$ and $-T\lambda_F$ of~\eqref{eq:logmax} add up to
$-m^*L+\tau^*\lambda_F(\frac12\log L-t)$, because $\tau^*\lambda_F+k_F-1=m^*$. The term
$\frac{k_F-1}2\log T$ is $\frac{k_F-1}2\log L$ plus a constant, up to a small error. So the
multiples of $\log L$ add up to $\frac{m^*}2\log L$. This and $-m^*L$ are the same for all tied
faces, and what is left is the line $\ell_F(t)$. This is (i). A face that does not tie has
a larger exponent $\Phi_{\tau^*}$, and the first-order bounds already make it smaller by a power of
$N$. This is (ii). For (iii), two tied faces with different exit rates have lines of different
slopes, so the gap between their lines grows linearly in $|t|$ and beats every error term once
$|t|$ is large.

\begin{proof}
Every $F\in\mathcal F^*$ is admissible and irreducible, so it satisfies (H) by
Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}(b). We first put $T$ into~\eqref{eq:logmax}. Write
$T=\tau^*L(1+y)$ with $y=(t-\frac12\log L)/L$, so that $|y|\le\frac12$ for $|t|\le L/4$ and
large $N$. Then $\log T=\log\tau^*+\log L+\log(1+y)$ and
$T\lambda_F=\tau^*\lambda_F(L-\frac12\log L+t)$, and the error term of~\eqref{eq:logmax} is
$O(1/L)$. Since $\tau^*\lambda_F+k_F-1=m^*$, we get, uniformly for $|t|\le L/4$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:windowline}
\log M_F=-m^*L+\tfrac{m^*}2\log L+\ell_F(t)+\tfrac{k_F-1}2\log(1+y)+O(1/L)
\qquad(F\in\mathcal F^*).
\end{equation}

(i) For $t$ in a compact set, $y=O(\log L/L)$, so $\log(1+y)=O(\log L/L)$
in~\eqref{eq:windowline}.

(ii) Let $g>0$ be the least value of $\Phi_{\tau^*}(F)-m^*$ over the admissible
$F\notin\mathcal F^*$. (If there is no such face, (ii) says nothing, and we put $g=1$.) Put
$\varepsilon_1=\min\bigl(\tau^*/2,\,g/(4q_{\max}+4)\bigr)$. (Any constant below $g/(2q_{\max})$ would do
in the second entry. We use that $0\le\lambda_F\le q_{\max}$ for every face, since the Perron root of
$Q_F$ lies between its largest diagonal entry and $0$.) The entry $\tau^*/2$ keeps $T$ of
order $L$. Namely, if $|T/L-\tau^*|\le\varepsilon_1$, then
$\frac12\tau^*L\le T\le\frac32\tau^*L$. So for large $N$ we have $T_0\le T\le N^{1/3}$, which is
the range of Theorem~\ref{thm:first}(ii), and $\log T=O(\log L)$. Take an admissible
$F\notin\mathcal F^*$ and any $G\in\mathcal F^*$. The difference $\Phi_\tau(F)-\Phi_\tau(G)$ is at least $g$ at $\tau=\tau^*$,
and its slope in $\tau$ is $\lambda_F-\lambda_G$, which is at most $q_{\max}$ in absolute value. So
$\Phi_\tau(F)-\Phi_\tau(G)\ge g/2$ for $|\tau-\tau^*|\le\varepsilon_1$. With $\tau=T/L$ the
upper bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:first}(ii) for $F$ and the lower bound for $G$ give
$\log M_F-\log M_G\le-(\Phi_\tau(F)-\Phi_\tau(G))L+O(\log L)\le-\frac g4L$ for large $N$. So (ii)
holds with $\varepsilon_0=g/4$.

(iii) Take $\varepsilon=\min(\frac14,\varepsilon_1/(2\tau^*))$. Then for $|t|\le\varepsilon L$
and large $N$ we have $|T/L-\tau^*|=\tau^*|y|\le\varepsilon_1$, so (ii) applies and no global
mode lies on a face outside $\mathcal F^*$. It remains to compare the tied faces. Let $t_0\ge1$
be larger than every $|t_j|$, so that $E$ is a single line on $[t_0,\infty)$. Let
$F\in\mathcal F^*$ have this line, and let $G\in\mathcal F^*$ have another line. We show that
$M_F>M_G$ for $t_0\le t\le\varepsilon L$ if $t_0$ and $N$ are large. Since $\ell_F\ge\ell_G$
on $[t_0,\infty)$, the slopes satisfy $\lambda_F\le\lambda_G$. As both faces are tied,
\[
\Delta=k_F-k_G=\tau^*(\lambda_G-\lambda_F)\ge0 .
\]
There are two cases.

First let $\Delta=0$. Then the two lines are parallel, and $\ell_F-\ell_G=\log(K_F/K_G)>0$. The
terms with $\log(1+y)$ in~\eqref{eq:windowline} are the same for $F$ and $G$, so
$\log(M_F/M_G)=\log(K_F/K_G)+O(1/L)>0$ for large $N$.

Now let $\Delta>0$. Then $\ell_F(t)-\ell_G(t)=\Delta t+\ell_F(0)-\ell_G(0)$,
and~\eqref{eq:windowline} gives
\[
\log\frac{M_F}{M_G}=\Delta t+\ell_F(0)-\ell_G(0)+\frac\Delta2\log(1+y)+O\Bigl(\frac1L\Bigr).
\]
For large $N$ and $t\ge1$ we have $|\log(1+y)|\le2|y|\le(2t+\log L)/L\le t/2$. So the right
side is at least $\frac34\Delta t-C$, which is positive for $t\ge t_0$ once $t_0$ is large.

So for $t_0\le t\le\varepsilon L$ every global mode lies on a face whose line is $E$ on
$[t_0,\infty)$. The slope of $\ell_F$ is $-\tau^*\lambda_F$, and $E$ follows the line of largest
slope on the far right, so such a face has the least $\lambda_F$ in $\mathcal F^*$. The case
$t\le-t_0$ is the same with the signs reversed.

\emph{The consequence.} Fix $\eta>0$ and let
$S_\eta=[-t_0,t_0]\setminus\bigcup_j(t_j-\eta,t_j+\eta)$, a finite union of closed intervals
without kinks. Take $G\in\mathcal F^*$. The function $E-\ell_G$ is convex, piecewise linear and
nonnegative, and its slope changes only at kinks of $E$. So its zero set is empty, or a single
kink of $E$, or an interval whose finite endpoints are kinks of $E$. Hence each interval of
$S_\eta$ lies inside the zero set or is disjoint from it. In the first case $\ell_G=E$ on the
interval, and in the second $E-\ell_G\ge g_\eta$ there for some $g_\eta>0$. Now let $t\in S_\eta$
and let $F\in\mathcal F^*$ have $\ell_F(t)=E(t)$. By (i), every $G\in\mathcal F^*$ whose line is
not $E$ near $t$ has $\log(M_F/M_G)\ge g_\eta-O(\log L/L)>0$, and by (ii) the faces outside
$\mathcal F^*$ are smaller still. This is the claim for $t\in S_\eta$. For
$t_0\le|t|\le\varepsilon L$ it is (iii). Since $\eta$ is arbitrary, the line of the face that
carries the mode can change only at $t=t_j+o(1)$. It does change near each kink, because $E$ is
a different line on the two sides of a kink.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The two-state walk and the complete graph}\label{sec:papersAB}

We use the polynomials $S_{a,b}$ and $S_N$, the root $u_N$, the crossing $p_N$,
$z_N=N(1-p_N)$ and $c_0=\frac12\log(\pi/8)$ of Section~\ref{sec:notation}. The
first result shows that, at every $N$, the comparison of a symmetric pair with
one of its states is exactly the two-state walk.

\begin{proposition}[an exact pair identity]\label{prop:pair}
Let $i\ne j$ with $q_{ij}=q_{ji}=a$, $q_i=q_j=q$ and $\mu_i=\mu_j>0$, and let
$F=\{i,j\}$. For every $N\ge2$, $1\le m\le N-1$ and $h>0$ such that $I+hQ$ is
stochastic and $qh<1$,
\[
\frac{P_N(me_i+(N-m)e_j)}{P_N(Ne_i)}=S_{m,N-m}(u),\qquad u=\frac{ah}{1-qh}.
\]
So the balanced compositions, with $m=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ or
$m=\lceil N/2\rceil$, are the most likely ones with support $F$,
$M_F=M_{\{i\}}S_N(u)$, and $M_F>M_{\{i\}}$ exactly when $u>u_N$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We group the paths by their number of switches.
A path with this composition is a word in $i,j$ with $m$ letters $i$. Each stay has probability
$1-qh$ and each switch $ah$, so a word with $s$ switches has probability
$\mu_i(1-qh)^{N-1-s}(ah)^s=\mu_i(1-qh)^{N-1}u^s$, while $P_N(Ne_i)=\mu_i(1-qh)^{N-1}$. Sum over
these words and divide. This gives $\sum_wu^{s(w)}=S_{m,N-m}(u)$, which is the identity.
By~\cite[Proposition~4.1]{paperA} the bin polynomials grow coefficientwise as the bin moves toward the
middle, and $S_{m,N-m}=S_{N-m,m}$. So the balanced $m$ give the largest $S_{m,N-m}(u)$. Finally
$S_N$ increases strictly and equals $1$ only at $u_N$ (Section~\ref{sec:notation}).
\end{proof}

Lean checks the identity for a chain with two states. For $d\ge3$ the paths with
this composition never leave $F$, so the same computation applies, but this case
is not in Lean.

\begin{corollary}[the two-state walk]\label{cor:paperA}
Let $d=2$, $q_{12}=q_{21}=1$ and $\mu=(\frac12,\frac12)$, the walk of~\cite{paperA} with
$T=N(1-p)$. Then $K_{\{1\}}=K_{\{2\}}=\frac12$ and $K_{\{1,2\}}=\sqrt{2/\pi}$, so
$b_{FG}=c_0$ for $F=\{1,2\}$ and $G=\{1\}$. For every $N\ge2$, $M_{\{1,2\}}=M_{\{1\}}$ holds
exactly at $T=z_N$, and $z_N+\frac12\log z_N=L+c_0+O(1/L)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Corollary~\ref{cor:symface} with $k=2$, $\tree(q_F)=1$ and $\mu(F)=1$ gives $K_{\{1,2\}}$, and
then $b_{FG}=-\log(K_{\{1,2\}}/K_{\{1\}})=c_0$. Proposition~\ref{prop:pair} with $a=q=1$ gives
$M_{\{1,2\}}=M_{\{1\}}S_N(h/(1-h))$, and $h/(1-h)=u_N$ exactly when $p=p_N$. So for every $N$
the only zero of $D_N$ is $z_N$, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}(i) it lies in
$[L/2,2L]\subset[1,N^{1/3}]$ for large $N$. Then Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}(ii) with
$\Delta=\delta=1$ gives the equation.
\end{proof}

This is the crossing equation of~\cite{paperA} without its terms $1/(8z_N)$ and $R_N$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm} recovers the term $1/(8z_N)$. In its notation this term is
$(c_{\{1\}}-c_{\{1,2\}})/z_N$ with $c_{\{1\}}=0$ and $c_{\{1,2\}}=-\frac18$, and
\eqref{eq:crossingnext} with $\Delta=\delta=1$ gives
$z_N+\frac12\log z_N=L+c_0+\frac1{8z_N}+O(L^{-2})$.

\begin{corollary}[the complete graph]\label{cor:paperB}
Let $Q=J-dI$, where $J$ is the all-ones matrix, and let $\mu$ be uniform, the model
of~\cite{paperB}. A face of $k$ states has $\lambda_F=d-k$, $\alpha^*_F$ uniform and
$K_F=\frac1dk^{k+1/2}(4\pi)^{-(k-1)/2}$. So $\Phi_1(F)=d-1$ for every face, and all faces tie at
$\tau=1$. The crossing of a face of $k\ge2$ states with a vertex has $\Delta=\delta=k-1$ and
$b_{FG}=a_k=\frac12\log(4\pi)-\frac{k+1/2}{k-1}\log k$, the constant of the vertex crossing
of~\cite{paperB}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each $Q_F$ is symmetric with row sums $-(d-k)$, and the complete graph on $F$ has $k^{k-2}$
spanning trees by Cayley's formula. So Corollary~\ref{cor:symface} with $\mu(F)=k/d$ gives
$\lambda_F$, $\alpha^*_F$ and $K_F$. A vertex has $K=\frac1d$ and $\lambda=d-1$, so
$b_{FG}=-\frac1{k-1}\log(dK_F)=a_k$. The arithmetic that gives $b_{FG}$ from $K_F$ here and in
Corollary~\ref{cor:paperA} is checked in Lean. At $\tau^*=1$, Theorem~\ref{thm:window} describes
the tie window, and~\cite{paperB} resolves this window in full.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Reducible faces}\label{sec:reducible}

This subsection shows by an example that the coefficient $\frac12$ can fail on a face that is
not irreducible.

\begin{remark}[scope of the $\frac12$]\label{rem:half}
The coefficient $\frac12$ of $\log\log N$ in Theorems~\ref{thm:crossing} and~\ref{thm:window} is
due to the power $T^{(k-1)/2}$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax}, so it needs only (H). At a kink of
$E$ where the line of $G$ gives way to that of $F$ as $t$ increases, $\Delta=\tau^*\delta$ and
$t_j=b_{FG}$. So every change in a tie window is a crossing of Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}. The
best composition of a reducible face can visit a state only once,
with no Gaussian window in that direction. Then the $\frac12$ can fail, and in the next example it
is $1$ and then $0$.
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}[a directed $3$-cycle]\label{prop:threecycle}
Let $d=3$, $q_{12}=b>2$, $q_{23}=q_{31}=1$, all other rates $0$, and
$\mu_1=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item The admissible faces are $\{1\}$, $\{1,2\}$ (reducible, $\lambda=1$) and
$\{1,2,3\}$ (irreducible, $\lambda=0$). Exactly
$M_{\{1\}}=(1-bh)^{N-1}$ and $M_{\{1,2\}}=bh(1-h)^{N-2}$, and the latter is
attained only at $n=(1,N-1,0)$. For the full face,
$\alpha^*=(1,b,b)/(1+2b)$, $\det\nabla^2I=(1+2b)^4/(3b^2)$ and
$K_{\{1,2,3\}}=(1+2b)^2/(2\sqrt3\,\pi b)$.
\item The equation $M_{\{1\}}=M_{\{1,2\}}$ has exactly one root $T^{(1)}_N$ with $bh<1$. It
satisfies $(b-1)T+\log T=L-\log b+O(T^2/N)$, so
$T^{(1)}_N=\frac1{b-1}[L-\log L+\log\frac{b-1}b]+O(\log L/L)$, and the
coefficient of $\log\log N$ in the bracket is $1$.
\item Every root $T\in[L/2,2L]$ of $M_{\{1,2\}}=M_{\{1,2,3\}}$ satisfies
$T=L+\log\frac{2\sqrt3\,\pi b^2}{(1+2b)^2}+O(1/L)$, so the coefficient of
$\log\log N$ is $0$. These roots lie in an interval of length $O(1/L)$.
\item Both are changes of the global mode. For large $N$ and $T\le N^{1/3}$,
the mode is $(N,0,0)$ for $T<T^{(1)}_N$, it is $(1,N-1,0)$ between
$T^{(1)}_N$ and the smallest root in (iii), and it lies on the full face above
the largest root in (iii).
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In words, the face $\{1,2\}$ is reducible, and its best composition
$(1,N-1,0)$ visits state $1$ once and then stays in state $2$. Its maximum is
about $b(T/N)e^{-T}$, with the power $T^1$ where an irreducible pair would have
$T^{1/2}$. This changes the coefficient of $\log\log N$ at both crossings.

\begin{proof}
(i) \emph{The faces.} From state $1$ the chain can only move along $1\to2\to3\to1$. So a path
that starts at $1$ has support $\{1\}$, $\{1,2\}$ or $\{1,2,3\}$, and these are the admissible
faces. Inside $\{1,2\}$ the chain cannot return from $2$ to $1$, so this face is reducible with
components $\{1\}$ and $\{2\}$, of exit rates $b$ and $1$, and $\lambda_{\{1,2\}}=1$. The only
path with support $\{1\}$ stays at $1$ for $N-1$ steps, which gives $M_{\{1\}}$. A path with
support $\{1,2\}$ is $1^{n_1}2^{n_2}$, of probability $(1-bh)^{n_1-1}\,bh\,(1-h)^{n_2-1}$.
Raising $n_1$ by one multiplies this by $(1-bh)/(1-h)<1$, so the maximum is at $n_1=1$ only,
and it is $bh(1-h)^{N-2}$.

\emph{The full face.} Here $Q$ is a generator on $\{1,2,3\}$, so $\lambda=0$ and $r=\ones$. The
left vector $l$ is the stationary law. In it the three flows $b\,l_1$, $l_2$ and $l_3$ around the
cycle are equal, so $l=\alpha^*=(1,b,b)/(1+2b)$ and $(\mu\cdot r)(l\cdot\ones)=1$. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(a) it remains to find $\det\nabla^2I$ at $\alpha^*$, and then
$K_{\{1,2,3\}}=\sqrt{\det\nabla^2I}/(2\pi)$.

We first find the rate in closed form. By definition
\[
I(\alpha)=\sum_iq_i\alpha_i-\inf_{u>0}\Bigl[b\alpha_1\frac{u_2}{u_1}+\alpha_2\frac{u_3}{u_2}
+\alpha_3\frac{u_1}{u_3}\Bigr].
\]
The three ratios are positive numbers with product $1$, and every such triple occurs. So by the
AM--GM inequality the infimum is $3(b\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3)^{1/3}$, and
\[
I(\alpha)=\sum_iq_i\alpha_i-3b^{1/3}g(\alpha),\qquad g(\alpha)=(\alpha_1\alpha_2\alpha_3)^{1/3}.
\]
The first sum is linear, so the Hessian of $I$ is $-3b^{1/3}$ times the Hessian of $g$. In all
three coordinates, $\partial_i\partial_jg=-g\,\Omega_{ij}$ with
$\Omega=\frac13\operatorname{diag}(w)^2-\frac19ww^\top$ and $w=1/\alpha$ (entrywise). In the
coordinates $(\alpha_2,\alpha_3)$ we have $\alpha_1=1-\alpha_2-\alpha_3$, so raising $\alpha_2$ or
$\alpha_3$ moves $\alpha$ in the direction $v_2=e_2-e_1$ or $v_3=e_3-e_1$. Hence $\nabla^2I$ is
the $2\times2$ matrix with entries $3b^{1/3}g\,v_i^\top\Omega v_j$.

Now we evaluate at $\alpha^*$. Put $s=1+2b$ and $y=1/b$. Then $3b^{1/3}g=3b/s$ and
$w=s(1,y,y)$, and a short computation gives
\[
v_2^\top\Omega v_2=v_3^\top\Omega v_3=\tfrac29s^2(1+y+y^2),\qquad
v_2^\top\Omega v_3=\tfrac19s^2(2+2y-y^2).
\]
The determinant of this $2\times2$ matrix is a difference of two squares, namely
\[
\frac{s^4}{81}\bigl[(2+2y+2y^2)^2-(2+2y-y^2)^2\bigr]=\frac{s^4}{81}\cdot3y^2(2+y)^2
=\frac{s^6}{27b^4},
\]
since $2+y=s/b$. Hence $\det\nabla^2I=(3b/s)^2s^6/(27b^4)=s^4/(3b^2)$ and
$K_{\{1,2,3\}}=s^2/(2\sqrt3\,\pi b)$. The exact parts of (i) are checked in Lean.

(ii) We show that $D_1=\log M_{\{1,2\}}-\log M_{\{1\}}$ increases in $h$. By (i),
$D_1=\log(bh)+(N-2)\log(1-h)-(N-1)\log(1-bh)$, so
\[
D_1'(h)=\frac1h-\frac{N-2}{1-h}+\frac{b(N-1)}{1-bh}>0\qquad(bh<1),
\]
because $b>1$ gives $b/(1-bh)>1/(1-h)$. Also $D_1\to-\infty$ as $h\to0$ and $D_1\to\infty$ as
$bh\to1$. So there is exactly one root (this is checked in Lean). To locate it we expand in
$T=Nh$, which gives
\[
D_1=\log b+\log T-L+(b-1)T+O(T^2/N).
\]
This is negative at $T=L/(2(b-1))$ and positive at $T=L/(b-1)$ for large $N$. So the root lies
between them, and it satisfies the equation in (ii). Since the root is of order $L$, that
equation gives $\log T=\log L-\log(b-1)+O(\log L/L)$, and putting this back gives the expansion.

(iii) By (i), $\log M_{\{1,2\}}=\log b+\log T-L-T+O(T^2/N)$. The full face satisfies (H), and
Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax} with $k=3$ and $\lambda=0$ gives
$\log M_{\{1,2,3\}}=-2L+\log T+\log K_{\{1,2,3\}}+O(1/T+T^2/N)$. So on $[L/2,2L]$
\[
\log M_{\{1,2\}}-\log M_{\{1,2,3\}}=L-T+\log b-\log K_{\{1,2,3\}}+O(1/L).
\]
At a root the left side is $0$, which gives the formula in (iii) with the value of
$K_{\{1,2,3\}}$ from (i). Two roots then differ by $O(1/L)$.

(iv) We compare the three face maxima on three ranges of $T$. Note first that
Lemma~\ref{lem:upper} with $\theta=q$ gives $M_{\{1,2,3\}}\le C(T/N)^2$ for $T\le N^{1/3}$.

Let $T<T^{(1)}_N$. Then $M_{\{1\}}>M_{\{1,2\}}$ by (ii). Also $T<L/(b-1)$ by (ii), so
$M_{\{1\}}=(1-bh)^{N-1}\ge N^{-b/(b-1)-o(1)}$. This is larger than $C(T/N)^2$ because
$b>2$ gives $b/(b-1)<2$. So the mode is $(N,0,0)$.

Let $T>T^{(1)}_N$. Then $M_{\{1,2\}}>M_{\{1\}}$ by (ii), and we compare $\{1,2\}$ with the full
face. For $T\le L/2$ the bound on $M_{\{1,2,3\}}$ gives
$\log M_{\{1,2\}}-\log M_{\{1,2,3\}}\ge L-T-\log T-C>0$. On $[L/2,2L]$ the
difference in (iii) is continuous in $T$, positive at $L/2$ and negative at
$2L$. So it is positive below its smallest root and negative above its largest root. For
$2L\le T\le N^{1/3}$ the same expansions give a difference of at most $-L+C<0$. Since the
maximum of $M_{\{1,2\}}$ is attained only at $(1,N-1,0)$, this proves (iv).
\end{proof}

\subsection{The next term}\label{sec:nextterm}

The local law, the face maxima and the crossing equation each have a next term, of relative
order $1/T$. For a face $F$ that satisfies (H) with $k\ge2$ we write
$\Sigma_F=\Sigma_{\alpha^*_F}$ and $\gamma_F=\nabla\log C_F(\alpha^*_F)$, in the coordinates
$\alpha'$. The function $c$ and the constants $c_F$ below are not related to the conductances
$c_{ij}(\alpha)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}.

\begin{proposition}[the next term]\label{prop:nextterm}\label{rem:nextterm}
Let $F$ satisfy \textup{(H)}.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Let $k\ge2$. There is a real-analytic function $c$ on $\Delta_F^\circ$, given
by~\eqref{eq:c-alpha}, such that for every compact $\mathcal K\subset\Delta_F^\circ$, uniformly
for $\alpha\in\mathcal K$ and $T\ge1$,
\[
f_T(\alpha)=T^{(k-1)/2}C_F(\alpha)e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}
\Bigl(1+\frac{c(\alpha)}T+O_{\mathcal K}\Bigl(\frac1{T^2}\Bigr)\Bigr).
\]
\item Put $c_F=c(\alpha^*_F)+\frac12\gamma_F^\top\Sigma_F\gamma_F$ if $k\ge2$, and $c_F=0$ if
$k=1$. For large $N$, uniformly for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:logmaxnext}
\log M_F=-(k-1)L+\frac{k-1}2\log T+\log K_F-T\lambda_F+\frac{c_F}T
+O\Bigl(\frac1{T^2}+\frac{T^2}N\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\item Let $G$ also satisfy \textup{(H)}, with $\Delta$, $\delta$ and $D_N$ as in
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}. For large $N$, every zero $T_N\in[1,N^{1/3}]$ of $D_N$ satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:crossingnext}
T_N\Bigl(\Delta L-\frac\Delta2\log T_N-\delta T_N-\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}\Bigr)
=c_F-c_G+O\Bigl(\frac1L\Bigr).
\end{equation}
\item Let $F=\{i,j\}$ with $q_{ij}=q_{ji}>0$, $q_i=q_j$ and $\mu_i=\mu_j>0$. Then
$c_F=-1/(8q_{ij})$. Let $Q=J-dI$, and let $F$ have $k\ge2$ states with $\mu$ constant and
positive on $F$. Then $c_F=-(k-1)/8$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In words, the local law has a relative correction $c(\alpha)/T$, where $c(\alpha)$ is the
Gaussian mean of a polynomial in the Taylor coefficients of the torus integrand at its peak,
divided by the value of the amplitude of the integrand there. The best composition does not sit exactly at
$N\alpha^*_F$. It moves by about $N\Sigma_F\gamma_F/T$ toward larger $C_F$, and this gains
$\frac12\gamma_F^\top\Sigma_F\gamma_F/T$ in the logarithm. The crossing equation~\eqref{eq:crossing}
then gains the term $(c_G-c_F)/T_N$ on its right side, with an error $O(L^{-2})$.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:laplace}. For (i) we carry the Laplace
lemma one step further (Lemma~\ref{lem:laplace2}). The terms of order $T^{-1/2}$ and $T^{-3/2}$
are odd and integrate to $0$, so the error is $O(T^{-2})$. For (ii) the logarithm of the local law
near $\alpha^*_F$ is a concave quadratic plus the linear term $\gamma_F\cdot x$, and we complete
the square. The maximizer is within $O(1/T)$ of $\alpha^*_F$, and moving it to the nearest
lattice point changes the logarithm only by $O(T/N^2)$. Part (iii)
subtracts~\eqref{eq:logmaxnext} for the two faces. In (iv) we do not evaluate $c$ from its
formula. We compare our expansion with a second expansion of the same probability.

The two values in (iv) have different sources. The value for a symmetric pair is proved here.
It uses only Proposition~\ref{prop:pair}, the Bessel bound~\eqref{eq:planar-bessel}
of~\cite{paperA} and the classical expansion of $I_0+I_1$. These give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pairnext}
\log M_{\{i,j\}}=-L+\tfrac12\log T+\log K_{\{i,j\}}-T\lambda_{\{i,j\}}-\frac1{8q_{ij}T}
+O\Bigl(\frac1{T^2}+\frac{T^2}N\Bigr),
\end{equation}
for large $N$ and uniformly for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$. This expansion does not use the Laplace
lemma. The value for
the complete graph rests on the expansion of the balanced bins in~\cite[Theorem~3.1(i)]{paperB},
which we quote and do not prove again. In both cases the formula~\eqref{eq:c-alpha} gives the
same value. For the pair this is a short computation in the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm}(iv) in Appendix~\ref{app:nextterm},
and for the full faces of $K_3$ and $K_4$ the script \texttt{verify\_second\_order.py}
evaluates~\eqref{eq:c-alpha} exactly. For other faces $c_F$ has to be computed
from~\eqref{eq:c-alpha}, $\gamma_F$ and $\Sigma_F$, as we do for the $4$-cycle in
Section~\ref{sec:examples}.

\subsection{A tree formula for every face}\label{sec:forest}

The next proposition extends Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b) from reversible faces to every
irreducible face. It uses two counts of spanning trees in place of one, namely
$\tree$ as before and the count $\arb$ of spanning trees oriented toward a root, which is
defined just before Lemma~\ref{lem:forest}. Its proof rests on
Lemma~\ref{lem:forest} in Appendix~\ref{app:torus}.

\begin{proposition}[the constant on a general face]\label{prop:forest}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k\ge2$. For $\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$ put
\[
f_{ij}(\alpha)=l_{\alpha,i}\,q_{ij}\,r_{\alpha,j}\qquad(i\ne j\text{ in }F),
\]
which does not change when $r_\alpha$ is scaled.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The weights $f(\alpha)$ are the stationary flow of the tilted chain, that is,
$f_{ij}(\alpha)=\alpha_i(\widehat Q_\alpha)_{ij}$, and
$\sum_{j\ne i}f_{ij}(\alpha)=\sum_{j\ne i}f_{ji}(\alpha)=\alpha_i\theta_i(\alpha)$ for every $i$.
The number $\arb_i(f(\alpha))$ does not depend on the root $i$, and
\[
\det\Sigma_\alpha=\frac{(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2\,\tree\bigl(f(\alpha)+f(\alpha)^\top\bigr)}
{\arb(f(\alpha))^2}
\]
and
\[
C_F(\alpha)=\frac{(\mu\cdot r_\alpha)(l_\alpha\cdot\ones)}{(2\pi)^{(k-1)/2}}\,
\frac{\arb(f(\alpha))}{\prod_i\alpha_i\,\sqrt{\tree(f(\alpha)+f(\alpha)^\top)}} .
\]
\item Also $\arb(f(\alpha))/\prod_i\alpha_i
=\sum_{i\in F}\det\bigl(\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha)-A_F\bigr)_{(i)}$. At
$\alpha=\alpha^*_F$ this is $\prod_\rho(\rho-\lambda_F)$, the product over the other $k-1$
eigenvalues $\rho$ of $-Q_F$, counted with multiplicity. So with $l_F\cdot r_F=1$ and
$f_{ij}=l_{F,i}\,q_{ij}\,r_{F,j}$,
\[
K_F=\frac{(\mu\cdot r_F)(l_F\cdot\ones)}{(2\pi)^{(k-1)/2}}\,
\frac{\arb(f)}{\prod_i\alpha^*_{F,i}\,\sqrt{\tree(f+f^\top)}}
=\frac{(\mu\cdot r_F)(l_F\cdot\ones)}{(2\pi)^{(k-1)/2}}\,
\frac{\prod_\rho(\rho-\lambda_F)}{\sqrt{\tree(f+f^\top)}} .
\]
\item In the reversible case of Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b), $f(\alpha)$ is symmetric
and equals $c(\alpha)$, and \textup{(a)} reduces to Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b).
\item Let $F=\{1,\dots,k\}$ be a directed cycle, that is, $q_{i,i+1}=a_i>0$ with indices modulo
$k$, and $q_{ij}=0$ for all other $i\ne j$ in $F$. Then for every $\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$
\[
\det\Sigma_\alpha=\frac{k\,(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2}{\bigl(\prod_i\alpha_ia_i\bigr)^{(k-1)/k}} .
\]
If moreover $q_i=a_i$ for all $i$, then $\alpha^*_{F,i}=\varphi^*/a_i$ with
$\varphi^*=(\sum_i1/a_i)^{-1}$, and
\[
\det\Sigma_{\alpha^*_F}=\frac{k\,(\varphi^*)^{k+1}}{\prod_ia_i^2},\qquad
K_F=\frac{\mu(F)\,\prod_ia_i}{(2\pi)^{(k-1)/2}\,\sqrt k\,(\varphi^*)^{(k+1)/2}} .
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In words, up to the factor $(2\pi)^{-(k-1)/2}/\prod_i\alpha_i$, the Gaussian factor in $C_F$ is a count of oriented
spanning trees for the stationary flow of the tilted chain, divided by the square root of a count
of spanning trees for the symmetrized flow. For the directed $3$-cycle of Proposition~\ref{prop:threecycle} part (d) gives
$\varphi^*=b/(1+2b)$ and $K_F=(1+2b)^2/(2\sqrt3\,\pi b)$ again.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} Lemma~\ref{lem:forest} applies to the tilted generator
$\widehat Q_\alpha$, whose stationary law is $\alpha$. Divided by $\prod_i\alpha_i$, the count of
oriented trees is the sum of the principal minors of order $k-1$ of $-\widehat Q_\alpha$, and
these minors do not change under the diagonal conjugation that defines $\widehat Q_\alpha$. At $\alpha^*_F$ the sum of these minors is the elementary symmetric
polynomial of degree $k-1$ in the eigenvalues of $-Q_F-\lambda_FI$, namely the product of the
numbers $\rho-\lambda_F$ over the other eigenvalues $\rho$ of $-Q_F$. (Up to the sign
$(-1)^{k-1}$ this is the derivative of the characteristic polynomial of $-Q_F$ at its root
$\lambda_F$.)

\begin{proof}
(a) By Definition~\ref{def:tilt}, $(\widehat Q_\alpha)_{ij}=q_{ij}r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}$ for
$i\ne j$, and $\alpha_i=l_{\alpha,i}r_{\alpha,i}$. So
$\alpha_i(\widehat Q_\alpha)_{ij}=f_{ij}(\alpha)$. The diagonal entry of $\widehat Q_\alpha$ is
$-\theta_i(\alpha)$. So $\widehat Q_\alpha\ones=0$ gives
$\sum_{j\ne i}f_{ij}(\alpha)=\alpha_i\theta_i(\alpha)$, and $\alpha^\top\widehat Q_\alpha=0$
gives $\sum_{j\ne i}f_{ji}(\alpha)=\alpha_i\theta_i(\alpha)$. Now apply Lemma~\ref{lem:forest}
to $G=\widehat Q_\alpha$ and $\pi=\alpha$, for which $\Sigma=\Sigma_\alpha$. Part (iii) is the
formula for $\det\Sigma_\alpha$, and Definition~\ref{def:tilt} then gives $C_F(\alpha)$.

(b) We have $-\widehat Q_\alpha=R^{-1}(\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha)-A_F)R$ with $R$
diagonal, and a conjugation by a diagonal matrix does not change principal minors. So
Lemma~\ref{lem:forest}(i) gives the first claim. At $\alpha^*_F$ we have
$\theta(\alpha^*_F)=q-\lambda_F\ones$, so
$\operatorname{diag}\theta(\alpha^*_F)-A_F=-Q_F-\lambda_FI$. For a $k\times k$ matrix $M$ the sum
of the principal minors of order $k-1$ is the elementary symmetric polynomial of degree $k-1$ in
the eigenvalues of $M$. The eigenvalues of $-Q_F-\lambda_FI$ are the numbers $\rho-\lambda_F$,
where $\rho$ runs over the eigenvalues of $-Q_F$. Exactly one of them is $0$, because the Perron
root is a simple eigenvalue. So the symmetric polynomial is the product of the other $k-1$
numbers. Finally $r_{\alpha^*_F}$ and $l_{\alpha^*_F}$ are multiples of $r_F$ and $l_F$ with
$l_{\alpha^*_F}\cdot r_{\alpha^*_F}=1$, and (a) gives the two forms of $K_F$.

(c) By Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b),
$\alpha_i(\widehat Q_\alpha)_{ij}=c_{ij}(\alpha)$, so $f(\alpha)=c(\alpha)$ is symmetric. Then
$\arb(f(\alpha))=\tree(c(\alpha))$, because every spanning tree has exactly one orientation
toward a given root. Also $\tree(2c)=2^{k-1}\tree(c)$, because a spanning tree has $k-1$ edges.
So (a) gives $\det\Sigma_\alpha=2^{k-1}(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2/\tree(c(\alpha))$.

(d) Only the pairs $(i,i+1)$ carry flow, so by (a) the flow $f_{i,i+1}(\alpha)$ out of $i$
equals the flow $f_{i-1,i}(\alpha)$ into $i$. Hence all $f_{i,i+1}(\alpha)$ are equal to one
number $\varphi$. Their product is
$\prod_il_{\alpha,i}\,a_i\,r_{\alpha,i+1}=\prod_i\alpha_ia_i$, so
$\varphi=(\prod_i\alpha_ia_i)^{1/k}$. The only spanning tree oriented toward a root $i$ with
nonzero weight is the cycle without the edge that leaves $i$. So
$\arb(f(\alpha))=\varphi^{k-1}$.
The symmetrized flow has conductance $\varphi$ on each of the $k$ edges of the undirected cycle.
For $k\ge3$ the spanning trees are the cycle without one edge, so
$\tree(f(\alpha)+f(\alpha)^\top)=k\varphi^{k-1}$. For $k=2$ the two edges of the cycle are the
same pair, the conductance is $2\varphi$, and the count is again $k\varphi^{k-1}$. Now (a) gives
$\det\Sigma_\alpha=k(\prod_i\alpha_i)^2/\varphi^{k-1}$. If $q_i=a_i$, then $Q_F$ is a generator,
so $\lambda_F=0$, $r_F=\ones$ and $l_F=\alpha^*_F$ is the stationary law. Its flow $l_{F,i}a_i$
is constant, which gives $\alpha^*_{F,i}=\varphi^*/a_i$ and $\varphi=\varphi^*$ at $\alpha^*_F$. Then
$\prod_i\alpha^*_{F,i}=(\varphi^*)^k/\prod_ia_i$, and $(\mu\cdot r_F)(l_F\cdot\ones)=\mu(F)$. Putting
this into the formulas above gives $\det\Sigma_{\alpha^*_F}$ and $K_F$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The rate through flows}\label{sec:flows}

Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c) gives the rate $I_F$ in closed form on a reversible face. The flow
$f(\alpha)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:forest} gives the rate on every irreducible face, and it
shows what a face gains when it is not reversible. A \emph{balanced flow} on $F$ is a family
$J=(J_{ij})$ of numbers $J_{ij}\ge0$, one for each ordered pair $i\ne j$ in $F$, with $J_{ij}=0$
when $q_{ij}=0$ and with $\sum_jJ_{ij}=\sum_jJ_{ji}$ at every state $i$. The flow $f(\alpha)$ is
balanced (Proposition~\ref{prop:forest}(a)). The numbers $\alpha_iq_{ij}$ are the flow of
the original chain when its law is $\alpha$, and they need not be balanced. For
$x\ge0$ and $y>0$ let
\[
\mathsf h(x\,|\,y)=x\log\frac xy-x+y,\qquad\mathsf h(0\,|\,y)=y .
\]
It is the relative entropy per unit time of a Poisson process of rate $x$ with respect to a
Poisson process of rate $y$. It is strictly convex in $x$, and it vanishes only at $x=y$. Finally let
$R_F(\alpha)$ be the right side of the formula in Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c), which is
defined on every face.

\begin{proposition}[the rate through flows]\label{prop:flows}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k\ge2$, let $\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$, and let $f=f(\alpha)$.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The flow $f$ is positive on the edges of $G_F$, and around every directed cycle of $G_F$ it has the
same product as the numbers $\alpha_iq_{ij}$. It is the only balanced flow on $F$ with these
two properties. In particular $f_{ij}f_{ji}=\alpha_i\alpha_jq_{ij}q_{ji}$ for all $i\ne j$.
\item We have
\[
I_F(\alpha)=\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_iq_i-\sum_{i\ne j}f_{ij}
=\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F)+\min_J\sum_{i\ne j,\ q_{ij}>0}\mathsf h\bigl(J_{ij}\,|\,\alpha_iq_{ij}\bigr),
\]
where the minimum is over the balanced flows on $F$, and it is attained only at $J=f$.
\item The difference $R_F(\alpha)-I_F(\alpha)$ equals $\sum_{\{i,j\}}\bigl(\sqrt{f_{ij}}-\sqrt{f_{ji}}\bigr)^2\ge0$, the sum
over the unordered pairs in $F$. There is equality if and only if $F$ is reversible, that is,
$\nu_iq_{ij}=\nu_jq_{ji}$ for all $i,j\in F$ and some $\nu\in(0,\infty)^F$.
\item Let $F=\{1,\dots,k\}$ with $k\ge3$ be a cycle with both directions, that is,
$q_{i,i+1}=p_i>0$ and $q_{i+1,i}=m_i>0$ with indices modulo $k$, and $q_{ij}=0$ for all other
$i\ne j$ in $F$. Put $g_i=\sqrt{\alpha_i\alpha_{i+1}p_im_i}$. Then
\[
I_F(\alpha)=\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_iq_i-\sum_{i=1}^k\sqrt{\kappa^2+4g_i^2},
\]
where $\kappa$ is the only real number with
\[
\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{\kappa+\sqrt{\kappa^2+4g_i^2}}2=\prod_{i=1}^k\alpha_ip_i .
\]
It is the net flow $f_{i,i+1}-f_{i+1,i}$ across every edge, and it has the sign of
$\prod_ip_i-\prod_im_i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

In words, to hold the fractions $\alpha$ the chain keeps the product of its flow around every
cycle and changes the flow until it is balanced. The rate is the drop in the total jump rate,
from $\sum_i\alpha_iq_i$ to the total flow of $f$. It is also the rate of leaving $F$ plus the
least cost of a balanced flow. A reversible face balances every pair on its own.
A face that is not reversible does better than the formula for reversible faces, because it lets a
net flow circulate. On a cycle the size $\kappa$ of this net flow solves one equation.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} The tilted chain has rates $q_{ij}r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}$, and the
factors $r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}$ cancel around a cycle. A flow with the same cycle products as
$\alpha_iq_{ij}$ must have this form for some vector in place of $r_\alpha$, and balance says
that this vector is optimal in the definition of $I_F(\alpha)$. The minimum in (b) is a tangent
line bound for $\mathsf h$, summed over the edges. Part (c) is the AM--GM inequality once more, and
(d) solves a quadratic equation on each edge.

\begin{proof}
Let $\psi=\log r_\alpha$, so that $f_{ij}=\alpha_iq_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i}$, by the definition of
$f$ and $\alpha_i=l_{\alpha,i}r_{\alpha,i}$.

(a) An edge of $G_F$ has $q_{ij}>0$, so $f_{ij}>0$ there. Around a directed cycle the
differences $\psi_j-\psi_i$ sum to $0$, so $f$ and the numbers $\alpha_iq_{ij}$ have the same
product. A pair $i,j$ with $q_{ij}q_{ji}>0$ is a cycle of length two, and if $q_{ij}q_{ji}=0$
both sides of the last claim vanish. Now let $J$ be a balanced flow with the two properties, and
put $\ell_{ij}=\log(J_{ij}/(\alpha_iq_{ij}))$ on the edges of $G_F$. A closed walk in $G_F$ is
made of directed cycles, so the sum of $\ell$ along every closed walk is $0$. Take two walks from
state $1$ to a state $i$, and follow each of them by the same walk from $i$ back to $1$, which
exists because $F$ is irreducible. Both closed walks have sum $0$, so the two walks from $1$ to
$i$ have the same sum. Call it $\chi_i$, with $\chi_1=0$. A walk to $i$ followed by an edge
from $i$ to $j$ is a walk to $j$, so $\chi_j=\chi_i+\ell_{ij}$, which says that
$J_{ij}=\alpha_iq_{ij}e^{\chi_j-\chi_i}$. In the notation of the proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}, $J_{ij}=\alpha_iG^\chi_{ij}$, and the balance of $J$ says that
$\alpha$ is the stationary law of $G^\chi$. By the criterion there, $\chi$ attains
$I_F(\alpha)$, and by Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(b) it differs from $\psi$ by a constant.
Hence $J=f$.

(b) By Proposition~\ref{prop:forest}(a), $\sum_{j\ne i}f_{ij}=\alpha_i\theta_i(\alpha)$, and
$I_F(\alpha)=\langle q-\theta(\alpha),\alpha\rangle$. This is the first formula. For the second
we use that, for $x\ge0$, $y>0$ and real $t$,
\[
\mathsf h(x\,|\,y)\ge xt-y(e^t-1),\qquad\text{with equality exactly when }x=ye^t .
\]
Indeed the difference of the two sides is $\mathsf h(x\,|\,ye^t)$. Let $J$ be a balanced flow.
We use the bound on each edge with $t=\psi_j-\psi_i$ and add. This gives
\[
\sum_{q_{ij}>0}\mathsf h\bigl(J_{ij}\,|\,\alpha_iq_{ij}\bigr)
\ge\sum_{i\ne j}J_{ij}(\psi_j-\psi_i)+\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_iq_{ij}-\sum_{i\ne j}f_{ij}.
\]
The first sum on the right is $\sum_i\psi_i\bigl(\sum_jJ_{ji}-\sum_jJ_{ij}\bigr)=0$. The second
is $\sum_i\alpha_i(q_i-q_i^{\rm out}(F))$. So the right side is
$I_F(\alpha)-\sum_i\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F)$ by the first formula. There is equality exactly when
$J_{ij}=\alpha_iq_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i}=f_{ij}$ on every edge.

(c) Since $(\sqrt x-\sqrt y)^2=x+y-2\sqrt{xy}$ and
$q_i=\sum_{j\in F\setminus\{i\}}q_{ij}+q_i^{\rm out}(F)$, we have
$R_F(\alpha)=\sum_i\alpha_iq_i-2\sum_{\{i,j\}}\sqrt{\alpha_iq_{ij}\alpha_jq_{ji}}$, and the square
root is $\sqrt{f_{ij}f_{ji}}$ by (a). With the first formula of (b),
\[
R_F(\alpha)-I_F(\alpha)=\sum_{\{i,j\}}\bigl(f_{ij}+f_{ji}-2\sqrt{f_{ij}f_{ji}}\bigr)
=\sum_{\{i,j\}}\bigl(\sqrt{f_{ij}}-\sqrt{f_{ji}}\bigr)^2 .
\]
This vanishes exactly when $f$ is symmetric. If $F$ is reversible, then $f=c(\alpha)$ is
symmetric by Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}(b). Conversely, $f_{ij}=f_{ji}$ reads
$\alpha_iq_{ij}r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}=\alpha_jq_{ji}r_{\alpha,i}/r_{\alpha,j}$, which is
$\nu_iq_{ij}=\nu_jq_{ji}$ for $\nu_i=\alpha_i/r_{\alpha,i}^2$.

(d) Only the pairs $\{i,i+1\}$ carry flow. The balance at state $i$ says that
$f_{i,i+1}-f_{i+1,i}=f_{i-1,i}-f_{i,i-1}$, so the net flow across an edge is one number $\kappa$ for
all edges. By (a), $f_{i,i+1}f_{i+1,i}=g_i^2$. So $f_{i,i+1}$ is the positive root of
$x^2-\kappa x-g_i^2=0$, namely $\frac12(\kappa+\sqrt{\kappa^2+4g_i^2})$, and
$f_{i+1,i}=\frac12(-\kappa+\sqrt{\kappa^2+4g_i^2})$. Their sum is $\sqrt{\kappa^2+4g_i^2}$, and (b) gives the
formula for $I_F(\alpha)$. As $k\ge3$, the states $1,2,\dots,k$ form a directed cycle, and (a)
gives $\prod_if_{i,i+1}=\prod_i\alpha_ip_i$, which is the equation for $\kappa$. Its left side is
continuous and strictly increasing in $\kappa$, with the limits $0$ and $\infty$, so $\kappa$ is the only
solution. At $\kappa=0$ the left side is $\prod_ig_i=\prod_i\alpha_i\sqrt{p_im_i}$, which is less than
the right side exactly when $\prod_im_i<\prod_ip_i$. This gives the sign of $\kappa$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[three special cases, and the sources]\label{rem:flows}
(1) If the pairs $\{i,j\}$ with $q_{ij}>0$ form a tree, then removing a pair cuts $F$ in two
parts, and a balanced flow has no net flow between them. So $f$ is symmetric, such a face is
reversible, and Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c) is the whole answer. (2) On a directed cycle with
rates $q_{i,i+1}=a_i$, the flow $f$ takes one value $\varphi$ on all edges, and the cycle product in
(a) gives $\varphi^k=\prod_i\alpha_ia_i$. So
$I_F(\alpha)=\sum_i\alpha_iq_i-k(\prod_i\alpha_ia_i)^{1/k}$, as in the proofs of
Propositions~\ref{prop:threecycle} and~\ref{prop:forest}(d). Part (d) joins this formula to the
reversible one. (3) By the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c), $I_F\le R_F$ on all of
$\Delta_F$, and $R_F(\alpha)=-x^\top S_Fx$ for $x=\sqrt\alpha$ (entrywise), where $S_F$ has the
diagonal of $Q_F$ and the off-diagonal entries $\sqrt{q_{ij}q_{ji}}$. The least value of $R_F$ on $\Delta_F$ is minus the largest eigenvalue of $S_F$, because $S_F$
has an eigenvector with nonnegative entries for this eigenvalue. The least value of $I_F$ is
$\lambda_F$. So the largest real eigenvalue of $Q_F$ is at least that of $S_F$, which is the
inequality of Schwenk~\cite{schwenk1986} for $Q_F$ plus a multiple of the identity. Part (c)
adds that the inequality is strict unless $F$ is reversible. Indeed, let $R_F$ be least at
$\tilde\alpha$. If $\tilde\alpha=\alpha^*_F$ then $R_F(\tilde\alpha)>I_F(\tilde\alpha)$ by (c), and otherwise
$R_F(\tilde\alpha)\ge I_F(\tilde\alpha)>\lambda_F$.

The cost $\sum\mathsf h$ is the rate function of Bertini, Faggionato and
Gabrielli~\cite{bfg2015} for the empirical measure together with the empirical flow of a chain
that is not killed. Its least value over the flows is the Donsker--Varadhan
rate (see~\cite[(1.3) to (1.7)]{kl2024}). The second formula in (b) is this statement for the chain killed on leaving $F$,
with a direct proof, and (a) names the minimizer. In fact the killing only adds a
linear term. Writing $Q_F u$ out shows that
$I_F(\alpha)=\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F)+I^0_F(\alpha)$, where $I^0_F$ is the
Donsker--Varadhan rate of the chain on $F$ with the rates $q_{ij}$, $i,j\in F$, and no
killing. So several statements of this section are killed forms of known ones. The
uniqueness of the tilt $r_\alpha$ up to scaling is~\cite[Lemmas~A.2 and~A.3]{landim2023}
(stated as~\cite[Lemmas~5.5 and~5.6]{kl2024}), the existence and uniqueness of the
minimizing flow in (b) is~\cite[Theorem~1.6]{bfg2014}, and its form
$\alpha_iq_{ij}r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}$ is~\cite[Lemma~5.7]{kl2024}. The rate of the
directed cycle in (2) is~\cite[Example~5.4]{kl2024} for three states with unit
rates, and~\cite[Proposition~5.13]{kl2024} computes the second derivative of $I^0_F$.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[a cycle with a drift]\label{ex:drift}
Let $F$ be the whole state space $\{1,\dots,k\}$ with $k\ge3$, and let $q_{i,i+1}=p$ and
$q_{i+1,i}=m$ with $p>m>0$, and no other rates. Then $q_i=p+m$, the chain is not reversible, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:flows}(d) gives
\[
I_F(\alpha)=p+m-\sum_{i=1}^k\sqrt{\kappa^2+4pm\,\alpha_i\alpha_{i+1}},\qquad
\prod_{i=1}^k\frac{\kappa+\sqrt{\kappa^2+4pm\,\alpha_i\alpha_{i+1}}}2=p^k\prod_{i=1}^k\alpha_i .
\]
At the uniform law, $\kappa=(p-m)/k$ solves the equation, and $I_F=0$, as it must be at the stationary
law. The formula for reversible faces gives $R_F=(\sqrt p-\sqrt m)^2>0$ there. For $k=3$, $p=2$,
$m=1$ and $\alpha=(0.5,0.3,0.2)$ we get $\kappa=0.3059$ and $I_F(\alpha)=0.1600$, while
$R_F(\alpha)=0.3173$. If $k$ is even and $\alpha$ alternates between two values $a$ and $b$, then
all $g_i$ are equal, $\kappa=(p-m)\sqrt{ab}$, and $I_F(\alpha)=(p+m)(1-k\sqrt{ab})$. As $m\to0$ the
equation becomes $\kappa^k=p^k\prod_i\alpha_i$, and we recover the directed cycle of
Remark~\ref{rem:flows}.
\end{example}
