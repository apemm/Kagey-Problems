\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Which states does the most likely occupation-count vector of a slowly
switching Markov chain use? Fix a generator $Q$ on $d$ states and a start law,
and take $N$ steps with transition matrix $I+(T/N)Q$. The occupation counts
$n_i=\#\{t\le N:X_t=i\}$ have support $F=\{i:n_i>0\}$, which we call a face.
Write $M_F$ for the largest probability on that exact support and
$\lambda_F$ for its principal exit rate (Definition~\ref{def:faces}).
A face is admissible if a path of positive probability has exactly that
support. Put $\Phi_\tau(F)=\tau\lambda_F+|F|-1$.

\subsection{Face selection}\label{sec:intro-faces}

\begin{introtheorem}[first-order face selection]\label{thm:A}
Fix the rates $q_{ij}$ and the law of $X_1$, and let $T=\tau\log N$ with
$\tau>0$ fixed. Every admissible face $F$ has $M_F=N^{-\Phi_\tau(F)+o(1)}$.
For large $N$ the support of every most likely composition is an admissible
face that minimizes $\Phi_\tau$ among the admissible faces. If the chain can
pass between any two states of each such minimizing face without leaving it,
then the visit fractions of every most likely composition are within
$O(1/\log N)$ of the quasi-ergodic law of its support
(Definition~\ref{def:faces}).
\end{introtheorem}

Theorem~\ref{thm:A} is Theorem~\ref{thm:first}(ii)--(iv). Staying in $F$
costs about $e^{-T\lambda_F}$, while sharing its mass among count vectors
costs about $N^{-(|F|-1)}$, up to powers of $\log N$. At $T=\tau\log N$
these costs compete. The complete graph is the most degenerate case,
since every face ties at first order. Figure~\ref{fig:complete-envelope}
shows what the next order decides.

\paragraph{What is new.}
Herv\'e and Ledoux~\cite{herveledoux2013} give quantitative density local
limits, while occupation large deviations describe exponential
costs~\cite{dv1975,gnw2024,bgl2024,landim2023}. Our question compares individual
count probabilities across supports with different lattice dimensions.
We control their global maxima, obtain relative killed-face estimates and
their discrete approximation, and compute constants that resolve
first-order ties. The complete graph makes the need for these constants
visible.

\paragraph{Scope.}
The sharp local estimates assume an irreducible face with positive start
mass and stay away from its boundary. We do not prove uniform boundary
estimates, general sharp expansions on reducible faces, or uniqueness of
crossings for arbitrary chains. The first-order support rule still allows
reducible faces.

Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} reads the first-order winners from the convex hull
of $(\lambda_F,|F|-1)$, and Section~\ref{sec:worked} gives a four-state cycle
example. A small-set mode trades the cost of leaving against the number of
count vectors sharing its mass. As $\tau$ increases the winning size can
only grow. The exact three-state example in Figure~\ref{fig:simplex} shows
this competition at finite $N$.

\subsection{Second order and the complete graph}\label{sec:intro-second}

First order fixes a crossing only up to a factor
$1+o(1)$. To locate it up to $o(1)$ we need the constant in front. Say that a
face $F$ satisfies (H) if the chain can move between any two states of $F$
without leaving $F$, and the start gives $F$ positive mass. For such faces we
prove a local limit theorem inside the face (Theorem~\ref{thm:local}), and it
gives the following result.

\begin{introtheorem}[face maxima and crossings]\label{thm:B}
Fix the rates $q_{ij}$ and the law of $X_1$.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Let $F$ satisfy \textup{(H)}, with $k=\lvert F\rvert$ states. Uniformly
for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$,
\[
 M_F=N^{-(k-1)}\,T^{(k-1)/2}K_F\,e^{-T\lambda_F}\bigl(1+O(1/T+T^2/N)\bigr),
\]
where $K_F>0$ is the explicit constant of Definition~\ref{def:tilt} (for
$k=1$ it is the start mass of the state).
\item Let $F$ and $G$ satisfy \textup{(H)}, and suppose that $F$ has
$\Delta\ge1$ more states than $G$ and an exit rate smaller by $\delta>0$. For
large $N$ the equation $M_F=M_G$ has a root $T$ in $[1,N^{1/3}]$, and every
such root satisfies
\[
 \delta T+\frac\Delta2\log T=\Delta\log N-\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}
 +O\Bigl(\frac1{\log N}\Bigr).
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{introtheorem}

Part (i) is Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax} and part (ii) is
Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}. The factor
$T^{(k-1)/2}$ appears because the visit fractions fluctuate on the scale
$T^{-1/2}$ in each of the $k-1$ free directions. Solving the equation in (ii) gives
$T=\frac\Delta\delta[\log N-\frac12\log\log N+O(1)]$. So the coefficient $\frac12$ of
$\log\log N$ inside the bracket, which appears in the crossings of the two-state
case and of the complete graph, holds for every such
pair, whatever the graph, the rates, the sizes or the start, and without
reversibility.

On the complete graph with the uniform start, $Q=J-dI$ with $J$ the all-ones
matrix, and the constants are explicit. A face of $k$ states has
$K_F=\frac1dk^{k+1/2}(4\pi)^{-(k-1)/2}$, a mass over a Gaussian volume, and the
$k^{k-2}$ spanning trees of the complete graph $K_k$ enter through it. Put
$a_k=\frac12\log(4\pi)-\frac{k+1/2}{k-1}\log k$, and write $V_N$ for the probability of a
vertex. A composition is balanced if its positive coordinates differ by at most $1$.

\begin{introtheorem}[the complete graph]\label{thm:C}
Let $Q=J-dI$ and let the start be uniform.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Let $F$ be a face of $2\le k\le d$ states. For every $N\ge k$ the equation $M_F=V_N$ has
exactly one root $T_{N,k}$ in $(0,N/(d-1))$, and the switch probability $T_{N,k}/N$ at the
root decreases strictly as $N$ grows. As $N\to\infty$,
\[
 T_{N,k}+\tfrac12\log T_{N,k}=\log N+a_k+\frac1{8T_{N,k}}+O\Bigl(\frac1{(\log N)^2}\Bigr).
\]
\item For every $N$ and every $0<T\le N/d$, the most likely compositions with a given support
are exactly the balanced ones.
\item Since $a_2>a_3>\dots>a_d$, for large $N$ and $T$ up to any fixed multiple of $\log N$ the
mode changes exactly once, from the $d$ vertices straight to the balanced compositions with
$d$ positive coordinates.
\end{enumerate}
\end{introtheorem}

Part (i) is Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-crossing}, where the term $1/(8T)$ comes from the
next term of the local law (Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm}), which we evaluate by hand
for every complete face. Part (ii) is Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-balance} and part (iii) is
Corollary~\ref{cor:simplex-modes}. On the triangle the middle composition
$(\frac N3,\frac N3,\frac N3)$ overtakes the vertices while the middles of the edges are
still below them by the factor $e^{-(a_2-a_3)}=0.83$. A start law changes the constant of
a face through its start mass, and a weak field changes it through the field averaged
over the face. Either can let a face of intermediate size win
(Example~\ref{ex:K3-start} and Corollary~\ref{cor:weakfield-phases}). Near a vertex the
scale changes. A composition with one large count $b$ and a few rare counts crosses the
vertex at switch odds $\sqrt{y/b}\,(1+o(1))$, where $y$ is the root of a product of
Laguerre polynomials (Theorem~\ref{thm:vertex-uniform-root}).

The rest of Sections~\ref{sec:secondorder} to~\ref{sec:examples} add the
following.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Ties.} When several faces tie at first order,
Theorem~\ref{thm:window} describes the window of $T$ in which they compete.
\item \emph{The constant.} For reversible faces $K_F$ involves the square root
of a weighted count of spanning trees (Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}). On a
face that is not reversible the single count is replaced by two, a count of
oriented spanning trees for the flow of the tilted chain and a count of
spanning trees for the symmetrized flow (Proposition~\ref{prop:forest}).
\item \emph{The next term and the role of} (H). Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm}
adds the next term, of relative order $1/T$, to the local law, to $M_F$ and to
the crossing equation. Without (H) the coefficient of $\log\log N$ can change.
On a directed 3-cycle it is $1$ at one crossing and $0$ at the next
(Proposition~\ref{prop:threecycle}).
\item \emph{A weak field.} On a face satisfying (H), a field of size $\xi_i/N$ per
visit to state $i$ multiplies its leading constant by
$e^{\langle\xi,\alpha_F^*\rangle}$. If every face tied at first order is irreducible,
these factors shift the tie-window lines (Proposition~\ref{prop:weakfield}).
\item \emph{Examples.} Section~\ref{sec:examples} computes all the constants on
the 4-cycle and lists the winners on other graphs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

This subsection places the results among earlier work on occupation measures
and on exact laws of transition counts. Section~\ref{sec:firstorder} introduces a rate
$I_F$ for the visit fractions inside a face. It is the Donsker--Varadhan
rate of the occupation measure of the chain killed on leaving $F$. Dupuis
and Liu give this rate explicitly for reversible jump
processes~\cite[Section~3.1]{dupuisliu2015}, and on a finite chain their
formula agrees with Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c). On the complete graph it is
$d-(\sum_i\sqrt{\alpha_i})^2$~\cite[Eq.~(3.2)]{dupuisliu2015}. Chen and
Deng~\cite{chendeng2013} study large deviations for absorbing Markov chains
and use them to derive a variational representation of the principal
Dirichlet eigenvalue. Guillin,
Nectoux and
Wu~\cite{gnw2024} prove a large deviation principle for the occupation
measure of a process conditioned to stay in a set. For reversible processes
they show that the exit rate of the set is the least value of the
Donsker--Varadhan rate over laws on the set (their Cor.~2). In general they
prove an inequality between their rate, the Donsker--Varadhan rate and the
exit rate, and they conjecture equality (their (2.15)). For a finite chain
with $Q_F$ irreducible, the fact that $\lambda_F$ is the least value of $I_F$
is the variational formula of Donsker and Varadhan~\cite{dv1975}, which needs
no reversibility. Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(a) gives a short proof in our
notation. This does not address the conjecture of Guillin, Nectoux and Wu,
which concerns their conditional rate. The quasi-ergodic law goes back to
Darroch and Seneta~\cite{darrochseneta1965,darrochseneta1967}, the book of
Collet, Mart\'inez and San Mart\'in~\cite{cmsm2013} is the standard reference
for quasi-stationary and quasi-ergodic laws, and Colonius and
Rasmussen~\cite{coloniusrasmussen2021} treat the reducible case. Dietz and
Sethuraman~\cite{dietz2007} study chains that switch from $i$ to $j$ at
time $n$ with probability $G(i,j)/n$, where every $G(i,j)>0$. Their chains
also make about $\log n$ switches, but spread log-uniformly in time, and their
occupation fractions have a limit law with full support on the simplex.
In~\cite{dietz2005} they prove large deviations for chains whose transition
matrices converge to a reducible matrix. Bouguet and Cloez~\cite{bouguetcloez2018}
study empirical-measure limits and fluctuations for freezing finite-state chains,
including the regime with switch probabilities of order $1/n$. In these models the
rate changes along the trajectory. Here it is fixed during each path of length $N$,
and changes with $N$ instead.

Exact laws of transition counts go back to Whittle~\cite{whittle1955},
Dawson and Good~\cite{dawsongood1957} and Goodman~\cite{goodman1958}.
Polettini~\cite{polettini2015} and Carugno, Vivo and Coghi~\cite{cvc2022}
derive them from the BEST theorem and the matrix-tree theorem. The expression of
Carugno, Vivo and Coghi for the moment generating function is exact at every
time, and they use it for large deviations, where every count grows linearly.
For two states the composition has the Markov binomial law, and Dekking and
Kong~\cite{dekkingkong2011} find when it has one, two or three modes.
Edwards~\cite{edwards1960} introduced the Markov binomial distribution, for a
stationary chain on two outcomes that moves from $i$ to $j$ with probability
$\theta\delta_{ij}+(1-\theta)\nu_j$, where $\nu$ is its law at each trial, and
he wrote the generating function of the number of successes as a product of
matrices. Wang and Yang extended this chain to finitely many states, and we
follow the account of their work in Johnson, Kotz and
Balakrishnan~\cite[Chapter~35, Section~13.2]{jkb1997}. Their chain starts from
$\nu$ and moves from $i$ to $j$ with probability
$\theta\delta_{ij}+(1-\theta)\nu_j$, and the generating function of its vector
of category counts is a product of matrices. The complete graph with the
uniform start and a stay probability $p\ge1/d$ is the case where $\nu$ is
uniform and $\theta=p-T/N$. So its exact law is a special case of this theory,
and we do not claim it as new. A start law that is not uniform keeps the uniform
switching, so it gives a Wang--Yang chain only when it is uniform.
Hsiau~\cite[Theorem~1]{hsiau1997} proves compound Poisson limits for sums over
multistate chains with one nearly absorbing state, and Wang and
Tang~\cite{wangtang2003} prove Poisson-type limits for additive processes on
Markov chains. Kassan-Ogly and Filippov~\cite{kassanogly2003} solve the Potts
chain in a field in the thermodynamic limit. Their paper was not accessible to
us, but its abstract and introduction are free online, and we use nothing
beyond these. Stadje~\cite[Theorems~1 and~2]{stadje1999} gives the generating functions of the
joint numbers of visits of a finite chain, and limit laws when one state becomes
nearly absorbing and the chain leaves it with probability of order
$1/N$~\cite[Theorems~3 and~4]{stadje1999}. The other rows of his transition
matrix stay fixed, so the counts of the other states stay bounded. We group the words by runs instead
(Lemma~\ref{lem:runs}), which suits a regime with $O(\log N)$ switches.
In continuous time, Brydges, van der Hofstad and K\"onig~\cite{bhk2007} give
the joint density of the local times of a chain on the event that its range
is a given set, and Huang, Kious, Sidoravicius and Tarr\`es~\cite{hkst2018}
write it with Bessel functions and spanning trees for symmetric rates. Brydges, van der Hofstad
and K\"onig use their formula for large deviation upper bounds.
Theorem~\ref{thm:local} gives the leading term of the density, with its
constant, in our regime. For a fixed geometrically ergodic chain and a bounded
real functional, Kontoyiannis and Meyn~\cite[Theorem~6.3]{km2003} give exact
large deviation asymptotics of Bahadur--Rao type. Theorem~\ref{thm:local} is a
local form for the whole vector of counts, for a chain that is killed on
leaving $F$ and whose switch probabilities shrink with $N$, uniformly in $T$ and
with an explicit constant.
Herv\'e and Ledoux~\cite{herveledoux2013} prove a quantitative local limit theorem
for densities of finite-state Markov additive processes. Their application to local
times assumes irreducibility of the generator and of its one-state-deleted
subgenerators. Our continuum estimates are related to this theory. We need relative
estimates on killed faces, their discrete approximation, and a comparison of maxima
across supports.
Bertini, Gabrielli and Landim~\cite{bgl2024} and Landim~\cite{landim2023}
expand the level-two rate functions of finite chains whose rates depend on a
parameter. They describe the time scales of such chains but do not ask where
the mode of the composition sits.
Kim and Landim~\cite{kl2024} extend this to the joint rate function of
the empirical measure and the flow~\cite{bfg2015}, and they give conditions under
which the rate function of the occupation times determines the chain.
Remark~\ref{rem:flows} says how the rate $I_F$ of a killed chain relates to their
work and to that of Bertini, Faggionato and Gabrielli~\cite{bfg2014,bfg2015}.


The question grew out of Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem
Collection~\cite{kagey131}, a two-direction crossing problem. The present
paper incorporates the symmetric-model preprint~\cite{paperB}.

\subsection{Plan of the paper}\label{sec:plan}

Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the notation and gives the exact law of the
composition as a sum over runs. Section~\ref{sec:firstorder} works out four
states on a cycle and then proves first-order face selection
(Theorems~\ref{thm:first} and~\ref{thm:hull}). Section~\ref{sec:secondorder}
proves the local limit theorem inside a face, the face maxima, the crossing
equation and the tie windows. It then gives a tree formula for the constant,
shows on a directed 3-cycle what can change without (H), adds the next term and
treats a weak field. Section~\ref{sec:complete} recovers the two-state walk and
treats the complete graph, with a start law and a weak field.
Section~\ref{sec:vertex} treats compositions near a vertex of the complete graph.
Section~\ref{sec:examples} computes the 4-cycle with all its constants and lists
the winners on other graphs, and Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the computer
checks and the open questions. Appendix~\ref{app:torus} proves the local law, the
uniform upper bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:upper}, the first-order bounds for
reducible faces and the forest formula of Lemma~\ref{lem:forest}.
Appendix~\ref{app:laplace} proves the leading Laplace lemma and states its next term,
with the coefficient formula. Appendices~\ref{app:complete}
and~\ref{app:vertex} hold the proofs for the complete graph and near a vertex. The
supplement~\cite{supplement} has the proofs for the table of outcomes and the full
proof for the directed 3-cycle, followed by the higher-order Laplace proof and the
detailed proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm}.

We quote a few results of~\cite{paperA} and~\cite{paperB}. From~\cite{paperA}
we use the run count, the fact that the polynomials $S_{a,b}$ of
Section~\ref{sec:notation} grow toward the middle, the uniform Bessel bound
(recalled in~\eqref{eq:planar-bessel}), the clipped products of its Lemma~3.2 and
the expansion of $z_N$. From~\cite{paperB} we quote, without proof, only results that
we do not use, namely its Bessel majorant and its crossover near the boundary of the
simplex (Remark~\ref{rem:B-boundary}).
