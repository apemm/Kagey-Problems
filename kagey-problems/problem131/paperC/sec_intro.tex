\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

A ball falls through a Galton board with $N$ rows of pegs. At the first peg it
goes left or right with probability $\frac12$. At every later peg it repeats
its previous direction with probability $p$ and switches with probability
$q=1-p$. Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131} asks
for the law of the bin where the ball lands, and in particular when the middle
bin is exactly as likely as an endpoint. An endpoint is reached by a single
path, the one that never switches, so it has probability $\frac12p^{N-1}$. When
$p$ is close to $1$ this path wins, and when $p$ is smaller the middle wins.
Paper~A~\cite{paperA} answers the question at every $N$. The two are equally
likely when the expected number of switches $T=Nq$ is close to $\log N$. More
precisely, the crossing value $z_N$ of $T$ satisfies
$z_N+\frac12\log z_N=\log N+\frac12\log\frac\pi8+o(1)$.

Kagey also asks what changes on other boards. This paper answers his main
question, when the middle is as likely as the ends, for boards on which the
ball can move in more than two directions. A guiding example is Kagey's board in
two dimensions. A ball moves on the square lattice $\Z^2$, one step east,
north, west or south at a time. It starts in a uniform direction, and at each
step it keeps its direction, turns left or right, or reverses. Turns and
reversals are rare. Fix $T>0$. A turn to each side has probability $rT/N$ and a reversal
has probability $sT/N$, where $r,s\ge0$ are fixed rates with $2r+s>0$. After
$N$ steps, is the ball more likely to be at the corner $(N,0)$, which only the
path that never switches can reach, or back at the origin? Paper~E~\cite{paperE} shows
that for every even $N\ge4$ there is exactly one value $T^*_N$ of $T$ at which the two
are equally likely. The corner is more likely
below it and the origin above it, and as $N\to\infty$ through even integers
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-limit}
 \frac{T^*_N}{\log N}\to\min\Bigl(\frac2{2r+s},\frac1s\Bigr).
\end{equation}
The two values in
the minimum belong to two kinds of paths to the origin, and the general rule of this paper
explains them.

\subsection{Face selection}\label{sec:intro-faces}

Let the direction of the ball be a Markov chain on $d$ states that switches
from state $i$ to state $j$ with probability $q_{ij}T/N$ at each step, and
count the visits to each state. This count vector, the \emph{composition},
plays the role of the bin. Its support is a set $F$ of states, which we call a
\emph{face} (it indexes a face of the simplex of visit fractions). Which face
carries the most likely composition, and at which $T$ does the mode move to
another face? On Kagey's board there are two states. The two faces of one
state are the endpoints, and the face of two states is the set of interior
bins.

Put $T=\tau\log N$ and let $\lambda_F$ be the rate at which the chain leaves
$F$, that is, the principal Dirichlet eigenvalue on $F$ of $-Q$, where $Q$ is
the matrix of the rates $q_{ij}$. How likely the best composition with
support $F$ is depends on two effects.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The cost of staying.} The chain must stay inside $F$ for all $N$
steps. If the start gives $F$ positive mass, this has probability about
$e^{-T\lambda_F}=N^{-\tau\lambda_F}$. A
larger face is harder to leave, so the chain stays on it more easily.
\item \emph{The cost of spreading.} The mass of $F$ is shared among the
compositions with support $F$, and there are about $N^{\lvert F\rvert-1}$ of
them, one factor $N$ for each free coordinate. Up to powers of $\log N$, the
best single composition gets a share $N^{-(\lvert F\rvert-1)}$.
\end{itemize}
Call a face admissible if some path of positive probability has exactly this support. For an
admissible face $F$ the most likely composition with support $F$ has probability
$N^{-\Phi_\tau(F)+o(1)}$, where
\[
 \Phi_\tau(F)=\tau\lambda_F+\lvert F\rvert-1,
\]
and for large $N$ every mode sits on an admissible face that minimizes $\Phi_\tau$ among the
admissible faces (Theorem~\ref{thm:first}). In words, a sticky walk prefers a well-separated
community, a set of states that is hard to leave for its size. As $\tau$ grows
staying counts for more, and the mode moves to larger faces. On Kagey's board
an endpoint has $\Phi_\tau=\tau$ and the interior has $\Phi_\tau=1$, so the
middle takes over at $\tau=1$, which is the first-order form of the answer of
Paper~A. Section~\ref{sec:worked} works out four states on a cycle by hand,
and Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} reads the winners off the convex hull of the points
$(\lambda_F,\lvert F\rvert-1)$.

The planar walk fits this picture. The corner is the face of the single
direction east, with exit rate $2r+s$, so it has probability about
$N^{-(2r+s)\tau}$. A path to the origin either stays on one axis, a face of
two states with exit rate $2r$, or uses all four directions, a face with exit
rate $0$. On an axis the origin is a single composition, of probability about
$N^{-(2r\tau+1)}$. With all four directions a single composition has
probability about $N^{-3}$, but about $N/2$ of them end at the origin, which
gives $N^{-2}$. This suggests that the origin beats the corner when
$(2r+s)\tau>\min(2r\tau+1,2)$, which is~\eqref{eq:intro-limit}. It is only a
heuristic, since the origin is not a single composition, and
Paper~E proves the limit directly.

\emph{Second order.} First order fixes a crossing only up to a factor
$1+o(1)$. To locate it up to $o(1)$ we need the constant in front. Say that a
face $F$ satisfies (H) if the chain can move between any two states of $F$
without leaving $F$, and the start gives $F$ positive mass. For such faces we
prove a local limit theorem inside the face (Theorem~\ref{thm:local}). It
shows (Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax}) that the best composition with support $F$,
with $k=\lvert F\rvert$ states, has probability
\[
 M_F=N^{-(k-1)}\,T^{(k-1)/2}K_F\,e^{-T\lambda_F}\bigl(1+O(1/T+T^2/N)\bigr)
\]
uniformly for $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$, with an explicit constant $K_F$. The factor
$T^{(k-1)/2}$ appears because the visit fractions fluctuate on the scale
$T^{-1/2}$ in each of the $k-1$ free directions. Now let $F$ and $G$ satisfy
(H), and suppose that $F$ has $\Delta\ge1$ more states than $G$ and an exit
rate smaller by $\delta>0$. Then for large $N$ every $T\in[1,N^{1/3}]$ with
$M_F=M_G$ satisfies
\[
 \delta T+\frac\Delta2\log T=\Delta\log N-\log\frac{K_F}{K_G}
 +O\Bigl(\frac1{\log N}\Bigr)
\]
(Theorem~\ref{thm:crossing}). Solving this gives
$T=\frac\Delta\delta[\log N-\frac12\log\log N+O(1)]$. So the coefficient $\frac12$ of
$\log\log N$ inside the bracket, which Papers~A and~B found, holds for every such pair, whatever
the graph, the rates, the sizes or the start, and without reversibility. When several faces tie at
first order, Theorem~\ref{thm:window} describes the window of $T$ in which
they compete. For reversible faces $K_F$ involves the square root of a
weighted count of spanning trees (Proposition~\ref{prop:constant}), and the
constants of Papers~A and~B are short corollaries
(Corollaries~\ref{cor:paperA} and~\ref{cor:paperB}). On a face that is not
reversible the single count is replaced by two, a count of oriented spanning
trees for the flow of the tilted chain and a count of spanning trees for the
symmetrized flow (Proposition~\ref{prop:forest}). The rate inside such a face is the drop in
the total jump rate when the chain is tilted. It is also the rate of leaving the face plus the
least value, over balanced flows, of the rate of Bertini, Faggionato and
Gabrielli~\cite{bfg2015}, and on a cycle it comes from one equation for the net flow
(Proposition~\ref{prop:flows}).
Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm} adds the next term, of relative order $1/T$,
to the local law, to $M_F$ and to the crossing equation. Without (H) the
coefficient can change. On a directed 3-cycle it is $1$ at one crossing and
$0$ at the next (Proposition~\ref{prop:threecycle}).
Section~\ref{sec:examples} computes all the constants on the 4-cycle, shows on
the complete graph $K_3$ that the start can decide which face wins, and lists
the winners on other graphs.

The paper closes with an application, and nothing before it depends on it.
Section~\ref{sec:sticky} applies face selection to sticky priors for hidden
Markov models. There the exit rate of a set of hidden states is one minus the
Perron root of the kernel of the switches restricted to the set, and as the
stickiness decreases the most likely count vector under the prior moves from
one state to larger sets (Corollary~\ref{cor:map} and
Proposition~\ref{prop:thresholds}). Among symmetric kernels only the uniform
one jumps from one state straight to all of them
(Corollary~\ref{cor:uniform-kernel}). Section~\ref{sec:plan} says what each
section does.

\subsection{The other papers of the series}\label{sec:series}

This paper is one of six on Problem~131. We call them Paper~A~\cite{paperA},
Paper~B~\cite{paperB}, Paper~C (this paper), Paper~D~\cite{paperD}, Paper~E~\cite{paperE} and
Paper~F~\cite{paperF}. They
share the following notation.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=3pt]
\item Kagey's board has $N$ steps (his row $n$ has $N=n-1$). The first step is
left or right with probability $\frac12$, and each later step repeats the
previous one with probability $p$. We write $q=1-p$ and $u=q/p$, the odds of a
switch.
\item $P_N(k)$ is the probability of $k$ right steps, that is, that the ball
lands in bin $k$. The endpoints have $P_N(0)=P_N(N)=p^{N-1}/2$.
\item $L=\log N$, and $T=Nq$ is the expected number of switches (up to the
first step).
\item The crossing is the value of $p$ (or $T$) at which a bin is exactly as
likely as an endpoint. For the middle bin it is $p_N$, and $z_N=N(1-p_N)$.
\item With $d$ states, a face $F$ is a nonempty set of states, and the vertices
are the faces of size $1$. The exit rate $\lambda_F$ is the principal Dirichlet
eigenvalue of $-Q$ on $F$, and $K_F$ is the second-order constant.
\end{itemize}
Paper~A solves Kagey's board. Its uniform Bessel bounds bracket $z_N$ for every
$N\ge2$ and give $z_N+\frac12\log z_N=L+c_0+1/(8z_N)+R_N$ with
$c_0=\frac12\log(\pi/8)$ and an explicit $R_N$. Paper~B lets the ball switch to
each of $d-1$ other directions at the same rate. There every face ties at first
order, and constants $a_k$ break the tie at second order. Papers~D and~F ask what
changes when the ball remembers more than its last step (the elephant walk in Paper~D, runs
with heavy tails and aging walks in Paper~F). The present paper
treats every rate matrix $Q$. It explains which face carries the mode and why,
computes the constants $K_F$, and recovers $c_0$ and $a_k$ as special cases. Paper~E treats the
planar walk. Its first-order answer is the heuristic above, and its proofs use the present paper
only through the exact identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:pair}.

We quote a few results from the earlier papers. From Paper~A we use the run count, the
fact that the bin polynomials grow toward the middle, the uniform Bessel bound
(recalled in~\eqref{eq:planar-bessel}) and the expansion of $z_N$. From
Paper~B we use only its expansion of the balanced bins, for one constant in
Proposition~\ref{prop:nextterm}(iv). Figure~\ref{fig:program} compares the
crossings of the series.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

This subsection places the results among earlier work on occupation measures, on exact laws of
transition counts and on correlated random walks. Section~\ref{sec:firstorder} introduces a rate
$I_F$ for the visit fractions inside a face. It is the Donsker--Varadhan
rate of the occupation measure of the chain killed on leaving $F$. Dupuis
and Liu give this rate explicitly for reversible jump
processes~\cite[Eq.~(3.2)]{dupuisliu2015}, and on a finite chain their
formula agrees with Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(c). Chen and
Deng~\cite{chendeng2013} study large deviations for absorbing Markov chains,
and according to their abstract they obtain a variational representation of
the Dirichlet principal eigenvalue from the large deviation rate. Guillin,
Nectoux and
Wu~\cite{gnw2024} prove a large deviation principle for the occupation
measure of a process conditioned to stay in a set. For reversible processes
they show that the exit rate of the set is the least value of the
Donsker--Varadhan rate over laws on the set (their Cor.~2). In general they
prove an inequality between their rate, the Donsker--Varadhan rate and the
exit rate, and they conjecture equality (their (2.15)). For a finite chain
with $Q_F$ irreducible, the fact that $\lambda_F$ is the least value of $I_F$
is the variational formula of Donsker and Varadhan~\cite{dv1975}, which needs
no reversibility. Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(a) gives a short proof in our
notation. This does not address the conjecture of Guillin, Nectoux and Wu,
which concerns their conditional rate. The quasi-ergodic law goes back to
Darroch and Seneta~\cite{darrochseneta1965,darrochseneta1967}, the book of
Collet, Mart\'inez and San Mart\'in~\cite{cmsm2013} is the standard reference
for quasi-stationary and quasi-ergodic laws, and Colonius and
Rasmussen~\cite{coloniusrasmussen2021} treat the reducible case. Dietz and
Sethuraman~\cite{dietz2007} study chains that switch from $i$ to $j$ at
time $n$ with probability $G(i,j)/n$, where every $G(i,j)>0$. Their chains
also make about $\log n$ switches, but spread log-uniformly in time, and their
occupation fractions have a limit law with full support on the simplex.
In~\cite{dietz2005} they prove large deviations for chains whose transition
matrices converge to a reducible matrix.

Exact laws of transition counts go back to Whittle~\cite{whittle1955},
Dawson and Good~\cite{dawsongood1957} and Goodman~\cite{goodman1958}.
Polettini~\cite{polettini2015} and Carugno, Vivo and Coghi~\cite{cvc2022}
derive them from the BEST theorem and the matrix-tree theorem when every
count grows linearly. We group the words by runs instead
(Lemma~\ref{lem:runs}), which suits a regime with $O(\log N)$ switches.
In continuous time, Brydges, van der Hofstad and K\"onig~\cite{bhk2007} give
the joint density of the local times of a chain on the event that its range
is a given set, and Huang, Kious, Sidoravicius and Tarr\`es~\cite{hkst2018}
write it with Bessel functions and spanning trees. Brydges, van der Hofstad
and K\"onig remark that their formula is hard to evaluate asymptotically, and
they prove upper bounds. Theorem~\ref{thm:local} gives the leading term with its
constant in our regime.
Bertini, Gabrielli and Landim~\cite{bgl2024} and Landim~\cite{landim2023}
expand the level-two rate functions of finite chains whose rates depend on a
parameter. They describe the time scales of such chains but do not ask where
the mode of the composition sits.
Kim and Landim~\cite{kl2024} extend this to the joint rate function of
the empirical measure and the flow~\cite{bfg2015}, and they give conditions under
which the rate function of the occupation times determines the chain.
Proposition~\ref{prop:flows} is the form of the rate of~\cite{bfg2015} for a killed chain. The sticky HDP-HMM of Fox, Sudderth, Jordan
and Willsky~\cite{fox2011} adds a self-transition bias to the hierarchical
Dirichlet process hidden Markov model.

We did
not find the face-selection question in the literature. Paper~E discusses the work on
correlated random walks in the plane.

\subsection{Plan of the paper}\label{sec:plan}

Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the notation and gives the exact law of the
composition as a sum over runs. Section~\ref{sec:firstorder} works out four
states on a cycle by hand and then proves first-order face selection
(Theorems~\ref{thm:first} and~\ref{thm:hull}). Section~\ref{sec:secondorder}
proves the local limit theorem inside a face, the face maxima, the crossing
equation and the tie windows. It then recovers the constants of Papers~A
and~B, shows on a directed 3-cycle what can change without (H), adds the
next term (Section~\ref{sec:nextterm}), gives a tree formula for the constant on every
irreducible face (Section~\ref{sec:forest}), and writes the rate through flows
(Section~\ref{sec:flows}). Section~\ref{sec:examples} computes the 4-cycle with all its
constants and lists the winners on other graphs. Section~\ref{sec:sticky}
applies face selection to sticky priors for hidden Markov models, and
Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the computer checks and the open questions.
Appendix~\ref{app:torus} proves the local law, the uniform upper bound of
Lemma~\ref{lem:upper}, the first-order bounds for reducible faces and the forest formula of
Lemma~\ref{lem:forest}.
Appendix~\ref{app:laplace} proves the Laplace lemma that
Appendix~\ref{app:torus} uses, and the next term. Appendix~\ref{app:examples} has the proofs for the table of
outcomes, and Appendix~\ref{app:hdp} the limit of large concentration for the
direct jump under the sticky prior.
