\begin{abstract}
A ball bounces down a Galton board with $N$ rows, and each bounce repeats the
previous one with probability $p$. Kagey asked when the middle bin becomes as
likely as an end bin, which only the ball that never switches can reach. We
ask the same question for a ball with any finite number of directions. Its
direction is a Markov chain that rarely switches, and its bin records how many
steps it made in each direction. The most likely bin uses only some set of
directions, and we find which set. Two effects compete. The ball must never
switch to a direction outside the set, which is likely when the set is hard to
leave. But a larger set has more bins to share its probability, and each
single bin loses a factor $N$ for each extra direction. When the expected number
of switches is a fixed multiple of $\log N$, both effects are powers of $N$,
and the winning set minimizes the sum of the two exponents. A local limit
theorem inside a set then gives the constant in front, and with it the number
of switches at which two sets are equally likely, up to an error that tends to
zero, together with the next term. This needs that the ball can pass between
any two directions of a set without leaving it, and a ball that cycles through
three directions shows that the answer changes otherwise. Our main application
is Kagey's board in the plane, a ball on the square lattice that keeps its
direction, turns left or right, or reverses. Its far corner and the origin are
equally likely at exactly one switching rate, which we find to first order.
When the ball reverses more often than it turns, this crossing agrees with the
one on Kagey's board with $N/2$ rows, up to a negative power of $N$. When it
turns more often, the crossing comes from a local limit theorem in two
dimensions. On one curve of rates the planar walk is exactly two independent
copies of Kagey's board, and with turns only its crossing is Kagey's up to a
small error. We also apply the rule to sticky priors for hidden Markov models.
\end{abstract}
