\TITLE{Face selection for slowly switching Markov chains}
\SHORTTITLE{Face selection for slowly switching Markov chains}
\AUTHORS{Arjun Pemmasani\footnote{Department of Mathematics, Harvey Mudd College,
Claremont, CA 91711, USA. \EMAIL{apemmasani@hmc.edu}}}
\KEYWORDS{occupation times of Markov chains; mode; principal Dirichlet eigenvalue;
quasi-stationary distribution; local limit theorem; persistent random walk; correlated
random walk; Potts chain; spanning trees}
\AMSSUBJ{60J10; 60J27; 60F05; 60F10; 60G50; 82B20; 05C05}
\ABSTRACT{We consider a Markov chain on a finite state space that rarely switches
states, and its occupation counts, the numbers of steps that it spends in each
state up to time $N$. The most likely vector of counts uses only some set of
states, and we find which set. Two effects compete. The chain must never
switch to a state outside the set, and this costs less when the set is hard
to leave. But a larger set has more count vectors to share its probability, and
each single count vector loses a factor $N$ for each extra state. Let the
switch probabilities be $T/N$ times fixed rates. When $T$ is a fixed multiple
of $\log N$, both effects are powers of $N$, and the winning set minimizes the
sum of the two exponents. When the chain can pass between any two states of a
set without leaving it, a local limit theorem inside the set gives the
constant in front. With it we locate the value of $T$ at which two such sets
are equally likely, up to an error that tends to zero, and we find the next
term. A chain that cycles through three states shows that the answer can
change without this condition. On the complete graph, which is the finite Potts
chain, every set ties at first order, and explicit constants rank the sets at second
order, so the most likely vector jumps once, from one state to all states, while a
start law or a weak field can let a set of intermediate size win, and a suitable
field lets every size win in turn. Near a single state, the value of $T$ at which a
vector with a few rare counts is as likely as that state is given by the root of a
product of Laguerre polynomials, with an explicit error.}

\section*{A note on the use of AI}
Most results of Sections~\ref{sec:complete} and~\ref{sec:vertex} come from
the preprint~\cite{paperB}. For that preprint I brainstormed conjectures with Claude Opus 5.5
(Anthropic, through Claude Code), and several results came from it together with proofs. Of these,
this paper contains the tree form of the constants
(Example~\ref{ex:complete-face} and the constant $D(\alpha)$ in~\eqref{eq:interior}), the global
modes with a start (Corollary~\ref{cor:start-modes}) and the example in which the start decides
which face wins (Example~\ref{ex:K3-start}). I rechecked and rederived these proofs by hand. I
also checked the proofs with GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI), which found nothing beyond the earlier check.
The Lean~4 files in \nolinkurl{kagey-problems/problem131/lean/PaperC} and
\nolinkurl{kagey-problems/problem131/lean/PaperB} were generated by the model, and those in
\nolinkurl{lean/PaperB} were then revised and checked by hand against the corresponding results
(see \nolinkurl{MAP.md} in each folder). Parts of the Python verification scripts of this paper and
of~\cite{paperB} were also written by the model, which cross-checked them against each other.
I also used it for literature search, namely to find and verify sources beyond the ones I had
first checked on my own. Beyond this, I used the model only to revise the text and to point out
typos and spelling mistakes.

All mathematical claims, proofs and computations in this paper are my responsibility.
