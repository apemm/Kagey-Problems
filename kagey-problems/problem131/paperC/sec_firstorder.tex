\section{First-order face selection}\label{sec:firstorder}

\subsection{A worked example with four states on a cycle}\label{sec:worked}

Before the general theorem we work out one example by hand. Take $d=4$ states
on a cycle, with rate $1$ between neighbors (indices modulo $4$), and the
uniform start. So at each step the chain moves to each of its two neighbors with
probability $h=T/N$ and stays with probability $1-2h$. This is the direction of
the planar walk of Section~\ref{sec:planar} when it turns but never reverses
(Example~\ref{ex:models}(iv) with $r=1$, $s=0$). Up to rotation and reflection
there are four kinds of faces, namely a vertex, an adjacent pair, an arc of
three states and the whole cycle. (A face such as $\{1,3\}$ cannot be the
support of a path, since the chain cannot pass from $1$ to $3$ without visiting
$2$ or $4$.) Which kind carries the most likely composition?

For each kind we ask two questions. With what probability does the chain stay
in the face for all $N$ steps? Among how many compositions is this probability
shared? The best composition of the face gets about the first answer divided by
the second. In this subsection $T$ is large and $T^2/N$ is small, the sign
$\approx$ means equality up to a bounded factor, and we do not give proofs.
The proofs are in Theorems~\ref{thm:first} and~\ref{thm:facemax}, and
Corollary~\ref{cor:C4} has the constants.

\emph{A vertex}, say $\{1\}$. The chain stays at state $1$ only if it never
switches, which has probability $(1-2h)^{N-1}\approx e^{-2T}$. So the exit rate
is $\lambda_{\{1\}}=2$. The only composition is $(N,0,0,0)$, so nothing is
shared, and $M_{\{1\}}=\frac14(1-2h)^{N-1}\approx e^{-2T}$.

\emph{An adjacent pair}, say $\{1,2\}$. The chain leaves the pair by the
switches $1\to4$ and $2\to3$, while the switches between $1$ and $2$ keep it
inside. So at each step it leaves with probability $h$, whichever of the two
states it is in, and it stays in the pair with probability
$(1-h)^{N-1}\approx e^{-T}$. The exit rate is $\lambda_{\{1,2\}}=1$, half that
of a vertex. Given that the chain stays, it switches between $1$ and $2$ about
$T$ times, so it makes about $T$ runs of length about $N/T$ each. The number
$a$ of visits to state $1$ is the total length of half of these runs. So $a$
is close to $N/2$, with fluctuations of order $\sqrt T\cdot N/T=N/\sqrt T$.
Hence the mass $e^{-T}$ is shared by about $N/\sqrt T$ compositions
$(a,N-a,0,0)$, and the best one gets
\[
 M_{\{1,2\}}\approx\frac{e^{-T}}{N/\sqrt T}=N^{-1}\,T^{1/2}e^{-T}.
\]
For the pair we can check this against an exact formula. A path with support
$\{1,2\}$ is a word in the letters $1$ and $2$. Each stay has probability
$1-2h$ and each switch probability $h$, so a word with $j$ switches has
probability $\frac14(1-2h)^{N-1}u^j$ with $u=h/(1-2h)$. Summing over the words
with $a$ letters $1$ gives $P_N(a,N-a,0,0)=M_{\{1\}}S_{a,N-a}(u)$, the bin
polynomial of Kagey's board (Section~\ref{sec:notation}). It is largest for
the balanced compositions, and the uniform Bessel bound of Paper~A, recalled
in~\eqref{eq:planar-bessel} below, shows that $S_N(u)$ is
$\frac2N\sqrt{2T/\pi}\,e^{T}$ times a factor that tends to $1$. So
$M_{\{1,2\}}$ is $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}N^{-1}T^{1/2}e^{-T}$ times such a factor.

\emph{An arc of three states}, say $\{1,2,3\}$. Only the end states $1$ and $3$
can leave (both to state $4$), each at rate $1$, and the middle state $2$
cannot. So the chain stays longer if it prefers the middle state, and we need
an eigenvalue to find the exit rate. The chain killed on leaving the arc has the
matrix $I+hQ_F$ with $-Q_F=2I-A$, where $A$ is the adjacency matrix of the path
$1-2-3$. Its largest eigenvalue is $1-(2-\sqrt2)h$, with eigenvector
$(1,\sqrt2,1)$. So the chain stays in the arc with probability about
$e^{-(2-\sqrt2)T}$, and $\lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}=2-\sqrt2=0.586$. The visit
fractions now fluctuate in two directions, so the mass is shared by about
$(N/\sqrt T)^2$ compositions, and $M_{\{1,2,3\}}\approx
N^{-2}\,T\,e^{-(2-\sqrt2)T}$.

\emph{The whole cycle.} The chain never has to leave, so $\lambda_{[4]}=0$ and
the mass is $1$. It is shared by about $(N/\sqrt T)^3$ compositions near
$(\frac N4,\frac N4,\frac N4,\frac N4)$. So $M_{[4]}\approx N^{-3}T^{3/2}$.

\emph{Comparison.} The table above collects the four cases, up to bounded
factors. Now put $T=\tau\log N$. Then $e^{-\lambda T}=N^{-\tau\lambda}$, while
the powers of $T$ are only powers of $\log N$. So, up to such powers,
$M_F\approx N^{-\Phi_\tau(F)}$ with the exponent
$\Phi_\tau(F)=\tau\lambda_F+\lvert F\rvert-1$ in the last column. The mode sits
on the face with the smallest exponent. Figure~\ref{fig:C4worked}(a) plots the
four exponents against $\tau$. The vertices win for $\tau<1$, the adjacent
pairs for $1<\tau<2$ and the whole cycle for $\tau>2$. The arcs of three
states never win. They beat the pairs only for $\tau>1+\sqrt2$, and there the
whole cycle beats them, since that needs only $(2-\sqrt2)\tau>1$. Note also
that the whole cycle carries almost all the mass for every $\tau>0$. It
carries the mode only for $\tau>2$, because it spreads its mass over about
$N^3$ compositions.

\emph{The general rule.} For any face $F$ the same two effects give
$M_F\approx N^{-(\lvert F\rvert-1)}e^{-T\lambda_F}=N^{-\Phi_\tau(F)}$ up to
powers of $\log N$, as announced in Section~\ref{sec:intro-faces}. There is a
second way to see the factor $N^{-(\lvert F\rvert-1)}$. A path with support $F$
must enter each of the $\lvert F\rvert-1$ states of $F$ where it does not
start, and each first entry is a switch of probability about $T/N$. Every later
switch also has probability about $T/N$, but it multiplies the number of ways
to place the runs by a factor of order $N$, so it loses no power of $N$. Within
$F$ the best composition sits near the quasi-ergodic law $\alpha^*_F$. Here it
is $(\frac12,\frac12)$ on a pair, $(\frac14,\frac12,\frac14)$ on an arc (the
normalized squared Perron vector) and the uniform law on the cycle. Other
visit fractions $\alpha$ lose an extra factor
$e^{-T(I_F(\alpha)-\lambda_F)}$, with a rate $I_F$ that we now introduce.

The rest of this section proves the rule. Proposition~\ref{prop:rate} studies
the rate inside a face, Theorem~\ref{thm:first} proves the rule for every $Q$
and every start, and Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} reads the winners off a convex hull
as in Figure~\ref{fig:C4worked}(b). Section~\ref{sec:examples} returns to the
4-cycle with all constants (Corollary~\ref{cor:C4}).

\subsection{The rate inside a face}

For $\alpha\in\Delta_F$ let
\[
 I_F(\alpha)=\sup_{u\in(0,\infty)^F}\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_i\Bigl(-\frac{(Q_Fu)_i}{u_i}\Bigr)
 =\sup_{u\in(0,\infty)^F}\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_i\Bigl(q_i-\sum_{j\in F\setminus\{i\}}q_{ij}\frac{u_j}{u_i}\Bigr),
\]
and let $q_i^{\rm out}(F)=\sum_{j\notin F}q_{ij}$, the rate of leaving $F$ from
$i$. The function $I_F$ is the Donsker--Varadhan rate~\cite{dv1975} of the
occupation times of the chain killed on leaving $F$
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). The chance that the chain stays in $F$ with visit
fractions near $\alpha$ decays like $e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:first}(i)). Taking $u=\ones$ gives
$I_F(\alpha)\ge\sum_i\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F)$, and dropping the terms with
$q_{ij}$ gives $I_F(\alpha)\le\sum_i\alpha_iq_i$. So $I_F$ lies between the
rate of leaving $F$ and the rate of leaving the current state, both averaged
over $\alpha$. On the pair $\{1,2\}$ of Section~\ref{sec:worked}, part (c)
below gives $I_F(\alpha)=2-2\sqrt{\alpha_1\alpha_2}$, which is $2$ at a vertex
and has its least value $1$ at $(\frac12,\frac12)$. We need the properties of
$I_F$ without reversibility, and the proofs are short.

\begin{proposition}[the rate on a face]\label{prop:rate}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k=\lvert F\rvert\ge2$.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item $I_F\ge\lambda_F$ on $\Delta_F$, with equality exactly at
$\alpha^*_F=l_F\circ r_F$.
\item $I_F$ is convex on $\Delta_F$ and strictly convex on $\Delta_F^\circ$. For
$\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$ the supremum defining $I_F(\alpha)$ is attained at a
vector $u=r_\alpha$, which is unique up to a positive factor, and $\alpha$ is
the stationary law of the generator on $F$ with off-diagonal entries
$q_{ij}r_{\alpha,j}/r_{\alpha,i}$.
\item If there is $\nu\in(0,\infty)^F$ with $\nu_iq_{ij}=\nu_jq_{ji}$ for all
$i,j\in F$, then for every $\alpha\in\Delta_F$
\[
 I_F(\alpha)=\sum_{\{i,j\}\subseteq F,\ i\ne j}\bigl(\sqrt{\alpha_iq_{ij}}-\sqrt{\alpha_jq_{ji}}\bigr)^2
 +\sum_{i\in F}\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F).
\]
\item For every nonempty face $F'\subsetneq F$ and every
$\alpha\in\Delta_{F'}$, $I_F(\alpha)=I_{F'}(\alpha)\ge\lambda_{F'}>\lambda_F$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\emph{Idea of the proof.} For $u=r_F$ the quantity $-(Q_Fu)_i/u_i$ equals
$\lambda_F$ at every state $i$. So its average over any $\alpha$ is
$\lambda_F$, and $I_F\ge\lambda_F$. For the other properties we write
$u=e^\psi$. Then the function inside the supremum is concave in $\psi$ and
affine in $\alpha$, and $\psi$ is a maximizer exactly when $\alpha$ is
stationary for the chain on $F$ with rates $q_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i}$. For
$u=r_F$ this stationary law is $\alpha^*_F$, so $r_F$ is optimal there and
$I_F(\alpha^*_F)=\lambda_F$. Strict convexity then shows that $\alpha^*_F$ is
the only minimizer in the open face, and (d) handles the boundary. Part (d)
needs the lower bound on smaller faces, which may have no positive
eigenvector. So in the proof a resolvent applied to $\ones$ replaces $r_F$.

\begin{proof}
We prove the lower bound in (a) and the value at $\alpha^*_F$ first, and
then (b), (d), the rest of (a) and (c), in this order.

\emph{Setup.} With $u=e^\psi$ we have $I_F(\alpha)=\sup_\psi\Psi_\alpha(\psi)$,
where
\[
\Psi_\alpha(\psi)=\sum_i\alpha_iq_i-\sum_{i\ne j}\alpha_iq_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i}
\]
and $i,j$ run over $F$. Each exponential is convex in $\psi$, so $\Psi_\alpha$
is concave in $\psi$. It is affine in $\alpha$, and it does not change when we
add a constant to $\psi$. Let $G^\psi$ be the generator on $F$ with
off-diagonal entries $q_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i}$. It has the graph $G_F$, so it
is irreducible and has exactly one stationary law. Differentiating in each
$\psi_i$ gives $\nabla_\psi\Psi_\alpha=-\alpha^\top G^\psi$. A concave
function is largest exactly at its critical points. So $\psi$ attains
$I_F(\alpha)$ if and only if $\alpha$ is the stationary law of $G^\psi$. We
call this the criterion.

\emph{The lower bound in \textup{(a)}, for every face $F'$.} We show that
$I_{F'}\ge\lambda_{F'}$ on $\Delta_{F'}$ for every face $F'$, irreducible or
not. Let $s>\varrho(Q_{F'})=-\lambda_{F'}$. It is enough to find a positive
vector $u$ with $-(Q_{F'}u)_i/u_i>-s$ at every state $i$. Then
$I_{F'}(\alpha)>-s$ for every $\alpha$, and letting $s$ decrease to
$-\lambda_{F'}$ gives the claim. Write $Q_{F'}=B-cI$ with a matrix $B\ge0$ and
a number $c$. Then $s+c>\varrho(B)\ge0$, so the series
\[
u=(sI-Q_{F'})^{-1}\ones=\sum_{\ell\ge0}(s+c)^{-\ell-1}B^\ell\ones
\]
converges, and $u$ is positive because the term with $\ell=0$ is. Since
$(sI-Q_{F'})u=\ones$, we get $-(Q_{F'}u)_i/u_i=-s+1/u_i>-s$.

\emph{The value at $\alpha^*_F$.} Take $\psi=\log r_F$ and
$R=\operatorname{diag}(r_F)$. Then $G^\psi=R^{-1}(Q_F+\lambda_FI)R$, since the
off-diagonal entries agree and the rows of the right side sum to $0$ by
$Q_Fr_F=-\lambda_Fr_F$. As $\alpha^*_F=l_F\circ r_F$, we get
$(\alpha^*_F)^\top G^\psi=l_F^\top(Q_F+\lambda_FI)R=0$. By the criterion
$\psi$ attains the supremum. Its value is $\lambda_F$, because
$-(Q_Fr_F)_i/r_{F,i}=\lambda_F$ at every $i$. So $I_F(\alpha^*_F)=\lambda_F$.

(b) $I_F$ is convex as a supremum of functions that are affine in $\alpha$.
Now let $\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$.

\emph{The supremum is attained.} We normalize $\min_i\psi_i=0$. Let
$\bar\psi=\max_i\psi_i$, and let $a$ be the least positive rate $q_{ij}$ in
$F$. Take a shortest path in $G_F$ from a state where $\psi=0$ to a state
where $\psi=\bar\psi$. It has at most $k-1$ edges and $\psi$ increases by
$\bar\psi$ along it, so one of its edges $i\to j$ has
$\psi_j-\psi_i\ge\bar\psi/(k-1)$. Keeping only this edge in the second sum of
$\Psi_\alpha$ gives
\[
\Psi_\alpha(\psi)\le\sum_i\alpha_iq_i-a\,(\min_i\alpha_i)\,e^{\bar\psi/(k-1)} .
\]
The right side tends to $-\infty$ as $\bar\psi\to\infty$. So the supremum is
the supremum over a bounded set of normalized $\psi$, and the continuous
function $\Psi_\alpha$ attains it. We write $r_\alpha=e^\psi$ for a maximizer.
By the criterion $\alpha$ is the stationary law of $G^\psi$, which is the last
claim of (b).

\emph{The maximizer is unique up to a constant.} Suppose that $\psi$ and
$\psi+\delta$ both attain the supremum. By concavity $\Psi_\alpha$ is constant
on the segment $\psi+s\delta$, $0\le s\le1$. On this segment $\Psi_\alpha$ is
a constant minus the sum of the functions
$\alpha_iq_{ij}e^{\psi_j-\psi_i+s(\delta_j-\delta_i)}$, and each of these is
convex in $s$. A sum of convex functions is constant only if each of them is
affine, and an exponential $e^{x+sy}$ is affine in $s$ only if $y=0$. As every
$\alpha_i$ is positive, this gives $\delta_j=\delta_i$ on every edge of $G_F$.
Since $G_F$ is strongly connected, $\delta$ is constant.

\emph{Strict convexity.} Let $\alpha^0\ne\alpha^1$ lie in $\Delta_F^\circ$, let
$\alpha^s=(1-s)\alpha^0+s\alpha^1$ with $0<s<1$, and let $\psi$ attain
$I_F(\alpha^s)$. Since $\Psi_\alpha(\psi)$ is affine in $\alpha$,
\[
I_F(\alpha^s)=(1-s)\Psi_{\alpha^0}(\psi)+s\Psi_{\alpha^1}(\psi)
\le(1-s)I_F(\alpha^0)+sI_F(\alpha^1).
\]
Equality would mean that $\psi$ attains both $I_F(\alpha^0)$ and
$I_F(\alpha^1)$. By the criterion $\alpha^0$ and $\alpha^1$ would both be the
stationary law of $G^\psi$, which is impossible. So the inequality is strict.

(d) Let $\alpha\in\Delta_{F'}$. Only the rows $i\in F'$ enter
$\sum_i\alpha_i\bigl(q_i-\sum_{j\in F\setminus\{i\}}q_{ij}u_j/u_i\bigr)$, and
the terms with $j\in F\setminus F'$ are subtracted. Dropping them leaves the
function that defines $I_{F'}(\alpha)$, at the vector $(u_i)_{i\in F'}$. So
$I_F(\alpha)\le I_{F'}(\alpha)$. If we put $u_j=\varepsilon$ for
$j\in F\setminus F'$ and let $\varepsilon\to0$, the dropped terms tend to $0$,
so $I_F(\alpha)=I_{F'}(\alpha)$. Then $I_{F'}(\alpha)\ge\lambda_{F'}$ by the
lower bound above. Finally $\lambda_{F'}>\lambda_F$, because the Perron root
of an irreducible nonnegative matrix, here $Q_F+cI$ for a large number $c$, is
larger than that of each proper principal
submatrix~\cite[Ch.~2]{bermanplemmons1994}.

(a) The lower bound and the value at $\alpha^*_F$ are proved above, so let
$\alpha\ne\alpha^*_F$. If $\alpha$ lies on the boundary of $\Delta_F$, then
$\alpha\in\Delta_{F'}$ for the proper face $F'=\supp\alpha$, and (d) gives
$I_F(\alpha)>\lambda_F$. If $\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$ and
$I_F(\alpha)=\lambda_F$, then strict convexity gives
$I_F(\frac12(\alpha+\alpha^*_F))<\lambda_F$, against the lower bound.

(c) We bound the function inside the supremum pair by pair. Since
$q_i=\sum_{j\in F\setminus\{i\}}q_{ij}+q_i^{\rm out}(F)$, this function is
$\sum_i\alpha_iq_i^{\rm out}(F)$ plus, for each pair $\{i,j\}$, the term
$\alpha_iq_{ij}(1-u_j/u_i)+\alpha_jq_{ji}(1-u_i/u_j)$. By the AM--GM
inequality,
$\alpha_iq_{ij}u_j/u_i+\alpha_jq_{ji}u_i/u_j\ge2\sqrt{\alpha_iq_{ij}\alpha_jq_{ji}}$,
so the term is at most $(\sqrt{\alpha_iq_{ij}}-\sqrt{\alpha_jq_{ji}})^2$. This
gives the upper bound for $I_F(\alpha)$. For the lower bound first let
$\alpha\in\Delta_F^\circ$ and take $u_i=\sqrt{\alpha_i/\nu_i}$. Then
$\nu_iq_{ij}=\nu_jq_{ji}$ gives
$\alpha_iq_{ij}u_j/u_i=\alpha_jq_{ji}u_i/u_j$, so there is equality in the
AM--GM inequality for every pair at once. If some $\alpha_i$ vanish, we take
$u_i=\varepsilon$ for these $i$ and let $\varepsilon\to0$. A pair with
$\alpha_i=0<\alpha_j$ then has the term $\alpha_jq_{ji}(1-\varepsilon/u_j)$,
which tends to its bound $\alpha_jq_{ji}$. A pair with
$\alpha_i=\alpha_j=0$ has the term $0$, and the other pairs are as before.
\end{proof}

In words, the most likely way to stay on $F$ is to spend the fractions
$\alpha^*_F$ of the time in its states, and the rate at any other fractions is
strictly larger. Part (d) says that fractions on the boundary of $\Delta_F$,
which miss some states of $F$, have the rate of the smaller face that they
use, and this rate is larger than $\lambda_F$. The lower
bound in (a), the value at $\alpha^*_F$ and the interior form of (c) are checked
in Lean. For symmetric $Q_F$, $l_F=r_F$ and $\alpha^*_F$ is the entrywise square
of the Perron vector normalized in $\ell^2$.

\subsection{Face selection}

We also need an upper bound that holds on a whole face, including near its
boundary, where the local law of Section~\ref{sec:secondorder} does not apply.
For an irreducible face $F$ put
$V_F=\{\theta\in\R^F:\varrho(A_F-\operatorname{diag}\theta)=0\}$. Note that
$q-\lambda_F\ones\in V_F$, since $A_F-\operatorname{diag}(q-\lambda_F\ones)
=Q_F+\lambda_FI$. Each $\theta\in V_F$ gives an exponential bound with the
linear rate $\alpha\mapsto\langle q-\theta,\alpha\rangle$, and $I_F$ is the
supremum of these linear rates.

\begin{lemma}[a uniform upper bound]\label{lem:upper}
Let $F$ be irreducible with $k=\lvert F\rvert\ge2$, and let $\theta\in V_F$.
There is $C_\theta$, depending on $\theta$, $Q$ and $\mu$, such that for every
$n\in\Z_{\ge0}^F$ with $\supp n=F$ and every $h$ with $hq_{\max}\le\frac12$,
the probability that $X_1,\dots,X_{\lvert n\rvert}$ have composition $n$ is
at most
$C_\theta h^{k-1}\exp\bigl(-h\langle n,q-\theta\rangle+C_\theta h^2\lvert
n\rvert\bigr)$.
\end{lemma}

The proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:torus}. With $\theta=q-\lambda_F\ones$ it
gives $P_N(n)\le Ch^{k-1}e^{-T\lambda_F+CT^2/N}$ for every $n$ with support
$F$. As $h^{k-1}=T^{k-1}N^{-(k-1)}$, this is the upper half of the rule
$M_F\approx N^{-(k-1)}e^{-T\lambda_F}$ of Section~\ref{sec:worked}, up to a
power of $T$. The lemma is stated for a path of any length $\lvert n\rvert$,
not only $N$, so it also applies to the blocks of a path.

The next theorem uses the local law (Theorem~\ref{thm:local}) and the face
maxima (Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax}) of Section~\ref{sec:secondorder}, which
comes later. There is no circularity, since those two theorems are proved
there without Theorem~\ref{thm:first}.

\begin{theorem}[first-order face selection]\label{thm:first}
Fix $Q$ and $\mu$.
\begin{enumerate}[(i)]
\item Let $F$ satisfy (H), let $k=\lvert F\rvert$, and let
$\mathcal K\subset\Delta_F^\circ$ be compact. There is $N_0$ such that,
uniformly for $N\ge N_0$, $1\le T\le N^{1/3}$ and $\supp n=F$ with
$n/N\in\mathcal K$,
\[
 \log P_N(n)=-(k-1)L-T\,I_F(n/N)+O_{\mathcal K}(1+\log T).
\]
\item Let $F$ be admissible with components $C_1,\dots,C_r$, and let $k^*$ be
the least size of a component $C_j$ with $\lambda_{C_j}=\lambda_F$. There are
$\kappa$, $T_0$ and $N_0$ such that for $N\ge N_0$ and $T_0\le T\le N^{1/3}$
\begin{align*}
 \log M_F&\ge-(k_F-1)L+(k_F-k^*)\log T-T\lambda_F-\kappa,\\
 \log M_F&\le-(k_F-1)L+(k_F-1)\log T-T\lambda_F+\kappa.
\end{align*}
If $F$ is not admissible, $M_F=0$.
\item Let $T=\tau L$ with $\tau>0$ fixed, and let $\mathcal F_\tau$ be the set
of admissible faces that minimize $\Phi_\tau$. Then
$\log\max_nP_N(n)=-L\min_F\Phi_\tau(F)+O(\log L)$, with the minimum over
admissible faces, and for large $N$ every global mode $\hat n$ has
$\supp\hat n\in\mathcal F_\tau$. This holds for every start $\mu$.
\item If in (iii) every face in $\mathcal F_\tau$ is irreducible, then every
global mode $\hat n$ satisfies $\hat n/N=\alpha^*_F+O(1/L)$ with
$F=\supp\hat n$. This is the case when $Q$ has symmetric support or $\mu$ has
full support (Theorem~\ref{thm:hull}(c)).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In words, (i) says that a composition with fractions $\alpha$ inside $F$ has
probability $N^{-(k-1)}e^{-TI_F(\alpha)}$ up to a power of $T$. Part (ii) says
that the best composition on an admissible face has probability
$N^{-(k_F-1)}e^{-T\lambda_F}$ times a power of $T$ with exponent between $0$
and $k_F-1$, up to a bounded factor. So
by (iii), with $T=\tau L$ the exponent of $N$ is $-\Phi_\tau(F)$, and the mode
lies on a face that minimizes it, whatever the start. By (iv), inside the
winning face the mode sits near the quasi-ergodic law.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} For faces with (H), part (i) and the face maxima come
from the local law of Section~\ref{sec:secondorder}, which we prove
independently of this theorem. A reducible face is visited in blocks, one for
each strongly connected component. For the upper bound in (ii) we apply
Lemma~\ref{lem:upper} to each block, and for the lower bound we write down
explicit paths that spend almost all their time in a component of least exit
rate. Part (iii) compares the faces, and (iv) follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax}.

\begin{proof}
(i) For $k=1$ this is~\eqref{eq:vertexmax}, since $I_{\{i\}}=q_i$. For $k\ge2$,
Lemma~\ref{lem:discrete} gives $P_N(n)=N^{-(k-1)}f_T(n/N)(1+O(T^2/N))$ with $T^2/N\le N^{-1/3}$.
We claim that
$\log f_T(\alpha)=\frac{k-1}2\log T+\log C_F(\alpha)-TI_F(\alpha)+O_{\mathcal K}(1)$ for
$\alpha\in\mathcal K$ and $T\ge1$. For $T\ge T_1$, with $T_1$ large, this is Theorem~\ref{thm:local}.
For $1\le T\le T_1$ it holds because $f_T(\alpha)$ is positive and continuous on the compact set
$[1,T_1]\times\mathcal K$ (see the remarks after Theorem~\ref{thm:local}). Finally $C_F$ is positive
and continuous on $\mathcal K$, so $\log C_F$ is bounded there. So $\log f_T(n/N)=-TI_F(n/N)
+O_{\mathcal K}(1+\log T)$, and adding $\log N^{-(k-1)}=-(k-1)L$ gives (i).

(ii) If $F$ is not admissible, $M_F=0$. If $F$ is admissible and irreducible, it satisfies (H) by
Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}(b), and $k^*=k_F$. Then
Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax} gives both bounds, since $0\le\frac{k_F-1}2\log T\le(k_F-1)\log T$. For
a reducible face, a path visits each component in one block of consecutive times (proof of
Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}). For the upper bound, Lemma~\ref{lem:upper} (or a direct count if
$\lvert C\rvert=1$) bounds a block of length $N_C$ in the component $C$ by
$O(h^{\lvert C\rvert-1}e^{-h\lambda_CN_C})$, and each of the $r-1$
switches between blocks gives a factor $h$. As $\lambda_C\ge\lambda_F$, this gives
$M_F=O(h^{k_F-1}e^{-T\lambda_F})$. For the lower bound the path spends about $N/T$ visits in each
component except one of least exit rate, which loses a bounded factor per component, and we divide
by the number of compositions. The details are in Appendix~\ref{app:first}.

(iii) We compare the faces by (ii). For large $N$ we have $T_0\le T=\tau L\le N^{1/3}$, so (ii)
applies. There $T\lambda_F=\tau L\lambda_F$ and $\log T=O(\log L)$, so both bounds in (ii) read
$\log M_F=-L\,\Phi_\tau(F)+O(\log L)$ for every admissible face, while $M_F=0$ for the others.
Since $\max_nP_N(n)=\max_FM_F$ and there are finitely many faces, the first claim follows. For
the second claim let $m_\tau=\min_F\Phi_\tau(F)$. An admissible $F\notin\mathcal F_\tau$ has
$\Phi_\tau(F)\ge m_\tau+\varepsilon_0$ for a fixed $\varepsilon_0>0$, so
$M_F\le N^{-m_\tau-\varepsilon_0+o(1)}$, which is smaller than $\max_nP_N(n)=N^{-m_\tau+o(1)}$
for large $N$. So the support of a global mode is not such a face. A global mode has positive
probability, so its support is admissible, and hence it lies in $\mathcal F_\tau$.

(iv) By (iii), for large $N$ a global mode $\hat n$ maximizes $P_N$ on its face
$F\in\mathcal F_\tau$, which is irreducible and admissible and so satisfies (H) by
Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}(b). So Theorem~\ref{thm:facemax} with
$T=\tau L\le N^{1/3}$ gives $\hat n/N=\alpha^*_F+O(1/L)$.
\end{proof}

For a reducible face we only have the bounds in (ii), and
Proposition~\ref{prop:threecycle} shows that the power of $T$ can differ from
$(k_F-1)/2$.

\subsection{Reading the winners off a convex hull}

For an admissible face $F$ put $P_F=(\lambda_F,\lvert F\rvert-1)$, and let $S$
be the set of these points. Then $\Phi_\tau(F)=\tau x+y$ at $(x,y)=P_F$. Picture
a line of slope $-\tau$ moving up from far below. The first points of $S$ that
it touches are the winners at $\tau$. For each $\tau>0$ the points of
$\operatorname{conv}S$ where $\tau x+y$ is least form a vertex or an edge of
$\operatorname{conv}S$, and we call the union of these over all $\tau>0$ the
lower-left boundary of $\operatorname{conv}S$. Figure~\ref{fig:C4worked}(b) shows
it for the 4-cycle.

\begin{theorem}[geometry of the winners]\label{thm:hull}
Let $\mathcal F_\tau$ be the set of admissible faces that minimize
$\Phi_\tau$.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The lower-left boundary of $\operatorname{conv}S$ is a path whose
vertices lie in $S$. Read from right to left, they are
$v_i=(\lambda^{(i)},y^{(i)})$, $0\le i\le m$, with
$\lambda^{(0)}>\dots>\lambda^{(m)}$ and $y^{(0)}<\dots<y^{(m)}$. Put
$\tau_i=(y^{(i)}-y^{(i-1)})/(\lambda^{(i-1)}-\lambda^{(i)})$ for $1\le i\le m$,
$\tau_0=0$ and $\tau_{m+1}=\infty$. Then $\tau_1<\dots<\tau_m$. For
$\tau_i<\tau<\tau_{i+1}$, $\mathcal F_\tau$ is the set of faces $F$ with
$P_F=v_i$. At $\tau=\tau_i$ it is the set of faces whose point lies on the
closed edge $[v_{i-1},v_i]$.
\item If $0<\tau<\tau'$, $F\in\mathcal F_\tau$ and $G\in\mathcal F_{\tau'}$,
then $\lambda_F\ge\lambda_G$ and $\lvert F\rvert\le\lvert G\rvert$. Both
inequalities are strict when $\mathcal F_\tau\cap\mathcal F_{\tau'}=\emptyset$.
\item If $Q$ has symmetric support, or $\mu$ has full support, then every face
in $\mathcal F_\tau$ is irreducible, for every $\tau>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

In words, as $\tau$ grows the winners walk up the lower-left boundary of the
hull, from small faces with large exit rates to large faces with small exit
rates, and they change at the values $\tau_i$, minus the slopes of its edges.

\emph{Idea of the proof.} The least value $g(\tau)$ of $\Phi_\tau$ is a minimum
of finitely many affine functions of $\tau$, so it is concave and piecewise
affine. Its pieces are the vertices $v_i$ and its kinks are the values
$\tau_i$. Part (b) adds two inequalities, and part (c) replaces a reducible
face by one of its components.

\begin{proof}
(a) We study the least value $g(\tau)=\min_{(x,y)\in S}(\tau x+y)$ for $\tau>0$. Each point
$(x,y)$ of $S$ gives the affine function $\tau\mapsto\tau x+y$, whose slope is $x$, and different
points give different functions. So $g$ is a minimum of finitely many affine functions. It is
concave and piecewise affine. Let $\tau_1<\dots<\tau_m$ be the points of $(0,\infty)$ where its
slope changes, and put $\tau_0=0$ and $\tau_{m+1}=\infty$.

\emph{Between two kinks.} On $(\tau_i,\tau_{i+1})$ the function $g$ is affine. The affine
function of a point of $S$ is nowhere below $g$. So if it equals $g$ at one $\tau$ inside this
interval, it equals $g$ on the whole interval. Hence exactly one point of $S$ attains $g$ there.
We call it $v_i=(\lambda^{(i)},y^{(i)})$, and for $\tau_i<\tau<\tau_{i+1}$ the set
$\mathcal F_\tau$ consists of the faces $F$ with $P_F=v_i$. The slope of $g$ on this interval is
$\lambda^{(i)}$. The slope of a concave function decreases, and it changes at each $\tau_i$, so
$\lambda^{(0)}>\dots>\lambda^{(m)}$. By continuity both $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$ attain $g(\tau_i)$,
namely
\[
\tau_i\lambda^{(i-1)}+y^{(i-1)}=\tau_i\lambda^{(i)}+y^{(i)} .
\]
This is the formula for $\tau_i$ in the statement, and since $\tau_i>0$ it gives
$y^{(i)}>y^{(i-1)}$.

\emph{At a kink.} The points $(x,y)$ of $S$ that attain $g(\tau_i)$ are those on the line
$\tau_ix+y=g(\tau_i)$, which passes through $v_{i-1}$ and $v_i$. Let $(x,y)$ be one of them. For
$\tau$ slightly below $\tau_i$ we have $\tau x+y\ge g(\tau)=\tau\lambda^{(i-1)}+y^{(i-1)}$, with
equality at $\tau=\tau_i$. Subtracting gives $(\tau-\tau_i)(x-\lambda^{(i-1)})\ge0$, so
$x\le\lambda^{(i-1)}$. In the same way, $\tau$ slightly above $\tau_i$ gives
$x\ge\lambda^{(i)}$. So $(x,y)$ lies on the closed edge $[v_{i-1},v_i]$. Conversely every point of
$S$ on this edge lies on the line and so attains $g(\tau_i)$.

\emph{The boundary.} On $\operatorname{conv}S$ the function $\tau x+y$ is least exactly on the
convex hull of the points of $S$ where it is least. By the two steps above this hull is the
vertex $v_i$ for $\tau_i<\tau<\tau_{i+1}$ and the edge $[v_{i-1},v_i]$ for $\tau=\tau_i$. So the
lower-left boundary is the path $v_0\cdots v_m$, and its vertices lie in $S$.

(b) We add two inequalities. Since $F$ wins at $\tau$ and $G$ wins at $\tau'$, we have
$\Phi_\tau(F)\le\Phi_\tau(G)$ and $\Phi_{\tau'}(G)\le\Phi_{\tau'}(F)$. Adding them gives
$(\tau'-\tau)(\lambda_F-\lambda_G)\ge0$, so $\lambda_F\ge\lambda_G$. Then the first inequality
gives $\lvert F\rvert-\lvert G\rvert\le\tau(\lambda_G-\lambda_F)\le0$. If
$\lambda_F=\lambda_G$, the two inequalities give $\lvert F\rvert=\lvert G\rvert$, so
$\Phi_{\tau'}(F)=\Phi_{\tau'}(G)$ and $F\in\mathcal F_{\tau'}$. So if $\mathcal F_\tau$ and
$\mathcal F_{\tau'}$ are disjoint, then $\lambda_F>\lambda_G$, and the first inequality gives
$\lvert F\rvert<\lvert G\rvert$.

(c) With symmetric support every admissible face is irreducible by
Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}(c). Now let $\mu$ have full support, and let $F$ be admissible and
reducible. Take a component $C$ of $F$ with $\lambda_C=\lambda_F$. It is irreducible with
$\mu(C)>0$, so it is admissible by Lemma~\ref{lem:admissible}(b). It has the same exit rate as
$F$ and fewer states, so $\Phi_\tau(C)\le\Phi_\tau(F)-1$. Hence $F\notin\mathcal F_\tau$.
\end{proof}

The finite form of Theorem~\ref{thm:hull} is checked in Lean. A face with small $\lambda_F$ for
its size is a set of states that the chain rarely leaves, a well-separated community. As $\tau$
grows the exit rate counts for more, and the mode moves to larger communities. It need not
grow around its first vertex, and it can move to a disjoint community (Table~\ref{tab:outcomes}).
For symmetric $Q$, $\lambda_F$ is the least value of
$\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}(f_i-f_j)^2$, the sum over all pairs in $[d]$, over unit
vectors $f$ supported in $F$ (Rayleigh--Ritz), and it
decreases strictly as a connected face grows, by Proposition~\ref{prop:rate}(d). So only the least
exit rate of each size matters. Figure~\ref{fig:cycles}(a) shows $S$ for the 6-cycle.
