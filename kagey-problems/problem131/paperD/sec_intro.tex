\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The elephant random walk of Sch\"utz and Trimper~\cite{schutz2004} remembers
all its earlier steps. After a fair first step, it chooses an earlier step
uniformly and copies its sign, reversing that sign with probability
$\varepsilon$. (This reverses the sampled step, which need not be the most
recent one.) For $N$ steps put $x=N\varepsilon$. We study $x$ of order $\log N$,
so the reversal probability tends to zero even though the expected number of
reversals, $x-\varepsilon$, grows. Most limit theorems instead keep
$\varepsilon$ fixed~\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019,glrs2025,tv2026}.

The number of steps that disagree with the first step is already a known
chain, namely the resistant-cell count in the reversible fluctuation assay of
Bokes, Hlubinov\'a and Singh~\cite{bhs2023}, with equal switching probabilities.
Their first-switch and P\'olya-urn calculation gives its law to first order in
the switching probability~\cite[Eqs.~(6)--(10)]{bhs2023}, including the center
coefficient $4/(N+2)$. They also approximate a random-start law by two Landau
densities~\cite[Eqs.~(58)--(60)]{bhs2023}. Their matched asymptotic analysis does
not give rigorous error bounds. This is the starting point for our
quantitative comparison.

For comparison with other conventions, the memory parameter
of~\cite{schutz2004} is $1-\varepsilon$, the parameter $a$ of~\cite{glrs2025} is
$1-2\varepsilon$, and the broadcasting flip probability is $\varepsilon$.
The broadcasting picture puts the steps on a random recursive tree, where
each new vertex chooses a uniform earlier parent and copies or reverses its
color~\cite{adlv2022,ahs2026} (Lemma~\ref{lem:tree}). For
$\varepsilon\le\frac12$ one can instead copy the parent with probability
$1-2\varepsilon$ and otherwise choose a fresh fair sign. This is also the
retention probability in the percolation of Section~\ref{sec:prior}.

We ask when the walk is as likely to end in the middle as at each end. Let $N$
be even, and let $A_N$ be the number of steps equal to $+1$. The walk ends at
the origin, which we call the middle, when $A_N=N/2$. It ends at $N$ or at
$-N$, which we call the ends, when $A_N=N$ or $A_N=0$. If $\varepsilon$ is
close to $0$ the walk rarely reverses, so the ends win. If $\varepsilon=\frac12$
the steps are independent and fair, so the middle wins. The crossing is the
value of $\varepsilon$ in between at which the middle and each end are equally
likely. In the language of broadcasting, the walk ends in the middle when the
two colors tie.

In the persistent random walk each step repeats the previous one with
probability $p$ and switches with probability $q=1-p$. So with $q=\varepsilon$
it is the walk that copies its last step instead of a uniformly chosen one.
Its steps are the spins of the Ising chain in zero field with free ends, and
its position is the magnetization. For this Markov walk our question is part
of Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131}, which is
posed for a Galton board. As on that board, we call the values of $A_N$ bins.
Let $T=Nq$, which exceeds the expected number $(N-1)q$ of switches by $q$. The middle catches
up with the ends when $T$ is about $\log N$, and~\cite{paperA} locates this
crossing within explicit bounds for every $N\ge2$.

In this paper we find the crossing of the elephant walk. The middle needs
about twice as many reversals as the persistent walk needs switches, and
the crossing value of $x$ is about $2\log N$, not $\log N$. K\"ursten's
coupling~\cite{kursten2016} splits the steps into the clusters of bond
percolation on a random recursive tree, and our regime $x\asymp\log N$ lies in
the strongly supercritical regime of this
percolation~\cite{baur2016}. The results there describe cluster
sizes, not the probability of the middle (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:notation}

The walk takes $N$ steps $X_1,\dots,X_N\in\{+1,-1\}$, where
$+1$ means right. Let $A_N=\#\{i\le N:X_i=+1\}$ be the number of right steps,
and write $P_N(k)=\Prob(A_N=k)$ for the probability of bin $k$. Unless we say otherwise the first
step is fair, $\Prob(X_1=+1)=\frac12$, and the rules treat the two directions
alike, so $P_N(k)=P_N(N-k)$. We write $\Prob_+$ and $\E_+$ for probability and
expectation given $X_1=+1$. For even $N$ we put $h=N/2$ and call
$C_N=P_N(h)$ the center and $E_N=P_N(N)=P_N(0)$ the end. Whenever $C_N$
appears, $N$ is even. The crossing is the value of the parameter at which
$C_N=E_N$. All logarithms are natural, $L=\log N$,
$H_k=\sum_{j\le k}1/j$ is the $k$-th harmonic number, and $\gamma$ is Euler's
constant. Limits, $O(\cdot)$ and $o(\cdot)$ refer to $N\to\infty$, over even
$N$ when $C_N$ appears. The constants implied by $O(\cdot)$ are absolute
unless we name what they depend on.

The elephant walk (Definition~\ref{def:erw}) has the reversal probability
$\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $x=N\varepsilon$. Its crossing is $\varepsilon_N$,
and $x_N=N\varepsilon_N$. The symbols that only one section uses are defined in that section, where
they first appear.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:results}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main-bulk} is the main approximation tool. We first state
the center and crossing bounds, then the bulk and shape results.

\begin{theorem}[the center and the crossing]\label{thm:main-center}
Let $N\ge2$ be even.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The ratio $C_N/E_N$ is strictly increasing in $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, and
the crossing $\varepsilon_N$ exists, is unique and lies in $(0,\frac12)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mlr}).
\item For $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$ we have
$C_N\ge\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}(1-2\varepsilon)$. If
$\varepsilon(1+\log N)\le\frac1{100}$, then
$C_N\le\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}\,N^{2\varepsilon}e^{5200\varepsilon}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}, whose constant $5200$ uses an enclosure in ball
arithmetic).
\item As $N\to\infty$ over even $N$, the crossing satisfies
$x_N+\log x_N=2\log N-\log8+O(\log^2N/N)$, and so
\[
 x_N=2\log N-\log\log N-\log16+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\Bigr)
\]
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-crossing}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

So $C_N=\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}(1+O(\varepsilon\log N))$ when
$\varepsilon\log N$ is small, and the error factor is $1+O(\log^2N/N)$ at the
crossing. The persistent walk has its crossing at $T$ close to $\log N$, and
Theorem~S1.7 of Online Resource~1 compares the two crossings to the next order.

For the rest of the law let $M_N$ be the number of steps that disagree with
the first step. Its law $f_N(m)=\Prob(M_N=m)$ does not depend on $X_1$, and
$P_N(k)=\frac12[f_N(N-k)+f_N(k)]$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:erw-reduction}). Let
$\CP_N$ be the law of $\sum_{d=1}^{N-1}d\,\mathcal N_d$, where the
$\mathcal N_d$ are independent Poisson variables with means $x/(d(d+1))$.
Put $\lambda_m=(m-x\log x)/x$, and let
\[
 f_L(\lambda)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}e^{B(t)}\,dt,
 \qquad B(t)=-it\log\lvert t\rvert-\frac\pi2\lvert t\rvert,
\]
be the Landau density.

\begin{theorem}[the bulk]\label{thm:main-bulk}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For $N\ge1$ and $0<\varepsilon<1$,
\[
 \sup_{m\ge0}\bigl\lvert f_N(m)-\CP_N(m)\bigr\rvert\le\delta_\Sigma
 :=\varepsilon^2(NH_N-2N+1)+2\varepsilon^2(N-1)+4\varepsilon^2\sum_{k=0}^{N-2}(H_k^2+H_k),
\]
and $\delta_\Sigma\le\delta_B:=\frac{x^2\log N}{N}+\frac{4x^2}{N}e^{4x^2/N}+\frac{4x^2(H_N^2+H_N)}{N}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}).
\item If $0<\varepsilon\le\frac14$ and $x\ge1$, then for every integer $m\ge0$
\[
 \bigl\lvert xf_N(m)-f_L(\lambda_m)\bigr\rvert\le\frac{889+165\log x}x+\frac{6.6\,x}N+x\delta_\Sigma
 \le889\Bigl(\frac{1+\log x}x+\frac xN\Bigr)+x\delta_B .
\]
If $0<\varepsilon<1$, $x\to\infty$ and $x^3\log^2N/N\to0$, then $(M_N-x\log x)/x$
converges in law to the law with density $f_L$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}).
\item The density $f_L$ has exactly one mode $\lambda_{\mathrm L}$, and
$-0.22278299<\lambda_{\mathrm L}<-0.22278297$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode},
computer-assisted). There is $K$ such that for every large $N$ and every $\varepsilon$ with
$\log N\le x\le2\log N$, every mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ satisfies
\[
 \bigl\lvert N-a^*-x\log x-\lambda_{\mathrm L}x\bigr\rvert\le K\sqrt{x\log x},
\]
and every mode of $f_N$ satisfies the same in place of $N-a^*$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}(b)). This holds at the crossing for every large even $N$.
We do not compute $K$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

So at the crossing the law of $A_N$ has a hump near each end, about
$x\log x-0.2228\,x$ bins from it. The final position is $2A_N-N$, so in
physical distance each hump is twice as far from $\pm N$. For the shape of the law between
the humps let $D_N(a)=P_N(a+1)-2P_N(a)+P_N(a-1)$ be its second difference,
and for a mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ put $m^*=N-a^*$.

\begin{theorem}[the shape]\label{thm:main-shape}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item Let $N\ge3$ and $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$. Delete the zeros from the list
$D_N(1),\dots,D_N(N-1)$, replace each entry by its sign, and merge equal
neighbors. The result is a subsequence of $(+,-,+,-,+)$. In particular the
second difference changes sign at most four times
(Theorem~\ref{thm:five-blocks}).
\item For every even $N\ge8$, at the crossing, $E_N=C_N<P_N(N-1)$, so $P_N$
is not unimodal (Proposition~\ref{prop:dip-hyp}, computer-assisted). By contrast $f_N$ is unimodal for every $N$ and every
$\varepsilon$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:unimodal}, from~\cite{glrs2025}).
\item For every large $N$, even or odd, and every $\varepsilon$ with
$\log N\le x\le2\log N$, the following holds. For every mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ we
have $D_N(a)>0$ for $2m^*+1\le a\le N-2m^*-1$, so $P_N$ is strictly convex on
$\{2m^*,\dots,N-2m^*\}$. This holds at the crossing for every large even $N$, and there also
$P_N(a)>C_N$ for every $1\le a\le N-1$ with
$a\ne h$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:unique-min}). We do not compute the thresholds. Both conclusions rest on the
computer-assisted Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode}.
\item At the crossing, the convexity conclusion of (c) holds for every even $N$ with
$6\le N\le2000$, and the center-minimum conclusion holds for every even $N$ with
$8\le N\le2000$
(Propositions~\ref{prop:convex-certified}
and~\ref{prop:center-small}, computer-assisted).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For the even $N$ above $2000$ and below the thresholds of (c) both
conclusions are open (Conjecture~\ref{conj:convex} and
Remark~\ref{rem:convex-open}). The second would follow from the first by
Lemma~\ref{lem:center-convex} whenever each mode lies within $(N-2)/4$ of the
nearer end.

The three computational inputs are described in Section~\ref{sec:verify},
with the result each one supports. The tables and figures illustrate the
theorems and are not used in their proofs.

\paragraph{Why the elephant needs twice as many reversals.}
This is a heuristic for the scales, made precise in
Sections~\ref{sec:erw} and~\ref{sec:shape}. An end has probability about
$\frac12e^{-x}$, since the first step must point there and no reversal can
occur. The same holds for the persistent walk with its switch count $T$ in
place of $x$. What changes is the probability of the middle.

In the persistent walk the switches split the trajectory into runs of
typical length $N/T$. The number of right steps spreads over about
$N/\sqrt T$ bins, so its central mass is of order $\sqrt T/N$. Balancing
this with $e^{-T}$ gives $T\approx\log N$.

An elephant reversal is copied by later steps, and those copies are copied
again. Its descendants form a family whose share follows P\'olya's urn.
A late reversal usually has a small family. Reaching the middle instead
requires a family of about half the walk, hence an early reversal. Its
probability is of order $\varepsilon$, and landing at the exact middle costs
another factor $1/N$. Thus the central mass is of order
$\varepsilon/N=x/N^2$, in fact about $4x/N^2$. (At the crossing there are
about $2\log N$ reversals in all, but most are late.) Balancing with
$e^{-x}$ now gives $x\approx2\log N$.

Our contribution is a truncated compound-Poisson approximation with an
explicit transfer error, a Landau local limit with a rate, the resulting
mode and dip theorems for the walk itself, and the crossing expansion with
uniform relative control of the center.

\subsection{Prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The asymptotic behavior of the elephant walk for a fixed memory parameter is
well understood~\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019}. The coin-turning walk of Engl\"ander and
Volkov~\cite{ev2018} is the persistent walk with a switch probability
$p_n$ that depends on the time $n$. When the switch probability is
eventually $a/n$, that is, $p_n=a/n$ for $n\ge n_0$, the frequency of heads
tends to the Beta$(a,a)$ law~\cite[Section~4, Theorem~1]{ev2018}, which is
U-shaped for $a<1$ and humped for $a>1$, so in that limit the ends and the
middle trade places at $a=1$. Our question is a finite-$N$ version of this
comparison for the elephant walk, whose reversal probability depends on $N$
and not on the time.

For
$\varepsilon\le\frac12$ the steps of the elephant walk split into clusters, since a step either
copies an earlier step exactly, with probability $1-2\varepsilon$, or starts
a new cluster with a fair sign. This is K\"ursten's coupling with bond
percolation on random recursive trees~\cite{kursten2016}, and Baur and
Bertoin~\cite{baurbertoin2016} relate the walk to P\'olya-type urns. Our
regime $N\varepsilon\asymp\log N$ lies in the strongly supercritical regime
of Baur~\cite{baur2016}, who extends the results of
Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014} for retention probability $1-t/\log n$. Baur's
Corollary~1 gives the sizes of the largest clusters other than the root's (a
Poisson limit with intensity $a^{-2}\,da$). Heuristically, in our regime this
gives about $2x/m^2$ clusters of size $m$, since a new cluster starts with
probability $2\varepsilon$. Half of them have the sign opposite to the root,
which gives the $x/m^2$ families of size $m$ behind the tail of the law. These results
describe cluster sizes, not the center probability, which is a large deviation
of the root cluster. Miyazaki and Takei~\cite[Theorems~2.3 and~2.4]{miyazakitakei2020} prove limit
theorems for the `laziest' minimal walk of elephant type at every value of its
memory parameter, and with them for the size of the root cluster in
percolation on uniform random recursive trees~\cite[Section~2.3]{miyazakitakei2020}. The law that appears in the bulk
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cp} and~\ref{thm:landau}) is also known. Without its
truncation, our compound Poisson law is the Lea--Coulson form of the
Luria--Delbr\"uck distribution, and Kessler and
Levine~\cite[Eqs.~(14), (18) and~(19)]{kesslerlevine2015} describe its profile
$x/(m(m+1))$ for small $x$ and its Landau limit for large $x$.
M\"ohle~\cite[Theorem~4.1 and Corollary~4.1]{mohle2005} proves this stable
limit and gives integral formulas for its density, and his
Corollary~4.2 is a local limit theorem for the untruncated law, uniform on
the lattice, as the parameter tends to infinity through the integers. It has
no rate. Kessler and Levine also note that the law stops being monotone at
$\mu N=2$ and that its maximum sits at about $\mu N(\log\mu N-0.22)$ for
large $\mu N$~\cite[Section~2]{kesslerlevine2015}, where $\mu N$ plays the
role of our $x$. These are the analogues for the mutant count of the dip at
the end (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}) and of the place of the modes
(Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}). The same stable law governs the
fluctuations of the root cluster when the retention probability is
$1-c/\log n$. This follows from a result of
Schweinsberg~\cite{schweinsberg2012}, and Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014ejp}
explains it through the growth of the tree. What we add is the truncation at
$d=N-1$, a rate in the local limit, the transfer from this law to the
elephant walk in our regime (with an explicit error in
Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}), the dip and the modes for the walk itself rather
than for the compound Poisson law, and a certified enclosure of the Landau
mode.

The same model is studied as broadcasting on random recursive trees. Addario-Berry, Devroye, Lugosi and Velona~\cite{adlv2022} and Althaus,
Hartung and Steiner~\cite{ahs2026} study how well the color of the root can be
recovered from the colors at time $N$, with the flip probability fixed as
$N\to\infty$. In that language $N-2M_N$ is the number of vertices with the
color of the root minus the number with the other color, and the center $C_N$
is the probability of a tie, which enters the error of the majority vote.
Desmarais, Holmgren and Wagner~\cite{dhw2023} prove limit laws for the color
counts of broadcasting on linear preferential attachment trees, a class that
contains the random recursive tree, and Kardas, Shneer and
Wachtel~\cite{ksw2026} give martingale methods and error bounds, again with a
fixed flip probability.

Gu\'erin, Laulin, Raschel and Simon~\cite[Section~2, proof of Theorem~1.1]{glrs2025}
prove that the law with a fixed first step is unimodal, and their other
results fix the memory parameter as $N$ grows. So do the local limit theorem
of Trinas and Valle~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{tv2026} for the superdiffusive walk
and their bounds on where log-concavity fails. Gut and Stadtm\"uller~\cite[(1.5)]{gs2026} study a
walk with steps in $\{0,1\}$ whose first step is $1$ with probability $p$ and
whose later steps copy a uniform earlier step with probability $p$ and are $0$
otherwise. Given $k$ ones among the first $n$ steps, the next step is $1$ with
probability $pk/n$, while for our count $A_N$ the corresponding probability
is $\varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)k/n$. So their count cannot leave $0$ and
ours can, and their walk does not give our count $A_N$.

Kessler and Levine~\cite[Eqs.~(10), (13) and~(14)]{kesslerlevine2013} solve the irreversible
mutation chain, in which a mutant never reverts, to first order in the mutation probability,
with an outer solution that covers numbers of mutants of order $N$. Its law has the same first
derivative in $\varepsilon$ at $\varepsilon=0$ as the law of $M_N$. We did not find, in the elephant-walk,
broadcasting, percolation or Luria--Delbr\"uck literature that we read, a
rigorous estimate of the center with a uniform relative error when
$N\varepsilon$ is of order $\log N$.

\subsection{Organization}\label{sec:organization}

Section~\ref{sec:erw} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main-center}, with the bounds
on the center proved in Appendix~\ref{app:erw}. It also shows that $C_N$ is a polynomial
in $\varepsilon$ with $C_N'(0)=4/(N+2)$ exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:first-mutation}).
Section~\ref{sec:shape} proves
Theorems~\ref{thm:main-bulk} and~\ref{thm:main-shape}. The bulk is proved in
Appendix~\ref{app:bulk}, which uses the bound on the mode of $f_N$ from
Appendix~\ref{app:shape}, and the sign pattern of the second difference in
Appendix~\ref{app:signs}. Section~\ref{sec:shape} also bounds the dip $P_N(N-1)/E_N$ at the
end from both sides (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}), gives the place of the modes with explicit
constants (Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}(a)), and shows that one point of convexity gives
convexity between the humps (Theorem~\ref{thm:no-holes} and Corollary~\ref{cor:one-point}).
Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the
computations. Online Resource~1 holds the center and the crossing to second
order, the comparison with the persistent walk, the tail $P_N(N-m)\sim x/(2m^2)$ beyond the
humps, and the crossing and the convexity for odd $N$. None of Theorems~\ref{thm:main-center}
to~\ref{thm:main-shape} uses it.
