\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The elephant random walk of Sch\"utz and Trimper~\cite{schutz2004} remembers
its whole past. In~\cite{schutz2004} the first step is $+1$ with a
probability that is a parameter of the model, and we take it to be
$\frac12$, so that the first step is $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac12$
each. Each later step picks one of the earlier steps uniformly at random and
repeats it, except that with probability $\varepsilon$ it reverses it. For a
walk with $N$ steps we write $x=N\varepsilon$. The expected number of
reversals is $(N-1)\varepsilon=x-\varepsilon$, so $x$ is this number up to an
error below one. We let $A_N$ be the number of steps equal to $+1$.

We ask when the walk is as likely to end in the middle as at each end. Let $N$
be even. The walk ends at the origin, which we call the middle, when
$A_N=N/2$. It ends at $N$ or at $-N$, which we call the ends, when $A_N=N$ or
$A_N=0$. If $\varepsilon$ is close to 0 the walk rarely reverses, so the ends
win. If $\varepsilon=\frac12$ the steps are independent and fair, so the
middle wins. The crossing is the value of $\varepsilon$ in between at which the
middle and each end are equally likely.

In the persistent random walk each step repeats the previous one with
probability $p$ and switches with probability $q=1-p$. So with $q=\varepsilon$
it is the walk that copies its last step instead of a uniformly chosen one.
Its steps are the spins of the Ising chain in zero field with free ends, and
its position is the magnetization. For this Markov walk our question is part
of Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131}, which is
posed for a Galton board. As on that board, we call the values of $A_N$ bins.
Let $T=Nq$, which exceeds the expected number $(N-1)q$ of switches by $q$. The middle catches
up with the ends when $T$ is about $\log N$, and~\cite{paperA} locates this
crossing within explicit bounds for every $N\ge2$.

In this paper we find the crossing of the elephant walk. The middle needs
about twice as many reversals as the persistent walk needs switches. Namely,
the crossing value of $x$ is about $2\log N$, not $\log N$. The limit theorems
of~\cite{glrs2025,tv2026} for the elephant walk fix the memory parameter, here
$1-\varepsilon$, as $N$ grows, while we let $\varepsilon\to0$. K\"ursten's
coupling~\cite{kursten2016} splits the steps into the clusters of bond
percolation on a random recursive tree, and our regime $x\asymp\log N$ lies in
the strongly supercritical regime of this
percolation~\cite{baur2016}. The results there describe cluster
sizes, not the probability of the middle (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).

\paragraph{Why the elephant needs twice as many reversals.}
The following argument is heuristic, and Sections~\ref{sec:erw} and~\ref{sec:shape} make it precise.
The ends are easy. The walk ends in the right end bin exactly when its first
step goes right and it never changes direction. This has probability
$\frac12(1-\varepsilon)^{N-1}\approx\frac12e^{-x}$ for the elephant walk, and
the same with $q$ in place of $\varepsilon$ for the persistent walk. So only the
middle differs. In the persistent walk the $T$ switches are spread over the whole
walk, and each switch moves the final position by about one run length $N/T$. So the
number of right steps spreads over about $N/\sqrt T$ bins, the middle bin has
probability of order $\sqrt T/N$, and the ends lose when $e^{-T}$ falls to
about $1/N$, that is, when $T\approx\log N$.

The elephant walk spreads a reversal in a different way. A reversal at step
$t+1$ is copied by later steps, those copies are copied, and so on. The steps
that descend from it form a family, whose share of the walk behaves like the
share of one ball in P\'olya's urn started with one ball against $t$. So a late
reversal has a small family and hardly moves the walk.
To end exactly in the middle, the walk usually needs a family that fills
about half the walk, so in most such walks a reversal happens among the first few steps. This has probability
of order $\varepsilon$, and hitting the exact middle brings another factor of
order $1/N$. So the middle bin has probability of order $\varepsilon/N=x/N^2$ (in
fact about $4x/N^2$), and the ends lose only when $e^{-x}$ falls to about
$1/N^2$, that is, when $x\approx2\log N$. More precisely, at the crossing of
the persistent walk $\frac12e^{-T}\approx\sqrt{2T/\pi}/N$, and squaring this relation turns it
into the elephant relation $\frac12e^{-x}\approx4x/N^2$ with
$x\approx2T-\log(2\pi)$.

The small families also give the shape of the law. About $x/m^2$ reversals
have a family of $m$ steps, so the bin at distance $m$ from an end has
probability about $x/(2m^2)$ over a wide range of $m$. So the law has a hump
near each end, and the middle is where the two tails meet. Indeed, twice
$x/(2m^2)$ at $m=N/2$ is the $4x/N^2$ above.

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:notation}

The walk takes $N$ steps $X_1,\dots,X_N\in\{+1,-1\}$, where
$+1$ means right. Let $A_N=\#\{i\le N:X_i=+1\}$ be the number of right steps,
and write $P_N(k)=\Prob(A_N=k)$ for the probability of bin $k$. Unless we say otherwise the first
step is fair, $\Prob(X_1=+1)=\frac12$, and the rules treat the two directions
alike, so $P_N(k)=P_N(N-k)$. We write $\Prob_+$ and $\E_+$ for probability and
expectation given $X_1=+1$. For even $N$ we put $h=N/2$ and call
$C_N=P_N(h)$ the center and $E_N=P_N(N)=P_N(0)$ the end. Whenever $C_N$
appears, $N$ is even. The crossing is the value of the parameter at which
$C_N=E_N$. All logarithms are natural, $L=\log N$,
$H_k=\sum_{j\le k}1/j$ is the $k$-th harmonic number, and $\gamma$ is Euler's
constant. Limits, $O(\cdot)$ and $o(\cdot)$ refer to $N\to\infty$, over even
$N$ when $C_N$ appears. The constants implied by $O(\cdot)$ are absolute
unless we name what they depend on.

The elephant walk (Definition~\ref{def:erw}) has the reversal probability
$\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $x=N\varepsilon$. Its crossing is $\varepsilon_N$,
and $x_N=N\varepsilon_N$. The symbols that only one section uses are defined in that section, where
they first appear.

For the persistent walk, $p$, $q=1-p$ and $T=Nq$ are as above. Its end is
$E_N=p^{N-1}/2$, its crossing is $p_N$, and $z_N=N(1-p_N)$. So $x=N\varepsilon$
corresponds to $T$, and the crossing $x_N$ corresponds to $z_N$. We compare the
two crossings through the crossing equation of~\cite[Proposition~3.8]{paperA},
which says that for every $N\ge2$
\begin{equation}\label{eq:markov-crossing}
 z_N+\tfrac12\log z_N=L+c_0+\frac1{8z_N}+O(L^{-2}),\qquad
 c_0=\tfrac12\log\tfrac\pi8,
\end{equation}
where the error term is at most $\frac1{4L^2}+\frac{L^2+L}N$ in absolute
value, and that $L/2\le z_N\le L$. This is the only result of~\cite{paperA}
that we use, and we use it only to compare the two crossings in
Section~\ref{sec:erw}. The letters $p$ and $q$ have their meaning
for the persistent walk only in this introduction, and later sections reuse
them locally.

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:results}

For every even $N\ge2$ the crossing $\varepsilon_N$ exists, is unique and is
below $\frac12$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:mlr}). The center has derivative
exactly $4/(N+2)$ at $\varepsilon=0$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:first-mutation}), and
$C_N=\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}(1+O(\varepsilon\log N))$ for even $N\ge64$ and
$\varepsilon(1+\log N)\le\frac1{100}$, with explicit constants
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}). Let $g_N$ be the root of
$g+\log g=2L-\log8$. Then $x_N=g_N+O(L^2/N)$, and so
$x_N=2L-\log L-\log16+O(\log L/L)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-crossing}). Compared
with the persistent walk, $x_N=2z_N-\log(2\pi)+c_*/L+O(\log L/L^2)$ with
$c_*=\frac12\log(2\pi)-\frac14$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-markov}). We collect
the main statements.

\begin{mainthm}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For every even $N\ge2$ the elephant walk has a unique crossing
$\varepsilon_N\in(0,\frac12)$. As $N\to\infty$ over even $N$, the value
$x_N=N\varepsilon_N$ satisfies
\begin{gather*}
 x_N=2\log N-\log\log N-\log16+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\Bigr),\\
 x_N=2z_N-\log(2\pi)+\frac{c_*}{\log N}+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log N}{\log^2N}\Bigr),
\end{gather*}
where $z_N$ is the crossing value of $T$ for the persistent walk and
$c_*=\frac12\log(2\pi)-\frac14$.
\item Let $N$ be even and large, and take $\varepsilon=\varepsilon_N$. Every
mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ satisfies
$N-a^*=x_N\log x_N+\lambda_{\mathrm L}x_N+O(\sqrt{x_N\log x_N})$, where
$\lambda_{\mathrm L}\approx-0.2228$ is the mode of the Landau density. The law $P_N$ is
strictly convex on $\{2(N-a^*),\dots,2a^*-N\}$, and $P_N(a)>C_N$ for every
$1\le a\le N-1$ with $a\ne N/2$. The last statement also holds for every even
$N$ with $10\le N\le2000$.
\end{enumerate}
The thresholds for large $N$ in (b) are not explicit (Remark~\ref{rem:convex-open}). So for the
even $N$ above $2000$ that are below them, the last statement of (b) is open
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:convex}).
\end{mainthm}

Part (a) collects Proposition~\ref{prop:mlr} and
Theorems~\ref{thm:erw-crossing} and~\ref{thm:erw-markov}. Part (b) collects
Corollaries~\ref{cor:mode-location} and~\ref{cor:unique-min},
Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large} and Proposition~\ref{prop:center-small}.

We also go one order further. In the same range of $\varepsilon$ the center
is $\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}N^{2\varepsilon}(1+O(\varepsilon))$, with
explicit constants (Proposition~\ref{prop:second-order}). This gives
$x_N=g_N-(2g_NL+g_N^2/2)/(N(1+1/g_N))+O(L/N)$, so the root $g_N$ is above the
crossing for large $N$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:refined}).

The remaining results describe the whole law near the crossing. At the
crossing the end is below its neighbor for every even $N\ge8$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:dip} and Proposition~\ref{prop:dip-hyp}). For $N<1688$
this rests on a floating-point computation whose rounding error we bound
(Lemma~\ref{lem:rounding}). Let $M_N$ be the number of steps that disagree
with the first step. For $N\ge2$ and $\varepsilon\le\frac14$ the law of $M_N$
is within an explicit $\delta_B$ of a compound Poisson law, bin by bin, and
$\delta_B=O(x^2\log^2N/N)$ when $x^2\le N$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}). If
$x\to\infty$ and $x^3\log^2N/N\to0$, then $(M_N-x\log x)/x$ converges in law
to the Landau law, with a bound for each bin (Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}). So at
the crossing every mode lies at distance
$x_N\log x_N+\lambda_{\mathrm L}x_N+O(\sqrt{x_N\log x_N})$ from the nearer end, where
$\lambda_{\mathrm L}\approx-0.2228$ is the mode of the Landau density
(Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}). Farther from the ends, bin $N-m$ has
probability $\frac x{2m^2}(1+o(1))$ for $x\log^2(x+1)\ll m\ll N$, when
$x\ge1$ and $x\log^2N/N\to0$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:profile}). Finally, for large
even $N$ the center at the crossing is below every interior bin outside a
window of width of order $N^{1/2}\log N$ around it
(Theorem~\ref{thm:shape}). Inside the window we use second differences. For every
$N\ge3$ and every $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$ the signs of the second difference
of the law follow the pattern convex, concave, convex, concave, convex, where
some blocks may be empty (Theorem~\ref{thm:five-blocks}). So the law has at
most two concave stretches. For large even $N$ the law at the
crossing is strictly convex between the humps (from $2m^*$ to $N-2m^*$, with
$m^*$ the distance of a mode from the nearer end), and the center is its unique
interior minimum (Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:unique-min}). For odd $N$ we define the crossing as the
value of $\varepsilon$ at which the two central bins are as likely as each end.
The convexity also holds at this crossing for large odd $N$, because the two
central bins of an odd $N$ are the center of $N+1$ up to an explicit factor
(Proposition~\ref{prop:odd}). A certified computation gives the convexity
for $10\le N\le2000$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:convex-certified}), and with it the unique interior
minimum for every even $N$ in this range (Proposition~\ref{prop:center-small}). For
the $N$ above $2000$ that these results do not cover, the convexity is open
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:convex}).

\subsection{Prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The asymptotic behavior of the elephant walk for a fixed memory parameter is
well understood~\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019}, and
Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2022zeros} counts its returns to the origin. For
$\varepsilon\le\frac12$ the steps of the elephant walk split into clusters, since a step either
copies an earlier step exactly, with probability $1-2\varepsilon$, or starts
a new cluster with a fair sign. This is K\"ursten's coupling with bond
percolation on random recursive trees~\cite{kursten2016}, and Baur and
Bertoin~\cite{baurbertoin2016} relate the walk to P\'olya-type urns. Our
regime $N\varepsilon\asymp\log N$ lies in the strongly supercritical regime
of Baur~\cite{baur2016}, who extends the results of
Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014} for retention probability $1-t/\log n$. Baur's
Corollary~1 gives the sizes of the largest clusters other than the root's (a
Poisson limit with intensity $a^{-2}\,da$). In our regime this is the picture
of about $2x/m^2$ clusters of size $m$, since a new cluster starts with
probability $2\varepsilon$. Half of them have the sign opposite to the root,
which gives the $x/m^2$ families of size $m$ behind Theorem~\ref{thm:profile}. These results
describe cluster sizes, not the center probability, which is a large deviation
of the root cluster. Miyazaki and Takei~\cite[Theorems~2.3 and~2.4]{miyazakitakei2020} prove a
central limit theorem, with random centering and norming, and a law of the
iterated logarithm for the `laziest' minimal walk of elephant type, whose
steps are $0$ or $+1$, at every value of its memory parameter. As a
byproduct they obtain limit theorems for the size of the root cluster in
percolation on uniform random recursive trees~\cite[Section~2.3]{miyazakitakei2020}. The law that appears in the bulk
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cp} and~\ref{thm:landau}) is also known. Without its
truncation, our compound Poisson law is the Lea--Coulson form of the
Luria--Delbr\"uck distribution, and Kessler and
Levine~\cite[Eqs.~(14), (18) and~(19)]{kesslerlevine2015} describe its profile
$x/(m(m+1))$ for small $x$ and its Landau limit for large $x$ (see
also~\cite{mohle2005}). They also note that the law stops being monotone at
$\mu N=2$ and that its maximum sits at about $\mu N(\ln\mu N-0.22)$ for
large $\mu N$~\cite[Section~2]{kesslerlevine2015}, where $\mu N$ plays the
role of our $x$. These are the analogues for the mutant count of the dip at
the end (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}) and of the place of the modes
(Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}). The same stable law governs the
fluctuations of the root cluster when the retention probability is
$1-c/\log n$. This follows from a result of
Schweinsberg~\cite{schweinsberg2012}, and Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014ejp}
explains it through the growth of the tree. What we add is the comparison
of the elephant walk with this law in our regime (with an explicit error in
Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}), the dip and the modes for the walk itself rather
than for the compound Poisson law, and a certified enclosure of the Landau
mode.
Gu\'erin, Laulin, Raschel and Simon~\cite[proof of Theorem~1.1]{glrs2025}
prove that the law with a fixed first step is unimodal, and their other
results fix the memory parameter as $N$ grows. So do the local limit theorem
of Trinas and Valle~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{tv2026} for the superdiffusive walk
and their bounds on where log-concavity fails. We did not find an asymptotic
formula for the center probability when $\varepsilon\to0$ as $N$ grows in
the elephant-walk, percolation or Luria--Delbr\"uck literature that we
read. The Luria--Delbr\"uck results concern the number of mutants, which is
our $M_N$, and they do not reach the center $M_N=N/2$.

\subsection{Organization}\label{sec:organization}

Section~\ref{sec:erw} reduces the elephant walk to a counting chain, proves
that its crossing is unique, finds the crossing, and then gives the center
and the crossing to second order. Section~\ref{sec:shape} describes the whole
law near the crossing, namely the dip at each end, the compound Poisson and
Landau approximations of the bulk with the place of the modes, the tail
$x/(2m^2)$, the window around the center, and the convexity between the humps.
Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the checks. The longer proofs are in
the appendices. Appendix~\ref{app:erw} holds the proofs for
Section~\ref{sec:erw}. Appendix~\ref{app:shape} holds the proofs for the
tail in Section~\ref{sec:shape}, Appendix~\ref{app:bulk}
holds the proofs for the bulk and the place of the modes, and
Appendix~\ref{app:signs} holds the proofs for the sign pattern of the second
difference and for the rounding error of Lemma~\ref{lem:rounding}.
