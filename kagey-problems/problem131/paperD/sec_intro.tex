\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

The elephant random walk of Sch\"utz and Trimper~\cite{schutz2004} has steps
$X_1,\dots,X_N\in\{\pm1\}$ and remembers its whole past.
In~\cite{schutz2004} the first step is $+1$ with a probability that is a
parameter of the model, and we take it to be $\frac12$, so that the first
step is $+1$ or $-1$ with probability $\frac12$ each. Each later step picks
one of the earlier steps uniformly at random and repeats it, except that with
probability $\varepsilon$ it reverses it. The steps are the colors of
broadcasting on a random recursive tree~\cite{adlv2022,ahs2026}, in which each
new vertex attaches to a uniform earlier vertex, copies its color and changes
it with probability $\varepsilon$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:tree}). A reversal reverses
the sign of the sampled step, which need not be the step just before.
For $\varepsilon\le\frac12$ there is an equivalent description. A step
copies the sampled step exactly with probability $1-2\varepsilon$ and is
otherwise a fresh fair sign. The parameters of other papers translate as
follows. The memory parameter of~\cite{schutz2004} is $1-\varepsilon$, the
parameter $a$ of~\cite{glrs2025} is $1-2\varepsilon$, and the flip
probability of broadcasting is $\varepsilon$. For $\varepsilon\le\frac12$ the
retention probability of the percolation in Section~\ref{sec:prior} is also
$1-2\varepsilon$. Most limit theorems
for the walk fix $\varepsilon$ as $N\to\infty$~\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019,glrs2025,tv2026}.
For a walk with $N$ steps we write $x=N\varepsilon$. The expected number of
reversals is $(N-1)\varepsilon=x-\varepsilon$, so $x$ is this number up to an
error below one. We study the regime in which $x$ is of order $\log N$, so
that $\varepsilon\to0$ and the walk reverses only about $x$ times, and we
describe the law of its endpoint site by site.

We ask when the walk is as likely to end in the middle as at each end. Let $N$
be even, and let $A_N$ be the number of steps equal to $+1$. The walk ends at
the origin, which we call the middle, when $A_N=N/2$. It ends at $N$ or at
$-N$, which we call the ends, when $A_N=N$ or $A_N=0$. If $\varepsilon$ is
close to 0 the walk rarely reverses, so the ends win. If $\varepsilon=\frac12$
the steps are independent and fair, so the middle wins. The crossing is the
value of $\varepsilon$ in between at which the middle and each end are equally
likely. In the language of broadcasting, the walk ends in the middle when the
two colors tie.

In the persistent random walk each step repeats the previous one with
probability $p$ and switches with probability $q=1-p$. So with $q=\varepsilon$
it is the walk that copies its last step instead of a uniformly chosen one.
Its steps are the spins of the Ising chain in zero field with free ends, and
its position is the magnetization. For this Markov walk our question is part
of Problem~131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131}, which is
posed for a Galton board. As on that board, we call the values of $A_N$ bins.
Let $T=Nq$, which exceeds the expected number $(N-1)q$ of switches by $q$. The middle catches
up with the ends when $T$ is about $\log N$, and~\cite{paperA} locates this
crossing within explicit bounds for every $N\ge2$.

In this paper we find the crossing of the elephant walk. The middle needs
about twice as many reversals as the persistent walk needs switches. Namely,
the crossing value of $x$ is about $2\log N$, not $\log N$. K\"ursten's
coupling~\cite{kursten2016} splits the steps into the clusters of bond
percolation on a random recursive tree, and our regime $x\asymp\log N$ lies in
the strongly supercritical regime of this
percolation~\cite{baur2016}. The results there describe cluster
sizes, not the probability of the middle (Section~\ref{sec:prior}).

The number of steps that disagree with the first step is a known chain. With
equal switching probabilities in both directions, it is the number of
resistant cells in the reversible fluctuation assay of Bokes, Hlubinov\'a and
Singh~\cite{bhs2023}. They find its law to first order in the switching
probability by conditioning on the first switch and then running P\'olya's
urn~\cite[Eqs.~(6)--(10)]{bhs2023}. At the center this gives our first-order
coefficient $4/(N+2)$. For a first cell of random type they also approximate
the law by a mixture of two Landau densities, one at each
end~\cite[Eqs.~(58)--(60)]{bhs2023}. Their analysis uses matched asymptotics
and gives no rigorous error estimates. What we add are rigorous estimates of
the center with uniform relative errors while $x$ grows with $N$, the
expansion of the crossing, and theorems on the discrete shape of the law.

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:notation}

The walk takes $N$ steps $X_1,\dots,X_N\in\{+1,-1\}$, where
$+1$ means right. Let $A_N=\#\{i\le N:X_i=+1\}$ be the number of right steps,
and write $P_N(k)=\Prob(A_N=k)$ for the probability of bin $k$. Unless we say otherwise the first
step is fair, $\Prob(X_1=+1)=\frac12$, and the rules treat the two directions
alike, so $P_N(k)=P_N(N-k)$. We write $\Prob_+$ and $\E_+$ for probability and
expectation given $X_1=+1$. For even $N$ we put $h=N/2$ and call
$C_N=P_N(h)$ the center and $E_N=P_N(N)=P_N(0)$ the end. Whenever $C_N$
appears, $N$ is even. The crossing is the value of the parameter at which
$C_N=E_N$. All logarithms are natural, $L=\log N$,
$H_k=\sum_{j\le k}1/j$ is the $k$-th harmonic number, and $\gamma$ is Euler's
constant. Limits, $O(\cdot)$ and $o(\cdot)$ refer to $N\to\infty$, over even
$N$ when $C_N$ appears. The constants implied by $O(\cdot)$ are absolute
unless we name what they depend on.

The elephant walk (Definition~\ref{def:erw}) has the reversal probability
$\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and $x=N\varepsilon$. Its crossing is $\varepsilon_N$,
and $x_N=N\varepsilon_N$. The symbols that only one section uses are defined in that section, where
they first appear.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:results}

Our first theorem gives the center to first order with explicit bounds, and
with it the crossing.

\begin{theorem}[the center and the crossing]\label{thm:main-center}
Let $N\ge2$ be even.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The ratio $C_N/E_N$ is strictly increasing in $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$, and
the crossing $\varepsilon_N$ exists, is unique and lies in $(0,\frac12)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mlr}).
\item For $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$ we have
$C_N\ge\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}(1-2\varepsilon)$. If
$\varepsilon(1+\log N)\le\frac1{100}$, then
$C_N\le\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}\,N^{2\varepsilon}e^{5200\varepsilon}$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}, whose constant $5200$ uses an enclosure in ball
arithmetic).
\item As $N\to\infty$ over even $N$, the crossing satisfies
$x_N+\log x_N=2\log N-\log8+O(\log^2N/N)$, and so
\[
 x_N=2\log N-\log\log N-\log16+O\Bigl(\frac{\log\log N}{\log N}\Bigr)
\]
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-crossing}).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

So $C_N=\frac{4\varepsilon}{N+2}(1+O(\varepsilon\log N))$ when
$\varepsilon\log N$ is small, and the error factor is $1+O(\log^2N/N)$ at the
crossing. The persistent walk has its crossing at $T$ close to $\log N$, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-markov} compares the two crossings to the next order.

For the rest of the law let $M_N$ be the number of steps that disagree with
the first step. Its law $f_N(m)=\Prob(M_N=m)$ does not depend on $X_1$, and
$P_N(k)=\frac12[f_N(N-k)+f_N(k)]$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:erw-reduction}). Let
$\CP_N$ be the law of $\sum_{d=1}^{N-1}d\,\mathcal N_d$, where the
$\mathcal N_d$ are independent Poisson variables with means $x/(d(d+1))$.
Put $\lambda_m=(m-x\log x)/x$, and let
\[
 f_L(\lambda)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-i\lambda t}e^{B(t)}\,dt,
 \qquad B(t)=-it\log\lvert t\rvert-\frac\pi2\lvert t\rvert,
\]
be the Landau density.

\begin{theorem}[the bulk]\label{thm:main-bulk}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For $N\ge2$ and $0<\varepsilon\le\frac14$,
\[
 \sup_{m\ge0}\bigl\lvert f_N(m)-\CP_N(m)\bigr\rvert\le\delta_B
 :=\frac{x^2\log N}{N}+\frac{4x^2}{N}e^{4x^2/N}+\frac{4x^2(H_N^2+H_N)}{N}
\]
(Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}).
\item There is an absolute constant $C$ such that if $N\ge64$,
$0<\varepsilon\le\frac14$ and $x\ge1$, then
$\bigl\lvert xf_N(m)-f_L(\lambda_m)\bigr\rvert\le
C\bigl(\frac{1+\log x}x+\frac xN\bigr)+x\delta_B$ for every integer $m\ge0$.
If moreover $x\to\infty$ and $x^3\log^2N/N\to0$, then $(M_N-x\log x)/x$
converges in law to the law with density $f_L$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}).
We do not compute $C$.
\item The density $f_L$ has exactly one mode $\lambda_{\mathrm L}$, and
$-0.22278299<\lambda_{\mathrm L}<-0.22278297$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode},
computer-assisted). As $N\to\infty$ over even $N$, at the crossing, with
$x=x_N$, every mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ satisfies
\[
 N-a^*=x\log x+\lambda_{\mathrm L}x+O\bigl(\sqrt{x\log x}\bigr),
\]
and the largest mode $m_h$ of $f_N$ satisfies the same with $m_h$ in place
of $N-a^*$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}(b)). We do not compute the implied constant.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

So at the crossing the law of $A_N$ has a hump near each end, about
$x\log x-0.2228\,x$ bins from it. The final position is $2A_N-N$, so in
physical distance each hump is twice as far from $\pm N$. For the shape of the law between
the humps let $D_N(a)=P_N(a+1)-2P_N(a)+P_N(a-1)$ be its second difference,
and for a mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ put $m^*=N-a^*$.

\begin{theorem}[the shape]\label{thm:main-shape}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item Let $N\ge3$ and $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$. Delete the zeros from the list
$D_N(1),\dots,D_N(N-1)$, replace each entry by its sign, and merge equal
neighbors. The result is a subsequence of $(+,-,+,-,+)$. In particular the
second difference changes sign at most four times
(Theorem~\ref{thm:five-blocks}).
\item For every even $N\ge8$, at the crossing, $E_N=C_N<P_N(N-1)$, so $P_N$
is not unimodal (Proposition~\ref{prop:dip-hyp}, computer-assisted through a
certified enclosure of $\varepsilon_N$ for $N<1688$ and through the constant
$5200$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}(b) for $N\ge1688$). By contrast $f_N$ is unimodal for every $N$ and every
$\varepsilon$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:unimodal}, from~\cite{glrs2025}).
\item For every large even $N$, at the crossing, the following hold, with
thresholds that we do not compute. For every mode $a^*\ge N/2$ of $P_N$ we
have $D_N(a)>0$ for $2m^*+1\le a\le N-2m^*-1$, so $P_N$ is strictly convex on
$\{2m^*,\dots,N-2m^*\}$. Also $P_N(a)>C_N$ for every $1\le a\le N-1$ with
$a\ne h$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large} and
Corollary~\ref{cor:unique-min}). Both conclusions rest on the computer-assisted
Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode}.
\item The two conclusions of (c) hold for every even $N$ with
$10\le N\le2000$ (Propositions~\ref{prop:convex-certified}
and~\ref{prop:center-small}, computer-assisted).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For the even $N$ above $2000$ and below the thresholds of (c) both
conclusions are open (Conjecture~\ref{conj:convex} and
Remark~\ref{rem:convex-open}). The second would follow from the first by
Lemma~\ref{lem:center-convex} whenever each mode lies within $(N-2)/4$ of the
nearer end.

Three inputs to the proofs rest on computation, and Section~\ref{sec:verify}
describes them. The first is an enclosure in ball arithmetic of a sum of 60
positive terms, which gives the constant $5200$ in
Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}(b). This constant gives the thresholds in
Propositions~\ref{prop:dip-hyp} and~\ref{prop:odd}(c) and the explicit
second-order bound in Online Resource~1. The asymptotic statements need only
that the series converges. The second is the enclosure of the mode of the
Landau density in Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode}. It is used in
Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}, Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large},
Corollary~\ref{cor:unique-min} and Proposition~\ref{prop:odd}(d). The third
is a certified enclosure of $\varepsilon_N$ and of the law at the crossing
for $10\le N\le2000$. It proves Propositions~\ref{prop:convex-certified}
and~\ref{prop:center-small}. For even $N$ the enclosure of $\varepsilon_N$
also gives Proposition~\ref{prop:dip-hyp} for $10\le N<1688$. Every other
statement is proved by hand, apart from short exact computations in rational
arithmetic, and no proof uses the tables or the figures.

\paragraph{Why the elephant needs twice as many reversals.}
The following argument is heuristic, and Sections~\ref{sec:erw} and~\ref{sec:shape} make it precise.
The ends are easy. The walk ends in the right end bin exactly when its first
step goes right and it never changes direction. This has probability
$\frac12(1-\varepsilon)^{N-1}\approx\frac12e^{-x}$ for the elephant walk, and
the same with $q$ in place of $\varepsilon$ for the persistent walk. So only the
middle differs. In the persistent walk the $T$ switches are spread over the whole
walk, and each switch moves the final position by about one run length $N/T$. So the
number of right steps spreads over about $N/\sqrt T$ bins, the middle bin has
probability of order $\sqrt T/N$, and the ends lose when $e^{-T}$ falls to
about $1/N$, that is, when $T\approx\log N$.

The elephant walk spreads a reversal in a different way. A reversal at step
$t+1$ is copied by later steps, those copies are copied, and so on. The steps
that descend from it form a family, whose share of the walk behaves like the
share of one ball in P\'olya's urn started with one ball against $t$. So a late
reversal has a small family and hardly moves the walk.
To end exactly in the middle, the walk usually needs a family that fills
about half the walk. To first order in $\varepsilon$, a walk that ends in the
middle reverses once, and this reversal happens among the first few steps. (A walk at
the crossing reverses about $x\approx2\log N$ times in all, and most of these
reversals are late and have small families.) An early reversal has probability
of order $\varepsilon$, and hitting the exact middle brings another factor of
order $1/N$. So the middle bin has probability of order $\varepsilon/N=x/N^2$ (in
fact about $4x/N^2$), and the ends lose only when $e^{-x}$ falls to about
$1/N^2$, that is, when $x\approx2\log N$. More precisely, at the crossing of
the persistent walk $\frac12e^{-T}\approx\sqrt{2T/\pi}/N$, and squaring this relation turns it
into the elephant relation $\frac12e^{-x}\approx4x/N^2$ with
$x\approx2T-\log(2\pi)$.

The small families also give the shape of the law. About $x/m^2$ reversals
have a family of $m$ steps, so the bin at distance $m$ from an end has
probability about $x/(2m^2)$ over a wide range of $m$. So the law has a hump
near each end, and the middle is where the two tails meet. Indeed, twice
$x/(2m^2)$ at $m=N/2$ is the $4x/N^2$ above.

\paragraph{Further results.}
Section~\ref{sec:erw} also shows that $C_N$ is a polynomial in $\varepsilon$
with $C_N'(0)=4/(N+2)$ exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:first-mutation}), and it
compares $x_N$ with the crossing $z_N$ of the persistent walk
(Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-markov}, which uses~\cite[Prop.~3.8]{paperA}).
Section~\ref{sec:shape} bounds $P_N(N-1)/E_N$ from both sides for every
$N\ge3$ and $0<\varepsilon\le\frac12$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}), and it gives
the place of the modes with explicit constants apart from the constant $C$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main-bulk}(b) (Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}(a)). For
every $N\ge5$ and $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$, once $D_N$ turns positive to the
right of a negative value, it stays positive up to the mirror point
(Theorem~\ref{thm:no-holes}). So at the crossing for even $N\ge8$, and
whenever $P_N(0)<P_N(1)$, one point of convexity gives convexity between the
humps (Corollary~\ref{cor:one-point}). For odd $N$ the two central bins are
$(1-\frac{1-2\varepsilon}N)^{-1}C_{N+1}$, the crossing behaves as for even
$N$, and the law at the crossing is convex between the humps for large $N$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:odd}).

\subsection{Prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The asymptotic behavior of the elephant walk for a fixed memory parameter is
well understood~\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019}, and its returns
to the origin are counted in~\cite{bertoin2022zeros,fang2024}. The coin-turning walk of Engl\"ander and
Volkov~\cite{ev2018,evw2020} is the persistent walk with a switch probability
$p_n$ that depends on the time $n$. When the switch probability is
eventually $a/n$, that is, $p_n=a/n$ for $n\ge n_0$, the frequency of heads
tends to the Beta$(a,a)$ law~\cite[Section~4, Theorem~1]{ev2018}, which is
U-shaped for $a<1$ and humped for $a>1$, so in that limit the ends and the
middle trade places at $a=1$. Our question is a finite-$N$ version of this
comparison for the elephant walk, whose reversal probability depends on $N$
and not on the time. C\'enac, Le~Ny, de~Loynes and
Offret~\cite{clnlo2018,clnlo2019} study persistent walks with a variable
memory.

For
$\varepsilon\le\frac12$ the steps of the elephant walk split into clusters, since a step either
copies an earlier step exactly, with probability $1-2\varepsilon$, or starts
a new cluster with a fair sign. This is K\"ursten's coupling with bond
percolation on random recursive trees~\cite{kursten2016}, and Baur and
Bertoin~\cite{baurbertoin2016} relate the walk to P\'olya-type urns. Our
regime $N\varepsilon\asymp\log N$ lies in the strongly supercritical regime
of Baur~\cite{baur2016}, who extends the results of
Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014} for retention probability $1-t/\log n$. Baur's
Corollary~1 gives the sizes of the largest clusters other than the root's (a
Poisson limit with intensity $a^{-2}\,da$). Heuristically, in our regime this
suggests the picture of about $2x/m^2$ clusters of size $m$, since a new cluster starts with
probability $2\varepsilon$. Half of them have the sign opposite to the root,
which gives the $x/m^2$ families of size $m$ behind the tail of the law. These results
describe cluster sizes, not the center probability, which is a large deviation
of the root cluster. Miyazaki and Takei~\cite[Theorems~2.3 and~2.4]{miyazakitakei2020} prove limit
theorems for the `laziest' minimal walk of elephant type at every value of its
memory parameter, and with them for the size of the root cluster in
percolation on uniform random recursive trees~\cite[Section~2.3]{miyazakitakei2020}. The law that appears in the bulk
(Theorems~\ref{thm:cp} and~\ref{thm:landau}) is also known. Without its
truncation, our compound Poisson law is the Lea--Coulson form of the
Luria--Delbr\"uck distribution, and Kessler and
Levine~\cite[Eqs.~(14), (18) and~(19)]{kesslerlevine2015} describe its profile
$x/(m(m+1))$ for small $x$ and its Landau limit for large $x$.
M\"ohle~\cite[Theorem~4.1 and Corollary~4.1]{mohle2005} proves this stable
limit and gives integral formulas for its density, and his
Corollary~4.2 is a local limit theorem for the untruncated law, uniform on
the lattice, as the parameter tends to infinity through the integers. It has
no rate. Kessler and Levine also note that the law stops being monotone at
$\mu N=2$ and that its maximum sits at about $\mu N(\log\mu N-0.22)$ for
large $\mu N$~\cite[Section~2]{kesslerlevine2015}, where $\mu N$ plays the
role of our $x$. These are the analogues for the mutant count of the dip at
the end (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}) and of the place of the modes
(Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}). The same stable law governs the
fluctuations of the root cluster when the retention probability is
$1-c/\log n$. This follows from a result of
Schweinsberg~\cite{schweinsberg2012}, and Bertoin~\cite{bertoin2014ejp}
explains it through the growth of the tree. What we add is the truncation at
$d=N-1$, a rate in the local limit, the transfer from this law to the
elephant walk in our regime (with an explicit error in
Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}), the dip and the modes for the walk itself rather
than for the compound Poisson law, and a certified enclosure of the Landau
mode.

The same model is studied as broadcasting on random recursive trees. Addario-Berry, Devroye, Lugosi and Velona~\cite{adlv2022} and Althaus,
Hartung and Steiner~\cite{ahs2026} study how well the color of the root can be
recovered from the colors at time $N$, with the flip probability fixed as
$N\to\infty$. In that language $N-2M_N$ is the number of vertices with the
color of the root minus the number with the other color, and the center $C_N$
is the probability of a tie, which enters the error of the majority vote.
Desmarais, Holmgren and Wagner~\cite{dhw2023} prove limit laws for the color
counts of broadcasting on linear preferential attachment trees, a class that
contains the random recursive tree, and Kardas, Shneer and
Wachtel~\cite{ksw2026} give martingale methods and error bounds, again with a
fixed flip probability.

Gu\'erin, Laulin, Raschel and Simon~\cite[Section~2, proof of Theorem~1.1]{glrs2025}
prove that the law with a fixed first step is unimodal, and their other
results fix the memory parameter as $N$ grows. So do the local limit theorem
of Trinas and Valle~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{tv2026} for the superdiffusive walk
and their bounds on where log-concavity fails. The work of~\cite{glrs2025}
builds on the fixed-point equation of Gu\'erin, Laulin and
Raschel~\cite{glr2026} for the superdiffusive limit. Moments of the limit
and of the position are computed in~\cite{bercu2018,bcr2019,kissveto2022},
and da Silva et al.~\cite{dasilva2013} give numerical and Fokker--Planck evidence that the
propagator is not Gaussian in the superdiffusive regime. Roy, Takei and Tanemura~\cite{rtt2025} keep the
memory parameter fixed and let the number of remembered steps grow with $N$
in a triangular array. Gut and Stadtm\"uller~\cite[(1.5)]{gs2026} study a
walk with steps in $\{0,1\}$ whose first step is $1$ with probability $p$ and
whose later steps copy a uniform earlier step with probability $p$ and are $0$
otherwise. Given $k$ ones among the first $n$ steps, the next step is $1$ with
probability $pk/n$, while for our count $A_N$ the corresponding probability
is $\varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)k/n$. So their count cannot leave $0$ and
ours can, and their walk does not give our count $A_N$.

Kessler and Levine~\cite[Eqs.~(10), (13) and~(14)]{kesslerlevine2013} solve
the irreversible mutation chain with a fixed final population to first order
in the mutation probability, and their outer solution covers numbers of
mutants of order $N$, including $N/2$. Their chain differs from $M$ for
$\varepsilon>0$, since a mutant never reverts, but the two laws have the same
first derivative in $\varepsilon$ at $\varepsilon=0$. The first-order formula
of~\cite{bhs2023} for the reversible chain also covers every $m$. These
formulas are first-order or matched asymptotic predictions, without error
bounds when $N\varepsilon$ grows. We did not find, in the elephant-walk,
broadcasting, percolation or Luria--Delbr\"uck literature that we read, a
rigorous estimate of the center with a uniform relative error when
$N\varepsilon$ is of order $\log N$. The limiting results in the broadcasting
papers fix the flip probability.

\subsection{Organization}\label{sec:organization}

Section~\ref{sec:erw} proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main-center}, with the bounds
on the center proved in Appendix~\ref{app:erw}, and compares the crossing
with that of the persistent walk. Section~\ref{sec:shape} proves
Theorems~\ref{thm:main-bulk} and~\ref{thm:main-shape}. The bulk is proved in
Appendix~\ref{app:bulk}, which uses the bound on the mode of $f_N$ from
Appendix~\ref{app:shape}, and the sign pattern of the second difference in
Appendix~\ref{app:signs}. For Theorem~\ref{thm:main-shape}, part (b) uses the
dip at the end (Theorem~\ref{thm:dip}), Proposition~\ref{prop:mlr},
Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-bounds}(b) and, for $N<1688$, the certified enclosure.
Part (c) uses Theorems~\ref{thm:five-blocks} and~\ref{thm:no-holes}, which
reduce the convexity to one inequality (Corollary~\ref{cor:one-point}), the
Landau limit, which gives that inequality for large $N$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:convex-large}), and Lemma~\ref{lem:center-convex} for the
center. Part (d) uses the certified enclosure with Theorem~\ref{thm:no-holes}
and Lemma~\ref{lem:center-convex}. Section~\ref{sec:verify} describes the
computations. Online Resource~1 holds the center and the crossing to second
order, the tail $P_N(N-m)\sim x/(2m^2)$ beyond the humps, and part of the
proof for odd $N$. None of Theorems~\ref{thm:main-center}
to~\ref{thm:main-shape} uses it.
