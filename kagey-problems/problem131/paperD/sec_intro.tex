\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}
\begin{quote}\small\itshape
An elephant and an ant are playing hide and seek. The elephant counts while
the ant hides among many identical anthills, then opens its eyes and knows
at once which one the ant is in. The ant's shoes are outside the door.
\end{quote}
\hfill\textup{(Anonymous)}
\medskip
\end{minipage}

Fix the first step of a superdiffusive elephant random walk.
Gu\'erin, Laulin, Raschel and Simon prove that its limit density is
unimodal~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{glrs2025}. Randomizing the first step mixes
this density with its reflection. The mixture can have two peaks,
although each component has one. They ask which biases give two,
conjecturing an open interval
centered at one half~\cite[Remark~2.1(b)]{glrs2025}.
We prove their conjecture and locate the unique memory threshold
where two peaks first appear under a fair start. A related conjecture,
eventual log-concavity, is disproved by Trinas and Valle in a recent
preprint~\cite{tv2026}.

Gu\'erin, Laulin, Raschel and Simon prove conditional unimodality
by induction in the finite walk and then pass to its limit.
We need an induction that allows two peaks and an arbitrary start.
We carry the boundary histories in augmented rows, so the interior
sign argument also controls signs entering at the ends. Restricting
these rows to the half favored by the first step excludes a valley
there. Lattice-window averages then transfer both shape bounds under
weak convergence (Lemma~\ref{lem:weak-shape-transfer}).
For curvature, Gaussian smoothing reduces the problem to Descartes'
rule for exponential sums. The rule is classical. The contribution
is the boundary and dominant-half construction that makes it apply
to the biased walk.

\subsection{The walk and its parameters}\label{sec:notation}

The elephant walk~\cite{schutz2004} takes steps
$X_1,\dots,X_N\in\{+1,-1\}$, with $+1$ meaning right.
At each step it chooses uniformly from its earlier steps and repeats
the chosen direction, except with probability $\varepsilon$, when it
goes the other way. Write $U_t$ for the step chosen at time $t+1$,
and call it the parent of that step. We choose all parents and all
reversal coins independently, also independently of $X_1$.

Put $A_N=\#\{i\le N:X_i=+1\}$ and $P_N(k)=\Prob(A_N=k)$.
The position is $S_N=2A_N-N$. We take a fair first step unless
another bias is specified, so $P_N(k)=P_N(N-k)$.
The notation $\Prob_+$ and $\E_+$ conditions on $X_1=1$.
For even $N$, write $k_{\mathrm c}=N/2$. All logarithms are natural.

For fixed memory put $a=1-2\varepsilon\in(1/2,1)$ and
condition on $X_1=1$. The almost-sure limit of
$S_N/N^a$ has density $h_a$. With $\Prob(X_1=1)=q$, its density is
\[
 g_{a,q}(u)=q h_a(u)+(1-q)h_a(-u),\qquad g_a=g_{a,1/2}.
\]
The copying probability in the original notation is $p=(1+a)/2$.
In the finite recurrences we use $r_t(k)=\varepsilon+(1-2\varepsilon)k/t$.

\subsection{Fixed memory}
\label{sec:results}

\begin{theorem}[the bias interval, analytic]\label{thm:main-limit}
For every $a\in(1/2,1)$ there is $b_a\in(0,1/2]$ such that $g_{a,q}$
has exactly two local maxima if and only if $b_a<q<1-b_a$.
Outside this interval, including its endpoints, it is unimodal with
a unique local maximum. For a fair start the density has exactly
two inflection points when unimodal and four when bimodal.
For $a\ge79/100$ it is bimodal. On the open set where $b_a<1/2$,
the boundary is continuously differentiable, and $b_a\to0$ as $a\uparrow1$.
\end{theorem}

Theorems~\ref{thm:two-modes} and~\ref{thm:biased-limit-modes} prove
the shape assertions, and Corollary~\ref{cor:analytic-range} gives
the moment criterion. The interior argument in
Theorem~\ref{thm:bias-continuity} proves positivity and regularity
where the interval is nonempty. Corollary~\ref{cor:bias-boundary-rate}
gives the limit at one. These arguments use no numerical certificate.

\begin{theorem}[the transition, computer-assisted]\label{thm:main-threshold}
There is a unique threshold $a_*$ such that the bias interval is
empty exactly when $a\le a_*$. Numerically, $a_*\approx0.6688$.
At $a_*$ the fair density has a quartic top. Its two modes emerge
at distance proportional to $\sqrt{a-a_*}$, and
\[
 \tfrac12-b_a\sim Q_*(a-a_*)^{3/2},
 \qquad Q_*\approx9.86698769.
\]
The interval strictly widens immediately above $a_*$.
\end{theorem}

The certified enclosure is in Appendix~\ref{app:threshold}.
The certificate establishes the signs and derivative bounds in
Theorem~\ref{thm:threshold} and Table~\ref{tab:critical-constants}.
The quartic and three-halves laws then follow by Taylor expansion
(Propositions~\ref{prop:critical-shape} and~\ref{prop:bias-cusp}).
Theorem~\ref{thm:bias-continuity} gives a continuous boundary,
continuously differentiable and positive on $(a_*,1)$, and a unique
optimizer in its variational formula. Continuity at $a_*$ uses the
certified critical expansion. We do not prove that the boundary
decreases throughout $(a_*,1)$.

\begin{theorem}[the finite shape bounds]\label{thm:shape-engine}
For every $N\ge1$, every $0<\varepsilon<1$ and every initial bias,
the count law has at most two modal plateaus, including its endpoints.
If $q\ge1/2$, it dominates its reflection on the right half and has
no strict valley there. For a fair start, $N\ge3$ and
$0<\varepsilon<1/2$, the nonzero signs of
\[
 D_N(k)=P_N(k+1)-2P_N(k)+P_N(k-1),\qquad 1\le k<N,
\]
after equal neighbors are merged, form a subsequence of $(+,-,+,-,+)$.
\end{theorem}

The proofs are Proposition~\ref{prop:all-bias-modes},
Proposition~\ref{prop:dominant-half} and Theorem~\ref{thm:five-blocks}.
Weak convergence is enough to carry the valley bounds to a continuous
limit (Lemma~\ref{lem:weak-shape-transfer}). For the curvature bound,
we first smooth by a Gaussian and then apply the sign bound.

\subsection{Proof overview}\label{sec:architecture}

\paragraph{Signs at the ends.}
The recurrence alone is not enough at the ends. We append the boundary
end values to each row, so that the induction keeps track of where a
new sign could enter. In this enlarged row, an entry is either copied
or is a positive sum of neighbors from the preceding row. We can
therefore bound the signs of the entire row. For first differences
this gives at most two modal plateaus. For second differences under
a fair start it gives at most five sign blocks.

\paragraph{The half favored by the first step.}
Take $q\ge1/2$. The right half has no valley and dominates its
reflection. A second peak therefore requires a decrease on the left,
and this is also sufficient. Fix two points on the left and compare
their values. The right value minus the left is affine in the bias
and nonnegative at $q=1$, by conditional unimodality. Once negative,
it stays negative as the bias moves toward one half. Taking all such
pairs gives an interval, and reflection gives its other half.
Analyticity excludes flat pieces. We later use the two-mode bound
again, this time to control the boundary ratio and prove continuity.

\paragraph{Passing to the limit.}
If the limit density had a forbidden valley, average over three short
intervals around its high and low points. The inequalities remain
strict, so weak convergence gives them for the finite law too.
The smallest atom in the middle interval and the largest in each
outer interval then give a forbidden finite valley.
For two valleys we use five intervals instead
(Lemma~\ref{lem:weak-shape-transfer}).
For curvature we first smooth the second differences with a Gaussian.
Descartes' rule controls their signs. Summation by parts then gives
the limit for a fixed Gaussian width, after which we let that width
tend to zero.

\paragraph{Locating the threshold.}
We integrate the moment series for $g_a''(0)$ and bound its Fourier
tail through the smoothing equation. Taylor models in memory retain
the cancellations lost by direct interval evaluation. Near the
zero, a positive parameter derivative makes the transition unique,
while a negative fourth spatial derivative gives the quartic top.
Table~\ref{tab:critical-constants} records the certified constants.
Appendices~\ref{app:threshold} and~\ref{or:threshold} prove every
estimate used in the certificate.

Section~\ref{sec:convex} gives the finite bounds and
Section~\ref{sec:bulk-applications} their limiting consequences.
Sections~\ref{sec:verify} and~\ref{sec:open} record verification scope
and open questions. Longer proofs are in the appendices.

\subsection{Prior work}\label{sec:prior}

The counting chain predates the elephant walk. In the formulation
of James, James and Qi~\cite[p.~2339]{jamesjamesqi2008}, the
Drezner--Farnum model~\cite{dreznerfarnum1993} has next-success
probability $(1-\theta)p+\theta k/t$.
Taking $p=1/2$ and $\theta=a$ gives our transition rule.
Drezner and Farnum's family includes bimodal laws.
Heyde~\cite{heyde2004} established the fixed-parameter limit
regimes, and James, James and Qi treat time-varying dependence.
We classify limiting shapes as the initial bias and memory vary.

Fixed-memory results include
\cite{coletti2017,bercu2018,kubotatakei2019}. Murahara and
Tokumitsu~\cite{muraharatokumitsu2026} give optimal Gaussian local and central limit rates for fixed copying
probabilities at most $3/4$. Vishwakarma~\cite{vishwakarma2026} studies moment recurrences in the three
regimes. We use the density and moments
of~\cite[Theorems~1.3 and~1.4]{glr2026} and conditional unimodality
from~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{glrs2025}. The local limit in Trinas and Valle's
preprint~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{tv2026} could replace our weak-transfer
argument. Their Theorem~4.4 disproves the log-concavity conjecture
of~\cite[Remark~2.3]{glrs2025}.

The same transition appears in the growing-network model of
Mohandas et al.~\cite{mohandas2025}. They observe the symmetric
bimodality transition and extrapolate its finite-length central
curvatures to a copying probability near $0.836$
(Supplemental Material, Section~IV.A).
Our certified threshold is $(1+a_*)/2\approx0.83440643$ in their
parameter. The slow finite-length approach is visible in
Remark~\ref{rem:modes-threshold}.

The bias question also belongs to the classical study of modality
in mixtures. Eisenberger~\cite{eisenberger1964} studies how the
mixing weight affects bimodality for two normal components, and
Robertson and Fryer~\cite{robertsonfryer1969} examine their shape
across parameter space. Here the two components are a non-Gaussian
limit density and its reflection. We prove that their bimodal
weights form an interval by controlling the finite walk, without
an explicit formula for the conditional density.

Dekking and Kong~\cite[Theorem~4.1]{dekkingkong2011} classify the Markov
binomial law as unimodal, bimodal or trimodal. It depends on the previous
state, so it differs from the walk here.
Trinas~\cite{trinas2026return} studies last returns to zero at fixed
superdiffusive memory. His leading constant involves $g_{a,q}(0)$, which
is independent of $q$ by reflection. The bias changes the shape away
from zero even though it leaves that value unchanged.

The sign arguments use classical variation diminution, as developed
in Karlin's work~\cite{karlin1964,karlin1968}.
For Gaussian smoothing we use the generalized Descartes rule from
P\'olya and Szeg\H{o}~\cite[Part~V, Chapter~1]{polyaszego1998}.
We include the elementary forms needed here. The walk-specific step
is to control the boundary histories, where the positive interior
recurrence alone gives no sign bound.
