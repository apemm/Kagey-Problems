\section{Proofs for the bulk}\label{app:bulk}

We prove Theorems~\ref{thm:cp} and~\ref{thm:landau},
Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode} and Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}. We keep
the notation of Section~\ref{sec:shape} and of Appendix~\ref{app:shape}. So
$\mathcal T$ is the tree of Lemma~\ref{lem:tree}, $\mathcal T_v$ is the family
of $v$, and $\mu_v$ is the mark of $v$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:urn} and the
discussion after Lemma~\ref{lem:tree}, $\lvert\mathcal T_v\rvert$ has the law
$\beta_v$, with mean $N/v$, and $\sum_{v=2}^N\beta_v(d)=N/(d(d+1))$ for
$1\le d\le N-1$. Let
\[
 \Psi_N(\theta)=\sum_{d=1}^{N-1}\frac{e^{i\theta d}-1}{d(d+1)},\qquad
 \Psi_\infty(\theta)=\sum_{d\ge1}\frac{e^{i\theta d}-1}{d(d+1)} .
\]
The law $\CP_N$ has the characteristic function
$\widehat{\CP}_N(\theta)=e^{x\Psi_N(\theta)}$.

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:cp}}\label{app:bulk-cp}

We use $\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)=\frac12\sum_k|P(k)-Q(k)|$.
The case $N=1$ is immediate. Assume $N\ge2$ and $0<\varepsilon<1$.
Put $Z_+=\sum_{v=2}^N\mu_v|\mathcal T_v|$. Unless two marked vertices
are strictly nested, $M_N=Z_+$. The family means therefore give
\[
 \Prob(M_N\ne Z_+)\le\varepsilon^2\sum_{v=2}^N(N/v-1)
 =\varepsilon^2(NH_N-2N+1).
\]
Given the tree, replace the independent Bernoulli marks by independent
Poisson variables of mean $\varepsilon$. The scalar total variation is
$\varepsilon(1-e^{-\varepsilon})\le\varepsilon^2$, since the only positive
Bernoulli-minus-Poisson mass is at $1$. Product coupling and the weighted
sum cost at most $(N-1)\varepsilon^2$. The resulting conditional law is
$\operatorname{CP}(\Lambda)$, where
$\Lambda=\varepsilon\sum_{v=2}^N\delta_{|\mathcal T_v|}$ has deterministic
mass $\lambda=(N-1)\varepsilon$. By the family identity,
$\E\Lambda\{d\}=x/[d(d+1)]$, so $\operatorname{CP}(\E\Lambda)=\CP_N$.

Use the L1 norm on signed measures and convolution $*$, for which
$\|\sigma*\tau\|_1\le\|\sigma\|_1\|\tau\|_1$. On intensities of mass
$\lambda$, the CP map and its derivatives in a zero-mass direction $D$ are
\[
 F(\nu)=e^{-\lambda}\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{\nu^{*r}}{r!},\qquad
 F'(\nu)[D]=F(\nu)*D,\qquad F''(\nu)[D,D]=F(\nu)*D*D,
\]
where $\nu^{*0}=\delta_0$. The series and derivatives converge in L1.
Let $\mathcal F_j=\sigma(U_1,\ldots,U_j)$,
$\Lambda_j=\E(\Lambda\mid\mathcal F_j)$ and
$D_j=\Lambda_j-\Lambda_{j-1}$. Each $\Lambda_j$ is nonnegative of mass
$\lambda$. Along the segment between consecutive intensities $F$ has norm
$1$, so Taylor's remainder has norm at most $\|D_j\|_1^2/2$.
The linear term has conditional mean zero given $\mathcal F_{j-1}$.
Telescoping expectations and dividing by two gives
\[
 \operatorname{TV}(\E F(\Lambda),F(\E\Lambda))
 \le\frac14\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\E\|D_j\|_1^2.
\]
If $\Lambda^{(j)}$ resamples only $U_j$, independence gives
$D_j=\E(\Lambda-\Lambda^{(j)}\mid\mathcal F_j)$.
Conditional Jensen thus bounds its squared norm in expectation by that of
$\Lambda-\Lambda^{(j)}$. Moving the subtree of $j+1$ changes family sizes
only along the old and new parent paths, excluding the root. Each altered
atom contributes at most $2\varepsilon$ in L1. Writing $\mathrm{dp}$ for
depth, and using $(u+v)^2\le2(u^2+v^2)$, gives
\[
 \E\|\Lambda-\Lambda^{(j)}\|_1^2
 \le16\varepsilon^2\E\mathrm{dp}(U_j)^2
 \le16\varepsilon^2(H_{j-1}^2+H_{j-1}).
\]
For the last inequality, the depth pgf satisfies $g_1(s)=1$ and
$j g_{j+1}(s)=s\sum_{v\le j}g_v(s)$. Subtraction gives
$g_v(s)=\prod_{i=1}^{v-1}(i-1+s)/i$. Thus the depth has the law of a sum
of independent Bernoulli$(1/i)$ variables, and its second moment is at
most $H_{v-1}^2+H_{v-1}$. Average over the independent uniform parent
$U_j\in\{1,\ldots,j\}$.

Adding the three errors proves the stronger bound
\[
 \operatorname{TV}(\mathcal L(M_N),\CP_N)
 \le\varepsilon^2\left\{NH_N-N+
       4\sum_{j=0}^{N-2}(H_j^2+H_j)\right\}
 \le\delta_\Sigma.
\]
Finally $H_N\le1+\log N$ and $H_j\le H_N$ give
$\delta_\Sigma\le(x^2/N)\{\log N+2+4(H_N^2+H_N)\}\le\delta_B$.
Every singleton discrepancy is at most total variation. \qed

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}}\label{app:bulk-landau}

We write $\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)=-it\log\lvert t\rvert-\frac\pi2\lvert t\rvert$ for real
$t\ne0$, and $\Psi_{\mathrm L}(0)=0$. For $t>0$ this is the value of $u\log u$ at $u=-it$,
with the principal logarithm, and $\Psi_{\mathrm L}(-t)=\overline{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}$. We need three
facts about $\Psi_N$ and $\Psi_\infty$.

\emph{Fact 1.} For real $\theta$,
$\lvert\Psi_N(\theta)-\Psi_\infty(\theta)\rvert\le\sum_{d\ge N}\frac2{d(d+1)}=\frac2N$.

\emph{Fact 2.} For $0<\lvert\theta\rvert\le\pi$ we have
$\Psi_\infty(\theta)=-i\theta\log(-i\theta)+R(\theta)$ with
$\lvert R(\theta)\rvert\le\theta^2\bigl(0.631\,\lvert\log\lvert\theta\rvert\rvert+1.53\bigr)$.
The reason is that $\Psi_\infty$ has a closed form in which $\theta$
enters only through $1-e^{i\theta}$, and $1-e^{i\theta}$ is close to
$-i\theta$ for small $\theta$. The term $R$ is what this replacement
changes. We prove it in the next four paragraphs.

We start with the closed form. For $0<\lvert s\rvert\le1$, $s\ne1$,
\[
 \sum_{d\ge1}\frac{s^d-1}{d(d+1)}=\chi(s):=\frac{(1-s)\log(1-s)}s,
\]
because $\frac1{d(d+1)}=\frac1d-\frac1{d+1}$, $\sum_ds^d/d=-\log(1-s)$,
$\sum_ds^d/(d+1)=(-\log(1-s)-s)/s$ and $\sum_d\frac1{d(d+1)}=1$. So
$\Psi_\infty(\theta)=\chi(e^{i\theta})$.

Next we compare $1-e^{i\theta}$ with $-i\theta$. Write
$1-e^{i\theta}=(-i\theta)\,\mathsf u$ with
$\mathsf u=\operatorname{sinc}(\theta/2)\,e^{i\theta/2}$, where
$\operatorname{sinc}y=\sin y/y$ is positive for $\lvert y\rvert\le\frac\pi2$.
So $\mathsf u$ is close to $1$ when $\theta$ is small. The argument of
$-i\theta$ is $\mp\frac\pi2$ for $\pm\theta>0$ and that of $\mathsf u$ is
$\theta/2$. They add up to a number in $(-\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2)$, so
$\log(1-e^{i\theta})=\log(-i\theta)+\log\mathsf u$ for principal logarithms.

Now we get a formula for $R$. By the two steps above,
$\chi(e^{i\theta})=(-i\theta)\,\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}\bigl(\log(-i\theta)+\log\mathsf u\bigr)$.
Subtracting $-i\theta\log(-i\theta)$ gives
\[
 R(\theta)=-i\theta\log(-i\theta)\bigl(\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}-1\bigr)
 -i\theta\,\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}\log\mathsf u .
\]

It remains to bound the three quantities in this formula. We show that
\[
 \lvert\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}-1\rvert\le0.631\,\lvert\theta\rvert,\qquad
 \lvert\log\mathsf u\rvert\le0.53\,\lvert\theta\rvert,\qquad
 \lvert\log(-i\theta)\rvert\le\lvert\log\lvert\theta\rvert\rvert+\frac\pi2 .
\]
For the first, put $y=\theta/2$. Then
$\lvert\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}-1\rvert=\lvert\operatorname{sinc}y-e^{iy}\rvert
\le(1-\operatorname{sinc}y)+\lvert1-e^{iy}\rvert\le\frac{y^2}6+\lvert y\rvert
\le(\frac12+\frac\pi{24})\lvert\theta\rvert$, where the last step uses
$\lvert\theta\rvert\le\pi$, and $\frac12+\frac\pi{24}\le0.631$. For the
second,
$\lvert\log\mathsf u\rvert^2=\log^2\operatorname{sinc}(\theta/2)+\theta^2/4$,
and $-\log\operatorname{sinc}y=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\zeta(2k)}{k\pi^{2k}}y^{2k}$
has positive coefficients. So
$\lvert\log\operatorname{sinc}(\theta/2)\rvert/\lvert\theta\rvert$ increases in
$\lvert\theta\rvert$, and its value at $\lvert\theta\rvert=\pi$ gives
$\lvert\log\mathsf u\rvert\le\bigl(\frac14+\frac{\log^2(\pi/2)}{\pi^2}\bigr)^{1/2}\lvert\theta\rvert
\le0.53\,\lvert\theta\rvert$. The third holds because $-i\theta$ has
modulus $\lvert\theta\rvert$ and argument $\pm\frac\pi2$. Since
$\lvert\mathsf ue^{-i\theta}\rvert\le1$, the formula for $R$ now gives
\[
 \lvert R(\theta)\rvert\le\lvert\theta\rvert\Bigl(\lvert\log\lvert\theta\rvert\rvert+\frac\pi2\Bigr)\cdot0.631\,\lvert\theta\rvert
 +\lvert\theta\rvert\cdot0.53\,\lvert\theta\rvert
 \le\theta^2\bigl(0.631\,\lvert\log\lvert\theta\rvert\rvert+1.53\bigr),
\]
as $0.631\cdot\frac\pi2+0.53\le1.53$.

\emph{Fact 3.} For $|\theta|\le\pi$,
$-\operatorname{Re}\Psi_\infty(\theta)\ge(2/5)|\theta|$.
For $0<\theta\le1/10$, Fact 2 gives
\[
 -\operatorname{Re}\Psi_\infty(\theta)/\theta
 \ge\pi/2-\theta\{0.631\log(1/\theta)+1.53\}>2/5.
\]
The subtracted expression increases on this interval. For
$1/10\le\theta\le\pi$ we use the exact identity
\[
 -\operatorname{Re}\Psi_\infty(\theta)
 =(1-\cos\theta)\log(2\sin(\theta/2))
   +\tfrac12(\pi-\theta)\sin\theta.
\]
Ball arithmetic on $305$ closed intervals of width $1/100$, starting
at $1/10$, proves that this exceeds $2\theta/5$. The intervals reach
$3.15>\pi$. Symmetry covers negative $\theta$, and equality holds at
zero. Appendix~\ref{app:data} gives the record.

\emph{The function $e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}}$ is a characteristic function of class $L$.} Let
$S_x$ have the law $\CP_\infty$ with parameter $x$, namely the law of
$\sum_{d\ge1}d\,\mathcal N_d$. Its characteristic function is
$e^{x\Psi_\infty(\theta)}$. By Fact 2, for $t\ne0$ and
$x\ge\max(1,\lvert t\rvert/\pi)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:bulk-AB}
 x\Psi_\infty(t/x)-it\log x=\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)+xR(t/x),\qquad
 \lvert xR(t/x)\rvert\le\frac{t^2}x\bigl(0.631(\lvert\log\lvert t\rvert\rvert+\log x)+1.53\bigr).
\end{equation}
So the characteristic function of $(S_x-x\log x)/x$ tends to $e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}$ as
$x\to\infty$, for every real $t$. The limit is continuous at $0$, so by
L\'evy's continuity theorem it is the characteristic function of a
probability law. It is integrable, so this law has the bounded continuous
density $f_L$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}. For $0<c<1$ and real $t$ we have
$\Psi_{\mathrm L}(ct)=c\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)-ict\log c$, and hence
\[
 e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}=e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(ct)}\cdot e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}((1-c)t)}\,e^{it[(1-c)\log(1-c)+c\log c]} .
\]
The last two factors form the characteristic function of a scaled and shifted
copy of the law. So the law is of class $L$ (self-decomposable), and $f_L$ is
unimodal by~\cite{yamazato1978}.

\emph{Proof of (a).} By Fourier inversion on $\Z$~\cite{katznelson2004} and the substitution
$\theta=t/x$,
\[
 x\,\CP_N(m)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi x}^{\pi x}e^{-i\lambda_mt}e^{A(t)}\,dt,\qquad
 A(t)=x\Psi_N(t/x)-it\log x .
\]
Let $\lvert t\rvert\le\pi x$. By \eqref{eq:bulk-AB} and Fact 1,
\[
 \lvert A(t)-\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)\rvert\le\frac{t^2}x\bigl(0.631(\lvert\log\lvert t\rvert\rvert+\log x)+1.53\bigr)+\frac{2x}N,
\]
and by Facts 1 and 3,
$\max(\operatorname{Re}A(t),\operatorname{Re}\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t))\le-\frac25\lvert t\rvert+\frac{2x}N$.
Now $\lvert e^u-e^v\rvert\le\lvert u-v\rvert\,e^{\max(\operatorname{Re}u,\operatorname{Re}v)}$,
and $\frac{2x}N\le\frac12$ because $N\ge4x$. So for $\lvert t\rvert\le\pi x$
\[
 \lvert e^{A(t)}-e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}\rvert\le e^{1/2}e^{-2\lvert t\rvert/5}
 \Bigl[\frac{t^2}x\bigl(0.631(\lvert\log\lvert t\rvert\rvert+\log x)+1.53\bigr)+\frac{2x}N\Bigr].
\]
The tangent bound for $\log s$ at $8$, together with
$s|\log s|\le1/e$ for $0<s<1$, gives
\[
 s^2|\log s|\le s^3/8+(\log8-1)s^2+s/e\qquad(s\ge0).
\]
Integrate the preceding estimate with $\rho=2/5$ and
$\int_0^\infty t^ke^{-\rho t}\,dt=k!/\rho^{k+1}$. The coefficient of
$\log x/x$ is
\[
 \frac{e^{1/2}}\pi\frac{2(0.631)}{\rho^3}<10.4,
\]
and that of $x/N$ is $2e^{1/2}/(\pi\rho)<2.7$.
The omitted Landau tail is
$2e^{-\pi^2x/2}/\pi^2\le4/(\pi^4x)$. Including it, the remaining
coefficient of $1/x$ is at most
\[
 \frac{e^{1/2}}\pi\left[
 0.631\left\{\frac6{8\rho^4}+\frac{2(\log8-1)}{\rho^3}
                         +\frac1{e\rho^2}\right\}
              +\frac{2(1.53)}{\rho^3}\right]+\frac4{\pi^4}<47.
\]
These strict numerical inequalities are enclosed in the same record.
Fourier inversion therefore proves (a), including negative integers
$m$, where $\CP_N(m)=0$.

\emph{Proof of (b).} Since $\varepsilon\le\frac14$, we have $N\ge4x$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:cp},
$\lvert xf_N(m)-x\,\CP_N(m)\rvert\le x\delta_\Sigma\le x\delta_B$. Add (a).

\emph{Proof of (c).} Drop the index $n$ and let $S_x$ have law
$\CP_\infty$. Adding the independent Poisson jumps of sizes $d\ge N$
couples $\CP_N$ to $S_x$ at cost at most
$1-e^{-x\sum_{d\ge N}1/(d(d+1))}=1-e^{-x/N}$. Thus
\[
 \operatorname{TV}(\mathcal L(M_N),\mathcal L(S_x))
 \le\delta_\Sigma+1-e^{-x/N}\longrightarrow0
\]
under the weaker condition $x^2\log^2N/N\to0$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:bulk-AB} and L\'evy's continuity theorem give
$(S_x-x\log x)/x\Rightarrow f_L$ as $x\to\infty$.
Total variation contracts under this affine map, so the same weak limit
holds for $M_N$. At the crossing $x_N\sim2\log N$, and both conditions
hold. \qed

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode}}\label{app:bulk-mode}

\emph{Step 1. A real integral.} We claim that for every real $\lambda$ and
$k\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:landau-real}
 f_L^{(k)}(\lambda)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty(-s)^k\,e^{-s\log s-\lambda s}\sin(\pi s)\,ds .
\end{equation}
For $k=0$ this is~\cite[Eq.~(4.3)]{mohle2005}, and we include a short proof.
Since $\Psi_{\mathrm L}(-t)=\overline{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}$, the definition of $f_L$ gives
$f_L(\lambda)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{Re}\int_0^\infty\Phi(-it)\,dt$ with
$\Phi(u)=\exp(u\log u+\lambda u)$. The function $\Phi$ is holomorphic off the
negative real axis. Put $u=\rho e^{i\varphi}$ with
$-\pi<\varphi\le-\frac\pi2$. Then
$\operatorname{Re}(u\log u)=\rho(\log\rho\cos\varphi-\varphi\sin\varphi)$,
where $\cos\varphi\le0$ and $\varphi\sin\varphi\ge0$. For
$\log\rho\ge\lvert\lambda\rvert+1$ this gives
$\operatorname{Re}(u\log u+\lambda u)\le-\rho(\lvert\cos\varphi\rvert+\varphi\sin\varphi)\le-\rho$,
because $\lvert\cos\varphi\rvert+\varphi\sin\varphi\ge1$ on
$[-\pi,-\frac\pi2]$ (for $\psi=\varphi+\pi\in[0,\frac\pi2]$ it equals
$\cos\psi+(\pi-\psi)\sin\psi\ge\cos\psi+\sin\psi\ge1$). Also $\Phi$ is bounded
near $0$. So by Cauchy's theorem we may turn the ray $u=-it$ to the lower
side of the negative real axis, where $u=-s$ and $\log u=\log s-i\pi$. There
$u\log u=-s\log s+i\pi s$, and $dt=i\,du=-i\,ds$. Hence
\[
 f_L(\lambda)=\frac1\pi\operatorname{Re}\Bigl[-i\int_0^\infty
 e^{-s\log s-\lambda s}\,e^{i\pi s}\,ds\Bigr]
 =\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-s\log s-\lambda s}\sin(\pi s)\,ds .
\]
The integrand decays faster than every exponential, so we may differentiate
under the integral sign. This proves \eqref{eq:landau-real}. Also $f_L''$
is bounded on the real line, because $t^2e^{\Psi_{\mathrm L}(t)}$ is integrable and we may
differentiate the Fourier formula for $f_L$ twice.

\emph{Step 2. Certified enclosures.} Let $-0.223\le\lambda\le0$ and
$k\in\{1,2\}$, and split the integral in \eqref{eq:landau-real} at
$\delta=2^{-20}$ and at $30$. On $[0,\delta]$ we have $-s\log s\le1/e$,
$-\lambda s\le0.223\,s$ and $\lvert\sin\pi s\rvert\le\pi s$, so this part is
at most $\pi e^{1/e+0.223\delta}\delta^{k+2}/(k+2)$ in absolute value. On
$[30,\infty)$ we have $s\log s+\lambda s\ge cs$ with $c=\log30-0.223>3$, so
this part is at most
$\int_{30}^\infty s^2e^{-cs}\,ds\le30^2e^{-30c}$. Both are below $10^{-17}$. On
$[\delta,30]$ the integrand is holomorphic in a neighborhood of the path, and
we enclose the integral with the rigorous integration algorithm
of~\cite{johansson2018}, in ball arithmetic with 128 bits. The
parameter $\lambda$ enters as a ball, so one run encloses the integral for
every $\lambda$ in an interval. The script \texttt{certify\_elephant.py} does
this and reports
\[
 f_L'(-0.2229)\in[9.232\cdot10^{-6},9.234\cdot10^{-6}],\qquad
 f_L'(-0.2227)\in[-6.548\cdot10^{-6},-6.546\cdot10^{-6}],
\]
and $f_L''(\lambda)\in[-0.0792,-0.0786]$ for every
$\lambda\in[-0.2229,-0.2227]$. In the same way it finds that $f_L'$ is
positive at $-0.22278299$ and negative at $-0.22278297$. The two enclosures are
$6.8984026\cdot10^{-10}$ and $-8.8809315\cdot10^{-10}$ with radii below $10^{-17}$, and
these are the smallest margins in the proof.

\emph{Step 3. The mode.} Write $[u_-,u_+]=[-0.2229,-0.2227]$. By Step 2, $f_L'$
is strictly decreasing on $[u_-,u_+]$ with $f_L'(u_-)>0>f_L'(u_+)$. So $f_L'$ has
exactly one zero $\lambda_{\mathrm L}$ in $[u_-,u_+]$, and $f_L$ is strictly increasing on
$[u_-,\lambda_{\mathrm L}]$ and strictly decreasing on $[\lambda_{\mathrm L},u_+]$. Since $f_L$ is
unimodal, there is a point $\lambda_*$ such that $f_L$ is nondecreasing on
$(-\infty,\lambda_*]$ and nonincreasing on $[\lambda_*,\infty)$. If
$\lambda_*>\lambda_{\mathrm L}$, then $f_L$ would be nondecreasing just to the right of
$\lambda_{\mathrm L}$, which is false. In the same way $\lambda_*<\lambda_{\mathrm L}$ is
impossible. So $\lambda_*=\lambda_{\mathrm L}$, and $f_L$ is nondecreasing on
$(-\infty,\lambda_{\mathrm L}]$ and nonincreasing on $[\lambda_{\mathrm L},\infty)$. By Step 2 again,
$\lambda_{\mathrm L}\in(-0.22278299,-0.22278297)$. This proves (a) and (b).

\emph{Step 4. The global gap.} Write $v=-0.22278298$ and
$r_0=10^{-8}$, so $|\lambda_{\mathrm L}-v|<r_0$.
Fourier inversion gives
\[
 \|f_L'\|_\infty\le4/\pi^3,\qquad
 \|f_L^{(3)}\|_\infty\le96/\pi^5.
\]
We evaluate \eqref{eq:landau-real} at $160$ bits with
$\delta=2^{-24}$ and upper cutoff $30$. All arguments exceed $-1.23$.
The omitted integrals are bounded as in Step 2, replacing $0.223$
by $1.23$ and using $\log30-1.23>2$.

First, $100$ equally spaced midpoint evaluations of $f_L''$ on
$[v-1/4,v+1/4]$, each enlarged by
$(96/\pi^5)(1/400+r_0)$, give $f_L''<-0.04$ throughout
$[\lambda_{\mathrm L}-1/4,\lambda_{\mathrm L}+1/4]$.
Taylor's formula gives the required quadratic gap there.
Next cover distances from $v$ between $0.249$ and $1.001$ on each
side by $188$ closed cells of width $0.004$. On a cell with midpoint
$y$ and largest possible distance $d$ from $\lambda_{\mathrm L}$,
the certificate verifies
\[
 f_L(v)-\left\{f_L(y)+\frac4{\pi^3}(0.002)\right\}
 >\frac1{50}\min(1,d^2).
\]
Since $f_L(\lambda_{\mathrm L})\ge f_L(v)$, this proves the gap on
the cells. They overlap the curvature region and extend past distance
one from the mode. Monotonicity then gives a gap of at least $1/50$
on both remaining tails. This proves (c). The record and the formal
deduction from these inequalities are in Appendix~\ref{app:data}. \qed

\subsection{Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:mode-location}}\label{app:bulk-cor}

The method is to compare $f_N$ at a mode with $f_N$ at the integer $m'$
nearest to the predicted place $x\log x+\lambda_{\mathrm L}x$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}(b) both values are close to values of $f_L$, and
$f_L$ falls off quadratically away from $\lambda_{\mathrm L}$. So a mode cannot be far
from $m'$.

We first prove (a). So we assume~\eqref{eq:H}, $E'<\kappa_L/2$ and $m_{\max}\le N/4$, with
$E_1$ and $E'$ as in the statement.

Let $m'$ be the integer nearest to $x\log x+\lambda_{\mathrm L}x$. This number
increases in $x\ge1$ and is at least $\lambda_{\mathrm L}>-\frac12$, so $m'\ge0$. Also
$\lvert\lambda_{m'}-\lambda_{\mathrm L}\rvert\le\frac1{2x}$, and since
$f_L'(\lambda_{\mathrm L})=0$,
$f_L(\lambda_{m'})\ge f_L(\lambda_{\mathrm L})-\lVert f_L''\rVert_\infty/(8x^2)$. Let
$\tilde m$ be a mode of $f_N$ or $\tilde m=m_N$. We claim that
$f_N(\tilde m)\ge f_N(m')-16xH_N/N^2$. For a mode of $f_N$ this is clear.
For $m_N$ we have
$f_N(m_N)+f_N(N-m_N)=2P_N(N-m_N)\ge2P_N(N-m')\ge f_N(m')$. Here
$N-m_N\ge\lceil N/2\rceil\ge\lceil N/4\rceil\ge m_{\max}$. So
$f_N$ is nonincreasing on $\{\lceil N/4\rceil,\dots,N\}$ by
Lemma~\ref{lem:unimodal}. There are at least $N/4$ integers from
$\lceil N/4\rceil$ to $\lceil N/2\rceil$. So Lemma~\ref{lem:B-sandwich}
(with $j=\lceil N/4\rceil\ge m_{\max}$ and $b=\lceil N/2\rceil$) gives the second
inequality below, and Lemma~\ref{lem:B-tail} gives the third,
\[
 f_N(N-m_N)\le f_N(\lceil N/2\rceil)\le\frac{\Prob(M_N\ge\lceil N/4\rceil)}{N/4}
 \le\frac{16xH_N}{N^2}.
\]
Now Theorem~\ref{thm:landau}(b), used at $\tilde m$ and at $m'$, gives
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 f_L(\lambda_{\tilde m})&\ge xf_N(\tilde m)-E_1
 \ge xf_N(m')-\frac{16x^2H_N}{N^2}-E_1\\
 &\ge f_L(\lambda_{m'})-2E_1-\frac{16x^2H_N}{N^2}
 \ge f_L(\lambda_{\mathrm L})-2E'.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The global gap in Lemma~\ref{lem:landau-mode}(c) and $2E'<\kappa_L$
give $(\lambda_{\tilde m}-\lambda_{\mathrm L})^2\le2E'/\kappa_L$.
This proves (a).

For (b), suppose $\log N\ge30000$ and $\log N\le x\le2\log N$.
The elementary bounds
\[
 H_N\le1+\log N,\qquad
 N=e^{\log N}\ge(\log N)^8/8!\ge160(\log N)^6
\]
give $e^{4x^2/N}\le2$ and $\delta_B\le40(\log N)^4/N$.
Consequently each of $2.7x/N$, $x\delta_B$ and
$16x^2H_N/N^2$ is at most $1/(2\log N)\le\log x/x$.
The rounding term is also at most $\log x/x$, since
$\|f_L''\|_\infty\le16/\pi^4$. As $\log x>10$,
\[
 E'\le\frac{47+14.4\log x}{x}
       <\frac{20\log x}{x}
       \le\frac{20\log30000}{30000}<\frac1{100}.
\]
The same bounds imply \eqref{eq:H},
$\varepsilon\log N\le1/40$, $804x\log(x+2)\le N$ and
$15400x\log^2(x+1)\le N/4$. Thus Lemma~\ref{lem:B-mode} gives
$m_{\max}\le N/4$. Part (a) now gives an error at most
$\sqrt{2000x\log x}<50\sqrt{x\log x}$, for every stated mode.
At the crossing
$x_N=2\log N-\log\log N+O(1)$ for even $N$, by Theorem~\ref{thm:erw-crossing}(b). So
$\log N\le x_N\le2\log N$ for large even $N$. \qed
