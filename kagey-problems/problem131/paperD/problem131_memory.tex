\begin{abstract}
Drop a ball through a Galton board on which every bounce after the first
repeats the previous direction with probability $p$. Kagey asked when the
middle bin is as likely as each end bin. For this Markov walk with $N$ steps
the crossing comes when the expected number of direction changes is about
$\log N$. We ask what changes when the ball remembers more than its last
step. In the elephant random walk each step copies a uniformly chosen
earlier step and reverses it with probability $\varepsilon$. We prove that
the crossing is unique and that $N\varepsilon$ at the crossing is
$2\log N-\log\log N-\log16+o(1)$, about twice the Markov value, because a
walk that ends in the middle needs a reversal among its first few steps.
At the crossing the law has a hump near each end, and the middle is the
lowest bin outside a window of width $O(N^{1/2}\log N)$. Next we take
alternating runs whose lengths have tail $k^{-\alpha}$ with $1<\alpha<2$,
started in equilibrium. Wang, Li and H\"anggi observed, as a heuristic for
heat conduction, that the ends and the middle decay at the same rate when
$\alpha$ is the golden ratio. We prove the lattice form of their observation,
with its constant. Namely, the ends beat the middle for large $N$ exactly when
$\alpha$ is below the golden ratio. Finally, an aging walk switches at step
$k$ with probability $c/k$. With a fixed first step each of the bins
$1,\dots,N-1$ ties with the end exactly at $c=1$, for every $N$. We prove the
same for an aging walk
with $d$ states, whose vector of counts is exactly uniform at the tie.
\end{abstract}
