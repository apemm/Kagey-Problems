\begin{abstract}
A ball falls through a Galton board with $d$ directions. It picks its first
direction uniformly, and at each later peg it keeps its direction with
probability $p$ and otherwise turns to one of the other $d-1$ directions at
random. For $d=2$ this is the persistent Galton board of Kagey's Problem 131,
and for $d=3$ it is his tetrahedral board. The bin is the vector of counts of
the $d$ directions, a point of a simplex. When $p$ is close to $1$ the most
likely bins are the vertices, which only a ball that never turns can reach. We
ask when the most likely bin on each face of the simplex becomes as likely as a
vertex. Put $T=N(1-p)/(d-1)$ for $N$ steps. To first order every face catches
up at the same place, $T\approx\log N$. We break this tie at second order. The
central bin of a face with $k$ directions crosses a vertex when
$T+\frac12\log T=\log N+a_k+1/(8T)+O((\log N)^{-2})$, with explicit constants
$a_2>a_3>\cdots$, each the mass of a face over a Gaussian volume. So for large
$N$ the mode jumps once, from the vertices straight to the center. A start law
or a weak field shifts the constants and can let an intermediate face win.
\end{abstract}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} persistent random walk; Galton board; Potts chain;

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The question and the answer}\label{sec:question}

Problem 131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey131} is about a
Galton board on which the ball tends to keep going the same way. At the first
peg the ball goes left or right with probability $\frac12$, and at every later
peg it repeats its previous direction with probability $p$. Kagey asks when the
middle bin is exactly as likely as the 2 end bins, and Paper~A~\cite{paperA}
answers this. Kagey also asks what changes on a tetrahedron. There each peg
sends the ball in one of 3 directions instead of 2, and the bins after $N$
steps form a triangle, one layer of the tetrahedron.

In this paper the ball has any number $d\ge2$ of directions. It picks its first
direction uniformly. At each later peg it keeps its direction with probability
$p$, and otherwise it turns to one of the other $d-1$ directions, each with
probability $r=(1-p)/(d-1)$. After $N$ steps its bin is the vector
$k=(k_1,\ldots,k_d)$ of the numbers of steps in each direction. So the bins
are the points of a simplex: a segment for $d=2$, Kagey's triangle for $d=3$.
The corners $Ne_i$ of the simplex are its vertices. Only a ball that never
turns reaches a vertex, so each vertex has probability $p^{N-1}/d$.

When $p$ is close to $1$ the ball rarely turns, and the vertices are the most
likely bins. As $p$ decreases the other bins catch up. On the triangle there
are 2 natural candidates to catch up first: the middle of an edge, where the
ball has used 2 directions, and the middle of the triangle, where it has used
all 3. Which one wins, and when?

Here is the answer. Put $L=\log N$ and $T=Nr$. Every face of the simplex
catches up with the vertices at the same place to first order, when $T$ is
about $L$. So to first order the question has no answer. We break this tie at
second order, and the center of the whole simplex wins. As $p$ decreases, the
most likely bin jumps from the vertices straight to the center, and for large
$N$ no edge or other proper face ever holds it. On the triangle the middle bin
$(\frac N3,\frac N3,\frac N3)$ overtakes the corners while the middles of the
edges are still about $17\%$ below them. Figure~\ref{fig:simplex} shows this
at $N=24$.

\paragraph{Why every face ties.}
Take a face with $k$ directions. A ball that uses only
these directions must avoid the other $d-k$ directions at every peg, and it
does so with probability $(1-(d-k)r)^{N-1}\approx e^{-(d-k)T}$. So the face
carries mass about $\frac kde^{-(d-k)T}$, while a vertex carries
$\frac1de^{-(d-1)T}$. On the face the ball turns about $T$ times towards each
direction, so the fractions $k_i/N$ fluctuate by about $1/\sqrt T$ around
$1/k$. The mass of the face is therefore spread over about $(N/\sqrt T)^{k-1}$
bins near its center, and the central bin gets a share of about
$(\sqrt T/N)^{k-1}$. Altogether
\[
\frac{\text{central bin of the face}}{\text{vertex}}
\approx\text{const}\cdot\Bigl(\frac{e^T\sqrt T}N\Bigr)^{k-1}.
\]
The bracket is the same for every face. It crosses $1$ when
$T+\frac12\log T=L+O(1)$, at the same place for every face, and even the term
$\frac12\log T$ is shared. Only the constant in front can tell the faces
apart.

\paragraph{How second order breaks the tie.}
The constant is $e^{-(k-1)a_k}$, with $a_k$ given in~\eqref{eq:intro-ak}
below. It has 2 sources. The face has $k$ times the start mass of a vertex,
and the fractions have Gaussian fluctuations with covariance $\Sigma_k/T$ for an
explicit matrix $\Sigma_k$. So $e^{-(k-1)a_k}=k\det(2\pi\Sigma_k)^{-1/2}$,
the mass of the face divided by a Gaussian volume
(Proposition~\ref{prop:mass-balance}). Now write the ratio as
$\exp((k-1)[\Psi-a_k])$ with the common bracket $\Psi=T-L+\frac12\log T$. As
$p$ decreases, $T$ and $\Psi$ increase. The constants decrease,
$a_2>a_3>\cdots>a_d$, so the full face, which has the smallest constant,
reaches the vertices first, when $\Psi=a_d$. At that moment a face with $k<d$
directions is still below the vertices by the factor $e^{-(k-1)(a_k-a_d)}$. On
the triangle this factor is $e^{-(a_2-a_3)}=0.827$, which is the $17\%$ above.
Before that moment every proper face is below the vertices, since
$\Psi<a_d<a_k$. After it the full center is above every proper face, since
$(d-1)(\Psi-a_d)>(k-1)(\Psi-a_k)$ when $\Psi\ge a_d$. So no proper face is
ever the mode.

\subsection{Setting and notation}\label{sec:setting}

We collect the notation here. Fix $d\ge2$ and a start law $\mu$ on the set
$[d]=\{1,\ldots,d\}$ of directions, which we also call states. Let
$X_1,X_2,\ldots$ be the Markov chain with $X_1\sim\mu$ that keeps its state
with probability $p\in[0,1]$ and moves to each of the other $d-1$ states with
probability $r=(1-p)/(d-1)$. Put $\sigma=p-r=(dp-1)/(d-1)$. Kagey's board is
$d=2$ with the uniform start.

\emph{Bins.} The count vector $K_N=(K_{N,1},\ldots,K_{N,d})$ records how often
each state occurs among $X_1,\ldots,X_N$. A bin is a vector $k$ of nonnegative
integers with sum $N$, and $P^\mu_N(k)=\Prob(K_N=k)$. For the uniform start
$\mu_i=1/d$ we write $P_N(k)$. The uniform start is the default everywhere
except in Section~\ref{sec:general-start}.

\emph{Faces and vertices.} A face is a nonempty set $F\subseteq[d]$, and
$\mu(F)=\sum_{a\in F}\mu_a$. A bin $k$ lies on the face
$\supp k=\{i:k_i>0\}$. The vertices are the faces of size $1$, that is, the
bins $Ne_i$. A word that reaches $Ne_i$ never switches, so
$P^\mu_N(Ne_i)=\mu_ip^{N-1}$, and for the uniform start we write
$V_N=p^{N-1}/d$.

\emph{Balanced bins.} A bin with $k$ positive coordinates is balanced if these
coordinates differ by at most $1$. For the uniform start all balanced bins with
$k$ positive coordinates have the same probability, since they differ only by a
permutation of the states. We call it $C_{N,k}(p)$, so $C_{N,1}=V_N$.

\emph{Scales.} Put $L=\log N$ and $T=Nr$. The expected number of switches is
$(N-1)(1-p)=(d-1)T(1-1/N)$, so $T$ is about the expected number of switches
divided by the number $d-1$ of possible targets. Put $\tau=T/L$. For $p>0$ let $u=r/p$, the odds of a switch to one given
state against no switch, and $z=Nu=T/p$. For $p>1/d$ we have $\sigma>0$ and
also use $v=r/\sigma=u/(1-u)$. Since $z-T=(d-1)Tz/N$, the 2 scales $z$ and $T$
differ by $O(L^2/N)$ when $T=O(L)$. This is smaller than every error term in
our asymptotic statements, so these statements hold with either one. Exact
statements are written in $u$.

\emph{Crossings.} For $2\le k\le d$, the crossing $p_{N,k}$ is the value of $p$
at which $C_{N,k}=V_N$, and $z_{N,k}=N(1-p_{N,k})/(d-1)$ is the value of $T$
there (not of $z$). We write $p^{(d)}_{N,k}$ when the number of states matters.

\emph{The constants.} For $k\ge2$ put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-ak}
a_k=\frac12\log(4\pi)-\frac{k+\frac12}{k-1}\log k,\qquad
D_k=e^{-(k-1)a_k}=\frac{k^{k+1/2}}{(4\pi)^{(k-1)/2}},\qquad
\Psi_N(T)=T-L+\frac12\log T .
\end{equation}
Numerically $a_2=-0.4674$, $a_3=-0.6571$ and $a_4=-0.8139$.

\emph{Matrix form.} The transition matrix is $I+(T/N)Q$ with $Q=J-dI$, where $J$
is the all-ones matrix, so $Q$ switches at rate $1$ to each other state. The
exit rate of a face $F$ with $k$ states is $\lambda_F=d-k$, the principal
Dirichlet eigenvalue of $-Q$ on $F$. Paper~C~\cite{paperC} calls $K_F$ the
second-order constant of a face. Here $K_F=\mu(F)k^{k-1/2}(4\pi)^{-(k-1)/2}$ for
$k\ge2$ and $K_{\{i\}}=\mu_i$ (Section~\ref{sec:general-start}), and for the
uniform start $a_k=-\frac1{k-1}\log(K_F/K_{\{i\}})$.

\emph{Potts chain.} With the uniform start and $0<p<1$, a word $x_1\cdots x_N$
has probability $\frac1dr^{N-1}(p/r)^{\#\{s<N:\,x_s=x_{s+1}\}}$. This is the
one-dimensional $d$-state Potts model with free boundaries. For $d=2$ it is
the Ising chain of Paper~A.

\emph{Asymptotic notation.} In each statement the constants in $O(\cdot)$, and
the threshold behind ``for large $N$'', depend only on the quantities declared
fixed in that statement (always including $d$ and $k$). Uniformity in the other
variables is stated each time.

\subsection{Main results}\label{sec:results}

Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-window} makes the heuristic of
Section~\ref{sec:question} precise. Uniformly for $cL\le T\le CL$, the
logarithm of $C_{N,k}/V_N$ is $(k-1)[\Psi_N(T)-a_k]$, up to a next term
$-(k-1)/(8T)$ that is again proportional to $k-1$ and an error
$O(T^{-2}+T^2/N)$. In the first-order language of Paper~C, where $T=\tau L$
and only multiples of $L$ count, a balanced bin on a face $F$ has probability
$\exp(-L[\tau\lambda_F+|F|-1]+O(\log L))$. On the complete graph every face
attains the minimum $d-1$ of $\tau\lambda_F+|F|-1$ at $\tau=1$, so the
first-order theory cannot choose.

By Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-crossing}, for every $N\ge k$ the balanced bin with
$k$ positive coordinates crosses the vertices at exactly one $p_{N,k}$, which
increases with $N$, and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-crossing}
z_{N,k}+\frac12\log z_{N,k}=L+a_k+\frac1{8z_{N,k}}+O(L^{-2}).
\end{equation}
For $k=2$ this is the crossing equation of Paper~A, and $a_2=\frac12\log(\pi/8)$
is its constant $c_0$. Since $a_2>a_3>\cdots>a_d$, Corollary~\ref{cor:simplex-modes}
shows that for large $N$ and all $T\le CL$ the global mode changes exactly
once, from the $d$ vertices straight to the balanced bins with $d$ positive
coordinates. All the face-center crossings lie in a $p$-interval of length
$(d-1)(a_2-a_d+o(1))/N$. For small $N$ this can fail: on the triangle with
$N=4$ an edge center can be the mode (Example~\ref{ex:simplex-edge-mode}).

The same method gives the crossing of every bin whose positive coordinates are
of order $N$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-interior}). For fractions $\alpha$ its
constant is $D(\alpha)=A^2/\sqrt{\det(2\pi\Sigma_\alpha)}$ with
$A=\sum_i\sqrt{\alpha_i}$, where $\Sigma_\alpha$ is the occupation covariance of
a tilted chain, and $\det\Sigma_\alpha$ is a weighted count of the spanning
trees of the complete graph $K_k$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:kirchhoff}). At the
center the count is Cayley's $k^{k-2}$, and $D(\alpha)=D_k$.

Then we perturb the model in 2 ways, each of which acts at order $1$, the order
of the constants $a_k$. A start law $\mu$ changes the constant of a face $F$
against a vertex $i$ to $a_k+\frac1{k-1}\log(k\mu_i/\mu(F))$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:start-crossings}). On the triangle with
$\mu=(\frac12,\frac12,0)$ this lets an edge hold the mode on a window of width
$\log(16/(9\sqrt3))=0.026$ in $T$ (Remark~\ref{ex:K3-start}). A Potts field of
size $h_i/N$ per visit to state $i$ adds to each face the field averaged over
the face (Theorem~\ref{thm:weakfield-phases}), and one field makes every
support size win in turn (Corollary~\ref{cor:weakfield-full-hierarchy}).

Finally we drop the assumption that the positive coordinates are of order $N$.
A Bessel majorant has relative error $O(L^2/N)$ for every positive count vector
when $T=O(L)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:uniform-simplex-majorant}). When at least 2
coordinates are of order $N$, a small coordinate starts to matter once it has
size $N/L^2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-boundary-crossover}). Near a vertex,
with one large count $b$ and small counts of total $o(b)$, the crossing odds
are $u=\sqrt{y/b}\,(1+o(1))$, where $y$ is the root of a product of
Laguerre-type polynomials (Theorem~\ref{thm:vertex-uniform-root}).

The sections follow this order: the exact law
(Section~\ref{sec:multistate-model}), the tie
(Section~\ref{sec:simplex-thresholds}), the start, the field and the boundary
(Sections~\ref{sec:general-start} to~\ref{sec:frontier-boundary}), and the
checks and open questions (Section~\ref{sec:verify}). The longer proofs are in
Appendices~\ref{app:thresholds} to~\ref{app:vertex}.

This is the second of 4 papers on Problem 131, with the common title ``The
middle and the ends'' (I is Paper~A, II is this Paper~B, III is Paper~C and IV
is Paper~D). They share the following notation.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Kagey's board has $N$ steps (his row $n$ has $N=n-1$). The first step is
left or right with probability $\frac12$, and each later step repeats the
previous one with probability $p$. Put $q=1-p$ and $u=q/p$, the odds of a
switch.
\item $P_N(k)$ is the probability of $k$ right steps, that is, of bin $k$. The
endpoints have $P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\frac12p^{N-1}$.
\item $L=\log N$ and $T=Nq$, which is the expected number $(N-1)q$ of switches
up to an error below $1$.
\item The crossing is the value of $p$, or of $T$, at which a bin is exactly as
likely as an endpoint. For the middle bin it is $p_N$, and $z_N=N(1-p_N)$.
\item In Papers~B and~C the chain has $d$ states. A face $F$ is a nonempty set
of states, the vertices are the faces of size $1$, $\lambda_F$ is the principal
Dirichlet eigenvalue of $-Q$ on $F$, and $K_F$ is the second-order constant.
\end{itemize}
With $d$ directions we replace $q$ by $r=q/(d-1)$, so $T=Nr$ and $u=r/p$. For
$d=2$ these are the quantities above, $P_N(k,N-k)$ is $P_N(k)$, and
$z_{N,2}=z_N$. Paper~A~\cite{paperA} brackets $z_N$ for every $N\ge2$ with
uniform Bessel bounds and proves its crossing equation with an explicit error.
Paper~C~\cite{paperC} gives the general theory for $P=I+(T/N)Q$, with the
first-order selection of Section~\ref{sec:results} and the second-order
constants $K_F$. The present paper is the case where the first order is silent
on every face, and it resolves the tie in full.
Paper~D~\cite{paperD} asks what memory changes. Figure~\ref{fig:program} shows
the crossings of all 4 papers.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

The law belongs to the theory of Markov multinomial distributions. Wang and
Yang~\cite{wangyang1995} extended the Markov Bernoulli model of
Edwards~\cite{edwards1960} to finitely many states. We have seen neither paper,
and we describe both through Wang and Tang~\cite{wangtang1997,wangtang2003}.
The chain of Wang and Yang starts from a law $\mu$ and moves from $i$ to $j$
with probability $\theta\delta_{ij}+(1-\theta)\mu_j$, and the generating
function of the sum of its states is a product of matrices. Our chain with the
uniform start is the case where $\mu$ is uniform and $\theta=\sigma$. So the
exact law for the uniform start is a special case of this theory, and we do
not claim it as new. Our general start changes only the initial law and keeps
the uniform switching, so it is a Wang--Yang chain only when $\mu$ is uniform.
Hsiau~\cite{hsiau1997} and Wang and Tang~\cite{wangtang2003} prove compound
Poisson limits for sums over multistate chains. Whittle~\cite{whittle1955} gave
the exact law of the transition counts of a finite chain. The count vector is
determined by the transition counts and the first state, so $P_N$ could also be
summed from his formula.

Brydges, van der Hofstad and K\"onig~\cite{brydges2007} give occupation
densities for continuous-time chains on the visited faces of the simplex.
Huang, Kious, Sidoravicius and Tarr\'es~\cite[Theorem~1]{huang2018} give the
joint density of the local times, the last-exit spanning tree and the net
crossing numbers of a walk with symmetric conductances, with Bessel functions.
So in continuous time the trees appear directly, but we do not use their
formula. Stadje~\cite{stadje1999} gives generating functions for the joint
numbers of visits of a discrete chain and limit laws when one state is nearly
absorbing. Dietz and Sethuraman~\cite{dietz2007} study chains that switch from
$i$ to $j$ at time $n$ with probability $G(i,j)/n$, where every $G(i,j)>0$.
Their chains make about $\log N$ switches by time $N$ but only $O(1)$ of them
after time $N/2$, and their occupation fractions have a limit law with full
support on the simplex, which is a Dirichlet law when $G(i,j)$ depends only on
$j$. Near our crossings the chain makes about $(d-1)\log N$ switches, spread
evenly in time. Dupuis and Liu~\cite{dupuisliu2015} give the rate function of
the occupation measure, which we compare with our exponent in
Section~\ref{sec:simplex-thresholds}. Kassan-Ogly and
Filippov~\cite{kassanogly2003} solve the Potts chain in a field in the
thermodynamic limit. For Whittle, Hsiau, Stadje and Kassan-Ogly and Filippov we
have read only the abstracts, and we use nothing beyond them. None of these
works compare one count vector with a vertex when $p$ depends on $N$, and we did
not find the face-center crossings or the tie in the literature. We take the
Bessel expansions from~\cite{dlmf}.

\section{Verification and open questions}\label{sec:verify}

No proof in this paper relies on computation. All 4 papers on Problem 131
share one verification supplement, \texttt{SUPPLEMENT.md} in the Problem 131
folder of \url{https://github.com/apemm/Kagey-Problems}, and
\texttt{python -B verify\_all.py --paper b} in that folder runs the checks for
this paper (\texttt{--full} adds a run of about 3 minutes). The scripts use only
the Python standard library. They compare the exact law with dynamic programs
and with enumeration of all words, and they check the crossings, the constants
and their tree forms. The statements that we mark as numerically verified or
with the proof omitted are checked as follows, each in 2 ways where possible.
{\sloppy
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Remark~\ref{rem:start-weights}: \texttt{verify\_general\_start.py}
computes the start weights from the run form and from the refresh form in
double precision, at $N=2000$ with 2 states, against the limit.
\item Remark~\ref{ex:K3-start}: dynamic programs over all bins in double
precision, at $N=90$ in \texttt{verify\_general\_start.py} and at $N=300$ and
$1002$ in \texttt{verify\_B\_tie\_repair.py}.
\item Remark~\ref{rem:K3-finite}: the 2 ends of the window by bisection in
double precision, the lower end from the run form and from Paper~A's polynomial,
at $N=300$ in \texttt{verify\_B\_tie\_repair.py} and at $N\ge3000$ in
\texttt{verify\_general\_start.py}.
\item Remark~\ref{rem:frontier-superlevel}: \texttt{verify\_frontier\_modes.py}
evaluates the refresh series in 65-digit decimal arithmetic and counts lattice
points against the volume formula.
\item The last paragraph of Subsection~\ref{sec:vertex-completion}:
\texttt{verify\_frontier\_boundary.py} and \texttt{verify\_vertex\_completion.py}
check exact identities in rational arithmetic against word enumeration, and
evaluate positive sums in 70-digit or 100-digit arithmetic at 2 series cutoffs.
\item The numbers in the remark after Theorem~\ref{thm:simplex-boundary-crossover}:
\texttt{verify\_crossover\_factor.py}, as described at the end of that remark.
\end{itemize}
}
None of these checks is interval-certified. Before the newer computations we
wrote the predictions in ledgers, which keep the 2 predictions that failed.
Many exact identities and finite bounds are also proved in Lean~4, in the
folder \texttt{lean/PaperB}: among them the exact law for the uniform start,
balancing, the order of the constants $a_k$, the arithmetic of
Corollary~\ref{cor:weakfield-full-hierarchy} and the bracket near a vertex. The
asymptotic results are not in Lean.

Several questions remain open. We have not proved the discrete term
$-\frac{(k-1)^2}2z^2/N$ or the second-order ends of the window of
Remark~\ref{rem:K3-finite}. We have not proved that balancing fails at all large
$N$ for every start that is not constant on the face.
Corollary~\ref{cor:simplex-modes} has no effective $N_0$, and by
Example~\ref{ex:simplex-edge-mode} it does not extend to every $N$. We say
nothing about the modes when $T$ is much larger than $L$. Near a vertex we know
the leading crossing uniformly for rare counts of total $o(b)$, but not the next
term uniformly in that range, and not with a start that is not uniform. We have
not treated the crossover with a start that is not constant on the support, in
particular one that sits only on the rare coordinates, or a number of states
that grows with $N$. Switching that is not uniform is the subject of Paper~C.
