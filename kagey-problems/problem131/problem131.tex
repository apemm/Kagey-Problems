\title{Endpoint competition in a Galton board with memory:\\
 uniform thresholds, boundary conditions, and simplex modes\\[1ex]
 \large Problem 131 and its persistent-walk extensions}
\author{Arjun Pemmasani}
\date{September 22, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A ball on a Galton board bounces left or right with equal probability at the first peg and afterwards
repeats its previous direction with probability $p$. Problem 131 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection
asks for the distribution over the bins, for the $p$ at which the middle bin is as likely as the
extreme bins, for the relation to lattice walks and to OEIS A035002 and A348595, for other board
geometries, and for the normal limit and its variance.

The model is the correlated random walk of Goldstein and Gillis, and the exact distribution is a
run count going back to Wald and Wolfowitz; we record it with a short proof, a rational generating
function and a three-term Pascal-type recurrence. We prove by explicit bijections that Kagey's
numerator table for $p=2/3$ is the rook-path array A035002 and that his table for $p=1/3$ is the
symmetric array whose lower half is A348595. The bin index after $N$ bounces has variance
$Np/(4(1-p))-(2p-1)\bigl(1-(2p-1)^N\bigr)/(8(1-p)^2)$ and is asymptotically normal for $0<p<1$. In
every row with $N\ge2$ there is exactly one $p=p_N$ at which the middle equals the extremes; $p_N$ increases
strictly from $p_2=2/3$, $p_3=1/\sqrt2$ to $1$. A uniform Bessel approximation proves
$N(1-p_N)=\log N-\tfrac12\log\log N+\tfrac12\log(\pi/8)+o(1)$ and yields a further
inverse-logarithm correction. We extend the relative Bessel bound to every interior bin,
with error at most $Nu^2/2+u$, where $u=(1-p)/p$. This yields a threshold expansion uniform
over all diverging distances from an edge, matched to a Laguerre-polynomial law at fixed
distance. For periodic Ising boundaries we obtain the corresponding estimate, a rigorous
refinement of a previously studied finite-size crossing, and an explicit $\log2$ boundary
shift. Both boundary conditions have the spatial crossing profile $e^{t-2y^2}$.
For the symmetric $d$-direction board, a positive Bessel integral gives sharp crossings
for every balanced simplex face and a global-mode transition in the logarithmic window.
An estimate uniform over all positive compositions then resolves a rare-coordinate
crossover at $N/(\log N)^2$. We also determine the spatial volume of superlevel bins
and prove that weak occupation fields can stabilize every intermediate support size.
Near a vertex, a product of finite Laguerre polynomials determines the crossing and
its first correction, which depends on the number and multiplicities of the rare colors.
The leading Laguerre-product root remains valid uniformly for arbitrary growing rare
counts whose total is $A=o(b)$, where $b$ is the dominant count, with relative root
error $O_\ell(\sqrt{(A+1)/b})$ for a fixed number $\ell$ of rare colors.
This root approximation can hold even when the corresponding relative probability
approximation fails by an unbounded factor.
Exact laws, ordinary normal limits, and fixed-scale Bessel distributions are established
theory; the proposed contributions are the quantitative moving-parameter refinements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Problem 131 of Kagey's collection \cite{kagey131} reads as follows. Model a Plinko or Galton board
by assuming that a ball (1) is equally likely to bounce in either direction off the first peg, and
(2) thereafter bounces in the same direction it previously bounced with probability $p$. So for
$p=1$ the ball always repeats its direction, for $p=\tfrac12$ the bounces are independent, and for
$p=0$ the ball alternates. The page shows ``numerator tables'' for $p=\tfrac13,\tfrac23,\tfrac56$
and the probability mass functions of the tenth row, and asks: \emph{for an arbitrary $p\in[0,1]$
and a triangle with $n$ rows, what is the distribution of balls in bin $k$?} Four related questions
follow.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2.2em,itemsep=0pt,topsep=3pt]
\item[\textbf{R1.}] What is the least/greatest value of $p$ such that at the $(2n)$-th row the
middle entry equals the extremes? How about the $(2n+1)$-st row?
\item[\textbf{R2.}] What changes on a different geometry (a cylinder, a tetrahedron)?
\item[\textbf{R3.}] How does this relate to lattice walks? (See A348595.)
\item[\textbf{R4.}] As $n\to\infty$, does the distribution converge to a normal distribution? If so,
what is the variance as a function of $p$?
\end{itemize}
The references given are A035002 (numerators for $p=\tfrac23$) and A348595 (``related'' to $p=\tfrac13$).

\paragraph{Conventions.}
We index by the number $N\ge0$ of bounces. After $N$ bounces the ball is in one of $N+1$ bins, and
bin $k\in\{0,\dots,N\}$ means $k$ bounces to the right. Kagey's $n$-th row has $n$ entries, so it is
our $N=n-1$; his tenth-row histograms have ten bars. We write $q=1-p$, $\rho=2p-1$, and $P_N(k)$ for
the probability of bin $k$ after $N$ bounces. For $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ in lowest terms, the
entries of Kagey's tables are
\[
n_N(k)=2(\alpha+\beta)^{N-1}P_N(k)\quad(N\ge1),\qquad n_0(0)=1 ,
\]
not reduced further. This normalization reproduces all 3 tables of the figure, rows $1$ to $9$.

\paragraph{What is answered.}
The main question is answered by Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf} and Proposition~\ref{prop:gf}. R3 and the two
OEIS references are settled by Theorems~\ref{thm:rook} and~\ref{thm:mathar}. R4 is answered by
Proposition~\ref{prop:var} and Theorem~\ref{thm:clt}: the limit is normal for $0<p<1$ and the
variance of the bin index is asymptotically $Np/(4(1-p))$. R1 is answered by
Theorem~\ref{thm:root} and the paragraph after it: each row has exactly one solution $p_N$, the
least values among nontrivial rows are $\tfrac23$ (odd rows) and $1/\sqrt2$ (even rows),
and there is no greatest value. Theorem~\ref{thm:bessel} proves a quantitative Bessel approximation
and its second-order expansion. Proposition~\ref{prop:adjacent} gives the adjacent-bin crossing.
For R2, Remark~\ref{rem:cyl} treats the cylinder, and Propositions~\ref{prop:dgf}
and~\ref{prop:dpmf} and Theorem~\ref{thm:dclt} give an exact law and a normal limit for the
explicit symmetric tetrahedral model defined below. Other geometric transition rules can define
different processes.

The threshold analysis is developed in Sections~\ref{sec:allbin},
\ref{sec:periodicwindow} and~\ref{sec:simplex-thresholds}. It extends the binary comparison
to all edge distances and compares free with periodic spin boundaries. In the specified
multidirectional model it also determines the sharp balanced-face crossings and the global
modes in their critical window. The complete finite-$N$ multistate mode classification
is not asserted: a four-step, three-direction example has its maxima on proper edges.
Sections~\ref{sec:frontier-boundary} and~\ref{sec:frontier-modes} extend the analysis to
rare coordinates, spatial superlevel volumes, and weak-field face phases.
Section~\ref{sec:vertex-completion} completes the leading near-vertex crossing law
uniformly over all rare-count totals $A=o(b)$, including arbitrarily unequal counts.

\paragraph{Literature.}
The position of the ball is the \emph{correlated} (or \emph{persistent}) random walk, studied since
Goldstein \cite{goldstein1951} and Gillis \cite{gillis1955}. Renshaw and Henderson
\cite{renshaw1981} treat exactly the walk that continues with probability $p$ and reverses with
probability $1-p$; according to its abstract that paper derives exact $n$-step transition
probabilities and limiting distributions. We have consulted only the abstract, and our proofs
are self-contained. The count of binary words by number of runs, which is all that
Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf} needs, is the classical computation of Wald and Wolfowitz
\cite{waldwolfowitz1940}. Hence, the exact law and the normal limit should not be regarded as new.
Dekking and Kong~\cite{dekkingkong2011} give the Markov-binomial law, variance, interior
log-concavity, and local modality classification; their parameters $a=b=1-p$ and symmetric
initial law give our model. Local modality is distinct from equality of central and endpoint
heights. Herrmann and Vallois~\cite{herrmann2010} give the classical telegraph limit with its
$I_0,I_1$ density. The quantitative estimate here treats the moving regime $Nu=O(\log N)$,
which does not follow from fixed-scale weak convergence alone.

The $d$-direction model belongs to Markov multinomial theory
\cite{wangyang1995,wangtang2003}; we have not obtained the original Wang--Yang paper and make
no novelty claim for the finite sum or normal limit given here. The OEIS entries
\cite{oeisA035002,oeisA348595} already record the relevant generating functions, and Kagey
already indicates their connections; our bijections explain these identifications.

The Ising literature gives a closer antecedent than the initial search identified.
Antal, Droz and R\'acz~\cite{antal2004} derive fixed-scale magnetization distributions
under several boundary conditions. Dantchev and Rudnick~\cite{dantchev2022} give exact
free and periodic fixed-magnetization formulas and periodic Bessel scaling.
Stepanyan et al.~\cite{stepanyan2024} explicitly define the periodic endpoint--center
and endpoint--adjacent crossings and report a finite-size approximation to the former.
Thus the crossing question itself is already present in the literature. The refinements
here control the discrete error while the persistence parameter moves with $N$, invert
the resulting comparison quantitatively, and determine a spatial superlevel-set window.
For the multistate extension, general continuous-time occupation densities on visited
simplex faces are also known~\cite{brydges2007}; the discrete moving-scale comparison
proved below is a different assertion.
The square-root composition quantity $A^2-1$, where $A=\sum_i\sqrt{\alpha_i}$,
is also classical: for the complete-graph generator $Q$ with off-diagonal entries one,
the symmetric occupation rate is $I_Q(\alpha)=d-A^2$. Thus $A^2-1$ is the difference
between the vertex rate and the rate at $\alpha$, not a new rate function.
The results proposed here concern uniform discrete relative errors and their
boundary-crossing and modal consequences.

No matching theorem for the full quantitative package was located in the inspected
sources. This bounded search does not establish priority. The full Renshaw--Henderson
and Wang--Yang papers remain to be checked. Library access allowed us to inspect
\v{C}ekanavi\v{c}ius and Mikalauskas~\cite{cekanavicius2001}: Theorem~1.1 assumes
$p,\bar q\le1/2$, where $p$ is the repeat probability in state one. It therefore
does not cover our symmetric $p\to1$ regime; the paragraph following Corollary~1.1
explicitly leaves a high-persistence range unresolved there.
Their separate large-deviation paper~\cite{cekanavicius2001ld} was also inspected:
Theorems~1.1--1.2 require $p\le1/50$ and Theorem~1.3 requires $p\le\bar q$.
These hypotheses likewise exclude the symmetric persistence limit studied here.

\section{The exact distribution}\label{sec:exact}

A run of the experiment with $N\ge1$ bounces is a word $w\in\{L,R\}^N$. Let $\chg(w)$ be the number of
indices $i<N$ with $w_i\ne w_{i+1}$ and $\rep(w)=N-1-\chg(w)$. By the definition of the model,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wordprob}
\Prob(w)=\tfrac12\,p^{\rep(w)}q^{\chg(w)} .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[bin probabilities]\label{thm:pmf}
Let $N\ge1$. Then $P_N(0)=P_N(N)=\tfrac12p^{N-1}$, and for $1\le k\le N-1$
\[
P_N(k)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}W(N,k,j)\,p^{N-1-j}q^{\,j},
\]
where, writing $a=k$ and $b=N-k$,
\[
W(N,k,2i-1)=2\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i-1},\qquad
W(N,k,2i)=\binom{a-1}{i}\binom{b-1}{i-1}+\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i}
\]
is the number of words with $a$ letters $R$, $b$ letters $L$ and exactly $j$ direction changes.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{eq:wordprob} it suffices to count words. A word with $j$ changes consists of $j+1$ maximal
runs, alternately of $R$'s and of $L$'s. If $k\in\{0,N\}$ there is one word and it has no change.
Let $a,b\ge1$. If $j+1=2i$ there are $i$ runs of each letter and the first letter is free; the run
lengths form a composition of $a$ into $i$ parts and a composition of $b$ into $i$ parts, and there
are $\binom{a-1}{i-1}\binom{b-1}{i-1}$ such pairs. If $j+1=2i+1$ the word starts and ends with the
same letter; starting with $R$ gives $i+1$ runs of $R$ and $i$ runs of $L$, that is
$\binom{a-1}{i}\binom{b-1}{i-1}$ words, and starting with $L$ gives the other summand.
\end{proof}

For instance $P_2=(\tfrac p2,\,q,\,\tfrac p2)$, $P_3(1)=P_3(2)=\tfrac12(1-p^2)$ and
$P_4(2)=(1-p)(1-p+p^2)$.

\begin{proposition}[recurrence and generating function]\label{prop:gf}
Let $R_N(k)$ and $L_N(k)$ be the probabilities of being in bin $k$ after $N$ bounces with last bounce
$R$, respectively $L$. Then $R_1(1)=L_1(0)=\tfrac12$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dp}
R_N(k)=p\,R_{N-1}(k-1)+q\,L_{N-1}(k-1),\qquad L_N(k)=p\,L_{N-1}(k)+q\,R_{N-1}(k).
\end{equation}
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gf}
\sum_{N\ge0}\sum_{k=0}^{N}P_N(k)\,x^kt^N=\frac{1-(p-\tfrac12)(1+x)\,t}{1-p(1+x)\,t+(2p-1)\,x\,t^2},
\end{equation}
and for all $N\ge2$ and all $k$ (with $P_M(k)=0$ outside $0\le k\le M$)
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rec}
P_N(k)=p\bigl(P_{N-1}(k)+P_{N-1}(k-1)\bigr)-(2p-1)\,P_{N-2}(k-1).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The recursion \eqref{eq:dp} follows by conditioning on the last two bounces. Put $F_R=\sum R_N(k)x^kt^N$ and
$F_L=\sum L_N(k)x^kt^N$. Then \eqref{eq:dp} and the initial values give
$F_R=xt\bigl(\tfrac12+pF_R+qF_L\bigr)$ and $F_L=t\bigl(\tfrac12+pF_L+qF_R\bigr)$. The determinant of
this linear system is $(1-pxt)(1-pt)-q^2xt^2=1-p(1+x)t+(p^2-q^2)xt^2$, and $p^2-q^2=2p-1$. Cramer's
rule gives $F_R=\tfrac12xt\,(1-\rho t)/\Delta$ and $F_L=\tfrac12t\,(1-\rho xt)/\Delta$ with $\Delta$
the determinant, and $1+F_R+F_L$ simplifies to \eqref{eq:gf}. Multiplying \eqref{eq:gf} by $\Delta$
and reading off the coefficient of $x^kt^N$ for $N\ge2$ gives \eqref{eq:rec}.
\end{proof}

At $p=\tfrac12$ the last term of \eqref{eq:rec} vanishes and \eqref{eq:rec} is Pascal's rule with
weights $\tfrac12$. In terms of numerators, \eqref{eq:rec} reads
$n_N(k)=\alpha\bigl(n_{N-1}(k)+n_{N-1}(k-1)\bigr)-(\alpha^2-\beta^2)\,n_{N-2}(k-1)$ for $N\ge3$.

The same computation works for a board on which each peg offers $d\ge2$ directions: the first
bounce is uniform, and afterwards the ball repeats its direction with probability $p$ and takes each
other direction with probability $q/(d-1)$. For $d=3$ this is a tetrahedral pile of pegs, with the
bins of layer $N$ indexed by $(k_1,k_2,k_3)$, $k_1+k_2+k_3=N$.

\begin{proposition}[$d$ directions]\label{prop:dgf}
Let $P_N(k_1,\dots,k_d)$ be the probability that direction $i$ is taken $k_i$ times. Put
$\sigma=p-\frac{q}{d-1}=\frac{dp-1}{d-1}$ and $A=\sum_{i=1}^d\frac{x_it}{1-\sigma x_it}$. Then
\[
\sum_{N\ge0}\sum_{k_1+\dots+k_d=N}P_N(k_1,\dots,k_d)\,x_1^{k_1}\cdots x_d^{k_d}\,t^N
=1+\frac{A/d}{1-\frac{q}{d-1}A}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $F_i$ be the generating function of the runs whose last direction is $i$, and $S=\sum_iF_i$.
Conditioning on the last bounce, $F_i=x_it\bigl(\tfrac1d+pF_i+\tfrac{q}{d-1}(S-F_i)\bigr)$, that is
$F_i(1-\sigma x_it)=x_it\bigl(\tfrac1d+\tfrac{q}{d-1}S\bigr)$. Summing over $i$ gives
$S=A\bigl(\tfrac1d+\tfrac{q}{d-1}S\bigr)$, and the generating function is $1+S$.
\end{proof}

For $d=2$, $x_1=x$, $x_2=1$ this reduces to \eqref{eq:gf}, since then $\sigma=\rho$.

\begin{proposition}[finite bin probabilities in $d$ directions]\label{prop:dpmf}
Put $r=q/(d-1)$ and $\sigma=p-r$. For $N\ge1$, let $k=(k_1,\ldots,k_d)$ have
nonnegative integer coordinates summing to $N$, and put $H=\{i:k_i>0\}$. Sum over
$m_i=0$ for $i\notin H$ and $1\le m_i\le k_i$ for $i\in H$, and set $M=\sum_i m_i$.
Then
\[
P_N(k)=\frac1d\sum_m\frac{M!}{\prod_i m_i!}\,
r^{M-1}\sigma^{N-M}\prod_{i\in H}\binom{k_i-1}{m_i-1}.
\]
All exponents are nonnegative, and $0^0=1$. Set $P_0(0,\ldots,0)=1$ separately.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
With $z_i=x_it$, Proposition~\ref{prop:dgf} becomes the formal power series
\[
1+\frac1d\sum_{M\ge1}r^{M-1}
\left(\sum_i\frac{z_i}{1-\sigma z_i}\right)^M.
\]
Expand by the multinomial theorem and use, for $k\ge m\ge1$,
\[
[z^k]\left(\frac{z}{1-\sigma z}\right)^m
=\binom{k-1}{m-1}\sigma^{k-m}.
\]
For $m=0$ only $k=0$ contributes. Multiplying the coefficients proves the formula.
\end{proof}

At $d=3$ this is a triple sum, with the support convention covering edge bins.
At $p=1/d$, $\sigma=0$ and only $M=N$ survives, giving the ordinary multinomial law.
At $p=1$, only $M=1$ survives, giving mass $1/d$ at each $Ne_i$.
The formula is valid also at $p=0$; for $\sigma<0$ its terms can cancel, so dynamic
programming or exact arithmetic is preferable numerically. This geometry assumes fixed global
directions and uniform switching to the other directions; a different physical boundary or
orientation rule need not have this law.


\section{The numerator tables and lattice paths}\label{sec:tables}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:num}
Let $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ with integers $\alpha,\beta\ge0$, not both zero. For $N\ge1$,
\[
n_N(k)=\sum_{w}\alpha^{\rep(w)}\beta^{\chg(w)},
\]
the sum over the words with $k$ letters $R$ and $N-k$ letters $L$. In particular the numerators are
integers, and
\[
\sum_{a,b\ge0}n_{a+b}(a)\,x^ay^b=
\frac{1-(\alpha-1)(x+y)+(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha+\beta-2)\,xy}{1-\alpha(x+y)+(\alpha^2-\beta^2)\,xy}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Multiply \eqref{eq:wordprob} by $2(\alpha+\beta)^{N-1}$. For the generating function let $F_R,F_L$
collect the weighted words ending in $R$, in $L$; then $F_R=x(1+\alpha F_R+\beta F_L)$ and
$F_L=y(1+\alpha F_L+\beta F_R)$, and solving as in Proposition~\ref{prop:gf} gives the stated
value of $1+F_R+F_L$.
\end{proof}

For $(\alpha,\beta)=(2,1)$ the generating function is $(1-x)(1-y)/(1-2x-2y+3xy)$, and for
$(\alpha,\beta)=(1,2)$ it is $(1-xy)/(1-x-y-3xy)$. These are the generating functions recorded in
A035002 (by Kruchinin and by Somos) and in A348595 (by Mathar) \cite{oeisA035002,oeisA348595}, which already identifies the
tables. We prove the identifications directly from the definitions of the two sequences, which
shows where the weights $2$ come from.

\begin{theorem}[$p=\tfrac23$ and rook paths]\label{thm:rook}
Let $T(a,b)$ be the number of ways to move a rook from $(0,0)$ to $(a,b)$ by moves that increase one
coordinate by a positive amount. Then for $p=\tfrac23$, $n_N(k)=T(k,N-k)$ for all $N\ge0$ and
$0\le k\le N$. In the indexing of A035002, whose array starts at $(1,1)$, this is the entry $(k+1,N-k+1)$, so
Kagey's $p=\tfrac23$ table read by rows is A035002 read by antidiagonals.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A035002 is defined by the rule that each entry is the sum of all entries to its left and all entries
above it, which is the recursion for $T$ obtained by removing the last rook move (the OEIS entry
records this interpretation). Given a rook path to $(a,b)$ with $a+b=N\ge1$, replace each move of
length $\ell$ to the right by $R^\ell$ and each move of length $\ell$ upward by $L^\ell$. This
gives a word $w$ with $a$ letters $R$ and $b$ letters $L$, together with the set of positions
$i<N$ at which one move ends and the next begins. Such a cut is forced wherever
$w_i\ne w_{i+1}$ and is optional wherever $w_i=w_{i+1}$, and every choice of optional cuts comes from
exactly one rook path. Hence, $T(a,b)=\sum_w2^{\rep(w)}$, which is $n_N(a)$ for
$(\alpha,\beta)=(2,1)$ by Proposition~\ref{prop:num}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[$p=\tfrac13$ and Mathar's blocked walks]\label{thm:mathar}
Call a lattice point $(x,y)$ \emph{blocked} if $x\equiv y\equiv1$ or $x\equiv y\equiv2\pmod 3$. Let
$M(a,b)$ be the number of walks from $(0,0)$ to $(3a,3b)$ with unit steps right and up that visit no
blocked point. Then for $p=\tfrac13$, $n_N(k)=M(k,N-k)$. The array $M$ is symmetric, and A348595
lists its lower triangle $M(n,k)$, $0\le k\le n$; this is the sense in which that entry is
``related'' to the $p=\tfrac13$ table.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Reduce coordinates modulo $3$. The allowed residues are $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, $(2,0)$, $(2,1)$, $(0,1)$,
$(0,2)$, $(1,2)$. Call a point with residue $(0,0)$ a \emph{corner}, and let $(X,Y)$ count how many
times the walk has crossed a vertical line $x\equiv0$, respectively a horizontal line $y\equiv0$,
by a step landing on it. From a corner the walk steps right or up. After a right step it is at
residue $(1,0)$, and the step up is blocked, so it continues to $(2,0)$; call this state $\mathsf A$.
From $\mathsf A$ a right step lands on a corner and increases $X$; an up step leads to $(2,1)$,
where up is blocked, and then right to residue $(0,1)$, which increases $X$, and then (right being
blocked) up to $(0,2)$; call this state $\mathsf B$. By symmetry, from a corner an up step leads
to $\mathsf B$ without changing $(X,Y)$, and from $\mathsf B$ the walk either reaches a corner or
reaches $\mathsf A$, increasing $Y$ by one in both cases. Hence, every walk from $\mathsf A$ emits one letter $R$ (an increase of $X$) and then is at a corner
or at $\mathsf B$; every walk from $\mathsf B$ emits one letter $L$ and then is at a corner or at
$\mathsf A$; and a corner leads to $\mathsf A$ or to $\mathsf B$ without emitting anything. A walk from the corner $(0,0)$ to the corner $(3a,3b)$ therefore
determines a word $w$ with $a$ letters $R$ and $b$ letters $L$: the letter $w_i$ is emitted from
$\mathsf A$ if it is $R$ and from $\mathsf B$ if it is $L$. Between $w_i$ and $w_{i+1}$ the walk goes
from one emitting state to the next. If $w_i=w_{i+1}$ it must pass through a corner (one way). If
$w_i\ne w_{i+1}$ it may pass through a corner or go directly (two ways). The start and the end are
forced. Hence $M(a,b)=\sum_w2^{\chg(w)}$, which is $n_N(a)$ for $(\alpha,\beta)=(1,2)$. In Mathar's note
\cite{mathar2022} attached to A348595 the ``squares'' of the sequence name are the lattice sites
$(x,y)$ themselves, as his recurrences show, so $M$ is his count; a direct count of the walks also
reproduces the published terms (Section~\ref{sec:verify}).
\end{proof}

For general $p=\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$, Proposition~\ref{prop:num} says that $n_N(k)$ counts monotone
lattice paths from $(0,0)$ to $(k,N-k)$ in which each straight continuation is colored in one of
$\alpha$ ways and each corner in one of $\beta$ ways. This is the general answer to R3.

\section{Variance and the normal limit}\label{sec:clt}

Write $e_i=+1$ if the $i$-th bounce is $R$ and $e_i=-1$ otherwise, $X_N=e_1+\dots+e_N$, and
$K_N=(N+X_N)/2$ for the bin index. Then $e_{i+1}=e_is_i$, where $s_1,s_2,\dots$ are independent of
each other and of $e_1$, with $\Prob(s_i=1)=p$ and $\Prob(s_i=-1)=q$, so $\E s_i=\rho$.

\begin{proposition}[mean and variance]\label{prop:var}
$\E K_N=N/2$, and for $p<1$
\[
\Var K_N=\frac14\Bigl(N\,\frac{1+\rho}{1-\rho}-\frac{2\rho\,(1-\rho^N)}{(1-\rho)^2}\Bigr)
=\frac{Np}{4(1-p)}-\frac{(2p-1)\bigl(1-(2p-1)^N\bigr)}{8(1-p)^2}.
\]
For $p=1$, $\Var K_N=N^2/4$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Note that $\E e_1=0$ and $e_{i+m}=e_is_i\cdots s_{i+m-1}$, so $\E e_i=0$ and
$\E e_ie_{i+m}=\rho^m$. Hence,
$\Var X_N=N+2\sum_{m=1}^{N-1}(N-m)\rho^m$, and summing the two geometric series gives the first
form; the second uses $(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)=p/q$ and $1-\rho=2q$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[normal limit]\label{thm:clt}
Let $0<p<1$. Then $(K_N-N/2)/\sqrt N$ converges in distribution to the normal law with mean $0$ and
variance $p/(4(1-p))$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi_N(\theta)=\E e^{i\theta X_N}$ and let $v_N$ be the column vector with entries
$\E\bigl[e^{i\theta X_N};e_N=+1\bigr]$ and $\E\bigl[e^{i\theta X_N};e_N=-1\bigr]$. By \eqref{eq:dp},
$v_{N+1}=Mv_N$ with
\[
M=\begin{pmatrix}pe^{i\theta}&qe^{i\theta}\\qe^{-i\theta}&pe^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\operatorname{tr}M=2p\cos\theta,\quad\det M=p^2-q^2=\rho .
\]
The eigenvalues are $\lambda_\pm(\theta)=p\cos\theta\pm\sqrt{q^2-p^2\sin^2\theta}$, using
$p^2-\rho=q^2$. For $|\sin\theta|<q/p$ they are real and distinct, so
$\varphi_N=c_+\lambda_+^{N-1}+c_-\lambda_-^{N-1}$ for $N\ge1$, with $c_\pm(\theta)$ determined by
$\varphi_1=\cos\theta$ and $\varphi_2=p\cos2\theta+q$:
\[
c_+=\frac{\varphi_2-\lambda_-\cos\theta}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-},\qquad c_-=\cos\theta-c_+ .
\]
As $\theta\to0$ we have $\lambda_+\to1$, $\lambda_-\to\rho$, $c_+\to1$, $c_-\to0$, and
\[
\lambda_+(\theta)=p\bigl(1-\tfrac{\theta^2}2\bigr)+q\Bigl(1-\frac{p^2\theta^2}{2q^2}\Bigr)+O(\theta^4)
=1-\frac{p}{2q}\,\theta^2+O(\theta^4).
\]
Fix $s\in\mathbb R$ and put $\theta=s/\sqrt N$. Since $|\rho|<1$, $c_-\lambda_-^{N-1}\to0$, and
$\lambda_+^{N-1}\to\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{p}{2q}s^2\bigr)$. Hence, $\varphi_N(s/\sqrt N)\to
\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{p}{2q}s^2\bigr)$, and by L\'evy's continuity theorem
\cite[Theorem~26.3]{billingsley1995} $X_N/\sqrt N$ converges to the normal law with variance
$p/q$. Finally $K_N-N/2=X_N/2$.
\end{proof}

For $p=1$ the ball is in bin $0$ or bin $N$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, and for $p=0$ it is
in a middle bin, so there is no normal limit in these two cases.

\begin{remark}[cylinder]\label{rem:cyl}
On a cylinder whose rows have $c$ pegs the local rule is unchanged, so the position is $X_N$ modulo
$2c$ (in half-peg units), and the discrete Fourier coefficients of its law are
$\varphi_N(\pi l/c)$, $0\le l<2c$. The formula $\varphi_N=c_+\lambda_+^{N-1}+c_-\lambda_-^{N-1}$
holds whenever $\lambda_+\neq\lambda_-$. For $0<p<1$ and $\theta\notin\pi\Z$ both eigenvalues have
modulus less than $1$: real eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda_\pm|\le p|\cos\theta|+q$, and complex
ones have modulus $\sqrt\rho$. The coefficients at $l=0$ and $l=c$ are $1$ and $(-1)^N$. Hence, on
the cylinder the law approaches the uniform law on the $c$ positions of the correct parity
geometrically fast, and there is no normal shape in the limit.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[tetrahedron]\label{rem:tetra}
For the board with $d$ directions of Proposition~\ref{prop:dgf} let $K_a$ be the number of bounces in
direction $a$. The direction chain has transition matrix $\sigma I+(1-\sigma)\tfrac1dJ$ with $J$ the
all-ones matrix, so the indicator of direction $a$ at time $i$ and of direction $b$ at time $i+m$ have
covariance $\sigma^m\bigl(\tfrac{\delta_{ab}}d-\tfrac1{d^2}\bigr)$, and as in
Proposition~\ref{prop:var}
\[
\Cov(K_a,K_b)=\Bigl(\frac{\delta_{ab}}d-\frac1{d^2}\Bigr)
\Bigl(N\,\frac{1+\sigma}{1-\sigma}-\frac{2\sigma(1-\sigma^N)}{(1-\sigma)^2}\Bigr),
\qquad \frac{1+\sigma}{1-\sigma}=\frac{d-2+dp}{d\,(1-p)} .
\]
These formulas hold for $p<1$; at $p=1$ the covariance is
$N^2(\delta_{ab}/d-1/d^2)$. The asymptotic multiplier of the multinomial covariance is
$(d-2+dp)/(d(1-p))$, which is $(1+3p)/(3(1-p))$ for the tetrahedron.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[simplex normal limit]\label{thm:dclt}
Let $d\ge3$ and $0\le p<1$, or $d=2$ and $0<p<1$. Set
\[
\pi=(1/d,\ldots,1/d),\quad C=\frac1d I-\frac1{d^2}J,
\quad c=\frac{1+\sigma}{1-\sigma}=\frac{d-2+dp}{d(1-p)}.
\]
Then $(K_N-N\pi)/\sqrt N$ converges in distribution to $\mathcal N_d(0,cC)$.
This Gaussian is supported on the sum-zero hyperplane and is nondegenerate there.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $\Pi=\mathbf1\pi$ and $T=\sigma I+(1-\sigma)\Pi$. Fix a real vector $a$ and
put $f_i=a_i-d^{-1}\sum_j a_j$, so $\pi f=0$ and $Tf=\sigma f$.
For $D(t)=\operatorname{diag}(e^{itf_i})$ and $B(t)=TD(t)$, stationarity gives
\[
\E\exp\left(it\sum_{j=1}^N f(X_j)\right)
=\pi D(t)B(t)^{N-1}\mathbf1.
\]
At zero, $B(0)=T$ has the simple eigenvalue $1$ and all other eigenvalues equal
$\sigma$, with $|\sigma|<1$. Analytic perturbation gives an eigenvalue $\lambda(t)$
and right eigenvector $h(t)$ with $\lambda(0)=1$, $h(0)=\mathbf1$ and $\pi h(t)=1$.
The complementary spectral part is uniformly exponentially small for sufficiently small
$t$: choose a circle of radius $\eta<1$ enclosing the complementary spectrum but not
$\lambda(t)$ and use the resolvent formula for matrix powers. Thus the characteristic
function equals $b(t)\lambda(t)^{N-1}+O(\eta^N)$ with $b(t)\to1$.

Differentiate $Bh=\lambda h$ at zero. Since $B'(0)=iT\operatorname{diag}(f)$,
\[
\lambda'(0)=0,\qquad (I-T)h'(0)=i\sigma f,
\qquad h'(0)=\frac{i\sigma}{1-\sigma}f.
\]
Differentiating again and multiplying by $\pi$ gives
\[
\lambda''(0)=\pi B''(0)\mathbf1+2\pi B'(0)h'(0)
=-\frac{1+\sigma}{1-\sigma}\pi(f^2)=-c\,\pi(f^2).
\]
Hence $\lambda(t)=1-c\pi(f^2)t^2/2+O(t^3)$. Substitute $t=s/\sqrt N$ to obtain
the scalar normal characteristic function with variance $c\pi(f^2)=ca^{\mathsf T}Ca$.
L\'evy's theorem and the Cram\'er--Wold device give the result.
\end{proof}

For $d=3$ the first two centered coordinates have limiting covariance
\[
\frac{1+3p}{27(1-p)}\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix};
\]
the third is minus their sum. Unlike the binary case, $p=0$ is included for $d\ge3$.
At $d=2,p=0$ bounded alternation gives the zero limit after division by $\sqrt N$.
At $p=1$ the centered sequence on this scale is not tight. This theorem asserts weak
convergence, not a local approximation for individual bin probabilities.


\section{When does the middle equal the extremes?}\label{sec:middle}

Let $N\ge2$ and let $m_N=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ be a middle bin (for odd $N$ the two middle bins have
equal probability by symmetry). Put $u=q/p$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:SN}
\frac{P_N(m_N)}{P_N(0)}=S_N(u):=\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}W(N,m_N,j)\,u^j ,
\end{equation}
a polynomial in $u$ with nonnegative integer coefficients and $W(N,m_N,1)=2$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:root}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For every $N\ge2$ there is exactly one $p=p_N\in[0,1]$ with $P_N(m_N)=P_N(0)$. The middle is
more likely than the extremes for $p<p_N$ and less likely for $p>p_N$.
\item $p_2=\tfrac23$, $p_3=1/\sqrt2$, $p_4$ is the real root of $3p^3-4p^2+4p-2$, and
$p_N<p_{N+1}$ for all $N\ge2$.
\item $p_N\to1$, and more precisely $1-p_N\sim(\log N)/N$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) At $p=0$ the extremes have probability $0$ and the middle does not. For $p>0$ the equation is
$S_N(u)=1$ by \eqref{eq:SN}. $S_N$ is continuous and strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$ with
$S_N(0)=0$ and $S_N(u)\ge2u$, so there is exactly one root $u_N\in(0,\tfrac12]$, and $p=1/(1+u)$ is a
decreasing bijection from $[0,\infty)$ to $(0,1]$.

(b) $S_2=2u$, $S_3=2u+u^2$ and $S_4=2u+2u^2+2u^3$ give the three values; for $p_4$ substitute
$u=(1-p)/p$ in $S_4(u)=1$. For monotonicity we compare coefficients. If $N=2m$ then the middle
$(a,b)=(m,m)$ becomes $(m,m+1)$ for $N+1$, and if $N=2m+1$ then $(m,m+1)$ becomes $(m+1,m+1)$. In
both cases one of $a,b$ grows by one and the other is unchanged, the formulas of
Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf} are symmetric in $(a,b)$, and every binomial coefficient $\binom{a-1}{\cdot}$
is nondecreasing in $a$. So $W(N+1,m_{N+1},j)\ge W(N,m_N,j)$ for every $j$. The coefficient of $u^2$
is $a+b-2=N-2$, which increases strictly. Hence, $S_{N+1}(u)>S_N(u)$ for $u>0$, so
$u_{N+1}<u_N$.

(c) Let $a=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ and $b=\lceil N/2\rceil$, and fix $\varepsilon\in(0,\tfrac12)$.

\emph{Lower bound for $u_N$.} Using $\binom{a-1}{r},\binom{b-1}{r}\le b^r/r!$ and writing $x=bu$,
the terms of $S_N(u)$ with $j=2i-1$ are at most $\tfrac2b\,x\bigl(x^{i-1}/(i-1)!\bigr)^2$ and those
with $j=2i$ are at most $\tfrac2b\,\tfrac{x^i}{i!}\tfrac{x^i}{(i-1)!}$. Since
$\sum_r(x^r/r!)^2\le e^{2x}$ and $\sum_i\tfrac{x^i}{i!}\tfrac{x^i}{(i-1)!}\le e^x\cdot xe^x$,
\[
S_N(u)\le\frac{4x}{b}\,e^{2x}.
\]
If $x\le(\tfrac12-\varepsilon)\log b$ the right side is at most $2b^{-2\varepsilon}\log b$, which is
less than $1$ for large $b$. Hence, $b\,u_N>(\tfrac12-\varepsilon)\log b$ for large $N$.

\emph{Upper bound for $u_N$.} Let $u^*=(\tfrac12+\varepsilon)(\log a)/a$, $y=au^*$ and
$r=\lfloor y\rfloor$. Keep only the term $j=2r+1$ of $S_N(u^*)$ and use
$\binom{b-1}{r}\ge\binom{a-1}{r}\ge(a-r)^r/r!$:
\[
S_N(u^*)\ge2u^*\Bigl(\frac{(a-r)^r(u^*)^r}{r!}\Bigr)^2
=\frac{2y}{a}\Bigl(1-\frac ra\Bigr)^{2r}\Bigl(\frac{y^r}{r!}\Bigr)^2 .
\]
From $\log r!\le\int_1^r\log t\,dt+\log r$ we get $r!\le e\,r\,(r/e)^r$, so
$y^r/r!\ge r^r/r!\ge e^{r-1}/r\ge e^{y-2}/y$. Also $(1-r/a)^{2r}\ge1-2r^2/a\to1$. Therefore
\[
S_N(u^*)\ge\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr)\frac{2e^{2y-4}}{a\,y}=\bigl(1-o(1)\bigr)\frac{2e^{-4}a^{2\varepsilon}}{y}\to\infty ,
\]
so $S_N(u^*)>1$ and $u_N<u^*$ for large $N$.

Since $a,b=N/2+O(1)$, the two bounds give $u_N\sim\log(N/2)/N\sim(\log N)/N$, and
$1-p_N=u_N/(1+u_N)\sim u_N$.
\end{proof}

In Kagey's terms: for the $(2n+1)$-st row ($N=2n$) the unique value is $p_{2n}$, and the least of
these is $p_2=\tfrac23$, which is why the third row of his $p=\tfrac23$ table is $2,2,2$. For the
$(2n)$-th row ($N=2n-1$, $n\ge2$) the least is $p_3=1/\sqrt2$; the second row is $1,1$ for every
$p$. There is no greatest value, since $p_N\uparrow1$. The tenth row has $p_9=0.82829\ldots$, just
below $\tfrac56$, which is why the extremes and the middle are nearly level in his third histogram.

\begin{theorem}[uniform Bessel approximation]\label{thm:bessel}
Put $h=N/2$, $J(x)=I_0(2x)+I_1(2x)$ and $F(x)=2xJ(x)$.
For every $N\ge2$ and $x>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uniformbessel}
0\le1-\frac{hS_N(x/h)}{F(x)}\le\frac{x(x+1)}h.
\end{equation}
Let $x_B$ be the unique positive root of $F(x_B)=h$, and put
$u_B=x_B/h$, $u_N=(1-p_N)/p_N$. Then
\[
0\le u_N-u_B=O((\log N)^2/N^2).
\]
Writing $L=\log N$ and $c_0=\tfrac12\log(\pi/8)$, we have
\begin{align}
N(1-p_N)&=L-\tfrac12\log L+c_0
+O\left(\frac{\log L}{L}\right),\label{eq:secondorder}\\
N(1-p_N)&=L-\tfrac12\log L+c_0
+\frac{\tfrac14\log L-\tfrac12c_0+\tfrac18}{L}
+O\left(\frac{(\log L)^2}{L^2}\right).\label{eq:thirdorder}
\end{align}
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $a=\lfloor N/2\rfloor$, $b=\lceil N/2\rceil$, and define
\[
A_r=\frac{r!}{h^r}\binom{a-1}{r},\qquad
B_r=\frac{r!}{h^r}\binom{b-1}{r}\quad(r\ge0).
\]
With $(v)_+=\max(v,0)$, these have the product expressions
\[
A_r=\prod_{k=1}^r\left(1-\frac{h-a+k}{h}\right)_+,
\qquad B_r=\prod_{k=1}^r\left(1-\frac{h-b+k}{h}\right)_+.
\]
They lie in $[0,1]$, including beyond their finite support, where a factor is zero.
For nonnegative $t_j$, the inequality $1-\prod_j(1-t_j)_+\le\sum_jt_j$
holds even when some $t_j>1$. Since $a+b=2h$, it gives
\[
0\le1-A_rB_r\le\frac{r(r+1)}h.
\]
The deficits for $A_{r+1}B_r$ and $A_rB_{r+1}$ are at most
$((r+1)^2+h-a)/h$ and $((r+1)^2+h-b)/h$, respectively. Averaging cancels
the parity offsets, so
\[
0\le1-\frac{A_{r+1}B_r+A_rB_{r+1}}2\le\frac{(r+1)^2}h.
\]
The run-count formula, with $u=x/h$, becomes
\[
S_N(u)=2u\left[\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r}}{(r!)^2}A_rB_r
+\sum_{r\ge0}\frac{x^{2r+1}}{r!(r+1)!}
\frac{A_{r+1}B_r+A_rB_{r+1}}2\right].
\]
Dropping the $A,B$ factors yields $2uJ(x)=F(x)/h$, using the Bessel series
\cite[Eq.~10.25.2]{dlmf}. Index shifts in those absolutely convergent series give
\begin{align*}
\sum_{r\ge0}r(r+1)\frac{x^{2r}}{(r!)^2}&=x^2I_0(2x)+xI_1(2x),\\
\sum_{r\ge0}(r+1)^2\frac{x^{2r+1}}{r!(r+1)!}&=x^2I_1(2x)+xI_0(2x).
\end{align*}
Their sum is $x(x+1)J(x)$, proving \eqref{eq:uniformbessel}, including the tail
beyond the finite polynomial support. In particular the relative error is
$O((\log N)^2/N)$ uniformly for $0<x\le C\log N$ for each fixed $C$.

Now let $x_N=hu_N$. Theorem~\ref{thm:root} gives $x_N\sim\tfrac12\log N$.
Differentiation gives $F'(x)=2F(x)+2I_0(2x)$, hence $F'/F\ge2$.
Set $\delta_N=x_N(x_N+1)/h$. For large $N$, $\delta_N<1$, and
\eqref{eq:uniformbessel} at the root gives
\[
h\le F(x_N)\le\frac{h}{1-\delta_N},\qquad
0\le x_N-x_B\le-\tfrac12\log(1-\delta_N)
=O((\log N)^2/N).
\]
Divide by $h$ for the assertion about $u$. Moreover,
\[
N(1-p_N)=\frac{2x_N}{1+x_N/h}=2x_B+O((\log N)^2/N).
\]
Put $z=2x_B$. The standard large-argument expansion
\cite[Eq.~10.40.1]{dlmf} is
\[
I_0(z)+I_1(z)=\frac{2e^z}{\sqrt{2\pi z}}
\left(1-\frac1{8z}+O(z^{-2})\right).
\]
Since $z(I_0(z)+I_1(z))=N/2$, taking logarithms gives
\[
z+\tfrac12\log z=L+c_0+\frac1{8z}+O(z^{-2}).
\]
First $z=L+O(\log L)$, then substitution proves \eqref{eq:secondorder}.
For one further term put $z=L+d+e/L$, where $d=-\tfrac12\log L+c_0$.
Matching coefficients of $1/L$ gives $e=\tfrac18-d/2$. The residual is
$O((\log L)^2/L^2)$; the derivative of $z+\tfrac12\log z$ is bounded below
by one, so inversion preserves that order. This proves \eqref{eq:thirdorder}.
\end{proof}

The ratios $u_B/u_N$ are $0.9761$, $0.9864$, $0.9924$ at $N=100,200,400$ and
$0.99579$, $0.99769$, $0.99874$ at $N=800,1600,3200$. The latter three were
predicted within registered intervals before computation (\texttt{data/ledger.md}).
Theorem~\ref{thm:bessel} now supplies the proof previously missing from that heuristic.

\begin{proposition}[adjacent bins and global extrema]\label{prop:adjacent}
For $N\ge2$, an adjacent bin has the same probability as an endpoint precisely at
\[
p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}=\frac{1+\sqrt{N-1}}{2+\sqrt{N-1}}.
\]
For $0<p<1$, endpoints attain the global minimum if $p<p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}$;
bins $1,N-1$ attain it if $p>p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}$; both sets do at equality.
At $p=p_N$ the middle and endpoints attain the global maximum and bins $1,N-1$
attain the global minimum, allowing additional ties in small rows.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
A word with one $R$ has one change in two cases and two changes in $N-2$ cases.
Thus $P_N(1)/P_N(0)=2u+(N-2)u^2$, and its positive root at level one is
$u=1/(1+\sqrt{N-1})$, including $N=2$. This yields the stated $p$ and sign change.
Dekking and Kong~\cite[Proposition~4.3]{dekkingkong2011} prove log-concavity on the
interior bins. Symmetry therefore makes the middle maximal and bins $1,N-1$ minimal
among interior bins. Comparing those minima with the endpoints proves the claims.
\end{proof}

The adjacent-bin scale is $1-p_N^{\mathrm{adj}}\sim N^{-1/2}$, distinct from
$1-p_N\sim(\log N)/N$. At $p=0,1$ additional zero-probability ties occur.

\section{Computational verification}\label{sec:verify}

The script \texttt{verify.py} uses exact rational arithmetic except where it says otherwise. It
computes $P_N$ in three ways: the closed form of Theorem~\ref{thm:pmf}, the recursion
\eqref{eq:dp}, and enumeration of all $2^N$ words weighted by \eqref{eq:wordprob}. The three agree,
and rows sum to $1$, for $N\le16$ and $20$ values of $p$; since $P_N(k)$ is a polynomial of degree
less than $N$ in $p$, this is an identity in $p$. The closed form and the recursion also agree for
$N\le40$. The numerators reproduce rows $1$ to $9$ of the three tables in Kagey's figure. The
generating functions \eqref{eq:gf} and of Proposition~\ref{prop:num} and the recurrence
\eqref{eq:rec} hold to degree $16$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:dgf} is checked against enumeration
for $d=2$ ($N\le12$) and $d=3$ ($N\le8$). The $p=\tfrac23$ numerators equal the $60$ published terms
of A035002 and a direct count of rook paths for $N\le24$; the $p=\tfrac13$ numerators equal the
$55$ published terms of A348595 and a direct count of the blocked walks for $n,k\le12$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:var} agrees exactly with the moments of the computed distribution for
$N\le16$ and $N=25,40$, and Remark~\ref{rem:tetra} with enumeration of all $3^N$ words for $N\le9$.
In floating point, the eigenvalue formula for $\varphi_N$ agrees with the computed distribution to
$10^{-12}$, and at $N=2000$ the distribution function of $K_N$, computed by \eqref{eq:dp} and again
by \eqref{eq:rec}, is within $1.1\times10^{-4}$ of the normal one (with continuity correction) for
$p=\tfrac13,\tfrac23,\tfrac56$.

For Theorem~\ref{thm:root} the script checks the polynomials in (b) exactly, the coefficient
inequality for $N\le300$, the single sign change of $P_N(m_N)-P_N(0)$ on the grid $p=i/200$ for
$N\le14$, and the monotonicity of $p_N$ for $N\le121$. Each entry of Table~\ref{tab:pn} is the root
of $S_N$ found by bisection in integer arithmetic and confirmed by evaluating \eqref{eq:dp} exactly
at the two ten-digit neighbours of the root. The roots at $N=800,1600,3200$ are confirmed by the
floating-point recursion, and $x_B$ is computed by power series and again by quadrature. The run
takes between one and two minutes.

The supplementary script \texttt{check\_bessel.py} checks the uniform bound on
1,400 even/odd test cases and the quantitative root bound through $N=10^8$, using
80-digit decimal arithmetic. These are numerical corroborations, not interval-certified
computations. The script \texttt{verify\_geometry.py} compares 6,733 exact rational bin
probabilities in 237 distributions with an independent dynamic program; 158 distributions
also agree with exhaustive word enumeration. It verifies normalization, nonnegativity, means,
covariances and endpoint cases for $d=2,3,4$. The script \texttt{verify\_adjacent.py}
checks the adjacent-bin polynomial and the consequences for global extrema. All checks can
be run with \texttt{python verify\_all.py}.

The additional script \texttt{verify\_allbin.py} checks 5,476 coefficient comparisons
exactly, 264 all-bin Bessel inequalities, 28 Laguerre--Bessel inequalities, and crossing
expansions with 80-digit arithmetic, including highly unbalanced bins.
The script \texttt{verify\_periodic\_window.py} checks 66 cyclic polynomials against
exhaustive word enumeration, 120 periodic relative-error inequalities, and root/window
samples through $N=10^{12}$. The script \texttt{verify\_simplex\_threshold.py} checks
the positive refresh formula against enumeration, strict balancing transfers, the small
edge-mode counterexample, and face-center crossing expansions. Numerical asymptotic
checks are corroboration, not interval certificates or substitutes for the analytic proofs.
The scripts \texttt{verify\_frontier\_boundary.py} and
\texttt{verify\_frontier\_modes.py} check the added uniform estimate, rare-coordinate
asymptotics, spatial profile and weak-field phase assertions against finite calculations.
Detailed records are saved in the accompanying JSON files under \texttt{data/}.
The script \texttt{verify\_vertex\_completion.py} adds 462 exact identity
comparisons, 200 rational elasticity checks, 16 explicit root-bracket checks,
and four high-precision growing-rare-count stress cases. The runner now includes
ten scripts. These finite checks do not formalize the uniform analytic proof.

The \texttt{lean/} directory contains separately compiled Lean proofs of finite product
and algebraic ingredients. It does not formalize the infinite Bessel sums, analytic
inversion, Laplace expansion, or complete asymptotic theorems. Its coverage note and
compiler/axiom logs give the exact formal statements checked.

\section{Further questions and scope}\label{sec:remarks}

The original distribution, lattice-walk and central-crossing questions are answered,
as are the cylinder pushforward and the explicitly specified symmetric tetrahedral model.
The extensions give quantitative thresholds for all binary bin distances and all fixed
simplex face dimensions, together with free/periodic boundary comparisons and critical
mode windows. The extensions also supply a composition-uniform relative bound,
mixed-face boundary crossovers, spatial superlevel volumes and weak-field face phases.
The leading near-vertex crossing is now uniform for every fixed number of rare
colors with total $A=o(b)$. Uniform higher-order corrections in that whole regime,
and a growing number of colors, are not established here.
Their proposed novelty lies in these refinements, subject to the prior-art
qualifications above. A complete multistate finite-size mode diagram outside the proved
window remains open here, as do nonuniform switching and alternative geometric rules.
The finite-size edge-mode example prevents promoting the asymptotic vertex/center
dichotomy to an unrestricted theorem.

The verification scripts (plain Python, standard library only) and prediction ledger
accompany this document. The ledger preserves the original predictions and adds the later
proof status. The repository's \texttt{PUBLICATION.md} records the separate literature and
venue assessment; it is not a claim of acceptance or novelty.
