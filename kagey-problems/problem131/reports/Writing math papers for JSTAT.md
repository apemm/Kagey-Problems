## Journal of Applied Probability rejected the significance, and JSTAT judges significance the same way

Journal of Applied Probability (JAP) gave no referee reports. The editor's whole objection was about where the problem came from and how deep it is: the problem was "taken from a researcher's online list of open problems ... and solved (with some help of AI)", and was "not at the level of the Applied Probability journals" (checked in this review). That is a desk rejection on framing. It says nothing about whether the proofs are correct. JSTAT's filter works the same way. Its acceptance criteria are "scientific quality, originality and relevance", its editors are told to apply a strict quality filter, and its About page states that "Serial publication should be avoided" ([JSTAT About](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=about)). Referees are asked whether a paper contains "significant new material" of "scientific interest" on subject matter the journal covers ([JSTAT For Referees](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=referee)). The editor's desk-reject option, "Not suitable for the journal", is meant for articles "clearly suited for another journal or articles of an obviously poor quality", needs only a brief reason, and **cannot be appealed** ([JSTAT For Editors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=editor)).

The lesson from JAP carries over directly. An editor who opens the PDF and sees "Kagey's Problem 131", a six-paper series, or a long AI note on page 1 will classify the paper before reading the theorem. An editor who sees a finite Ising chain, a JSTAT 2024 citation, and a sharper answer to a question that paper asked will send it to referees. Everything below is about controlling that first impression without hiding anything.

## JSTAT's controlled keywords decide which editor reads the paper

JSTAT's scope is "statistical physics", defined loosely as the fields covered by the "Statistical Physics" conferences ([IOP Publishing Support](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/journals/journal-of-statistical-mechanics-theory-and-experiment/)). The journal says its scope "should not be regarded as fixed" and lists no topics as out of scope ([JSTAT About](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=about)). It has five sections: quantum and integrable; classical equilibrium and non-equilibrium; disordered; interdisciplinary; and biological. None of them is for probability or mathematical physics ([JSTAT keyword list](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=kwList)). So a rigorous paper is in scope only to the extent that its subject is a stat-mech subject. The Ising retitling is what makes Paper A's subject one.

**Authors must choose 1 to 4 keywords from the controlled list, and the submission system uses them to route the paper to an editor automatically** ([JSTAT For Authors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=author); [JSTAT Flowchart](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=flow)). The keywords planned for the paper itself ("Ising chain; finite-size effects; magnetization distribution; persistent random walk; Markov binomial distribution; modified Bessel functions; computer-assisted proof") can stay in the manuscript as descriptive author keywords. They are not the submission keywords. "Random walks", "persistent random walk" and "probability" are not on JSTAT's list at all. The relevant terms that are on the list are "Exact results", "Correlation functions", "Fluctuation phenomena", "Stochastic processes", "Persistence" and "First Passage" (classical and interdisciplinary sections), plus "Rigorous results in statistical mechanics" and "Solvable lattice models" (listed under the quantum and integrable section) ([JSTAT keyword list](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=kwList)). In JSTAT, "Persistence" and "First Passage" mean survival and first-passage problems, and Paper A's crossing is neither, so those two would be misleading.

For the Ising framing, the best set is **Exact results; Rigorous results in statistical mechanics; Solvable lattice models; Fluctuation phenomena**, with the classical section marked as preferred. There is one routing risk: two of these keywords sit in the quantum and integrable section, and how JSTAT resolves a mixed set is not documented. Gastner's finite-Ising-chain JSTAT paper carried "Rigorous results in statistical mechanics, solvable lattice models" in its source ([arXiv:1502.02883](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02883)), which suggests the combination works in practice. If the author would rather be sure of a classical editor, "Stochastic processes" or "Correlation functions" can replace "Solvable lattice models".

On format, JSTAT prefers LaTeX with the IOP template ("JSTAT class"). It wants sections, references numbered in square brackets in order of citation, the .bbl file included with the source, every figure and table cited in the text, and a **Data/Software/Code Availability Statement at the end** ([JSTAT For Authors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=author)). The abstract must fit on the first page and should **contain no formulae and no references** (same source). The IOP rule for its other journals rescinds abstracts over 300 words ([IOP, J. Phys. A](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/journals/journal-of-physics-a-mathematical-and-theoretical/)). There is no length limit; editors decide whether the length is justified. At 28 pages, Paper A is in the normal range for comparable papers (see the next section).

Review is traditional and confidential. Author names are visible, referees do not sign their reports, and one editor handles each paper, sends it to "one or more" referees, and aims to finish within five weeks. Revisions are due within one month, and a cover letter answering the referees is mandatory at the revision stage ([JSTAT Flowchart](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=flow); [JSTAT For Authors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=author)). After an ordinary rejection the author may ask once for reconsideration on "serious and not polemic scientific reasons"; after "Not suitable" there is no appeal (same sources). If an arXiv ID is given, "the submitted version must be the same as the arXiv version" ([JSTAT For Authors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=author)). That is why every fix has to go into arXiv v2 before submission. Several things remain unknown: the APC amount, IOP's formal label for the review model, and whether there is a suggested-referee field. The iopscience pages that would answer these blocked automated access.

The AI rules have two layers. JSTAT requires authors "to declare and properly reference in detail the use of AI assisted technology", either in Methods or in the Acknowledgments ([JSTAT For Authors](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=author)). IOP's policy is stricter. It requires a **separate AI disclosure statement inside the Acknowledgements** that names the tool and version, describes how it was used, and explains "the extent to which the authors verified, validated and critically reviewed the AI-assisted outputs". It requires research-process use to be described in Methods as well. It treats fabricated references as strong evidence of irresponsible AI use. And it forbids authors to "Upload reports from reviewers to generative AI tools" or "Generate responses to reviewers" ([IOP ethical policy](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/ethical-policy-journals/); [IOP Generative AI tools](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/questions/generative-ai-tools/)). The last rule matters most for this project. However much AI helped produce the paper, the author must write any reply to JSTAT referees personally.

## Eleven of fourteen JSTAT exemplars state exact results without theorem environments

A parse of the arXiv LaTeX sources of 14 verified JSTAT papers on random walks, run-and-tumble particles and 1D chains found three styles. **Eleven, including every paper by Majumdar, Schehr, Dhar, Kundu, Evans and Godrèche-Luck in the sample, contain no theorem environments at all.** Results are "derived", stated as displayed formulas in "we show / we find" prose, and checked against simulations in figures, with long computations in appendices. Typical length is 20 to 36 arXiv pages, with 3 to 11 figures and 30 to 67 references ([arXiv:1711.08474](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08474); [arXiv:2107.00338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00338); [arXiv:2203.01102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01102)). Mori, Gradenigo and Majumdar put a section titled "The model and the summary of the main results" before any derivation ([arXiv:2107.00338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00338)). Godrèche and Luck use section titles such as "A key scaling result" in place of theorem labels, and they label exact and heuristic results separately in the abstract ([arXiv:2203.01102](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.01102)).

Theorem-proof papers do appear in JSTAT, but they are a minority. Coletti, Gava and Schütz (2017, 7 pp) and Coletti, de Lima and Gava (2019, 13 pp) use the full theorem apparatus, with "Main results" followed by "Proofs" and no figures ([arXiv:1707.06905](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06905); [arXiv:1908.09199](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.09199)). The most useful template for Paper A is **Mailler and Uribe Bravo (J. Stat. Mech. (2019) 093206)**. Their abstract names a claim from the physics literature ("analyzed in the physics literature ... where it is argued that ...") and then says "We prove rigorously" that claim. They also include a section titled "Main steps of the proofs and heuristics" ([arXiv:1810.02735](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.02735)). That is Paper A's situation exactly: Stepanyan et al. argued, and Paper A proves.

The closest JSTAT papers in subject are Gastner's constrained Ising chain (which reuses Antal-Droz-Rácz's domain-wall counting for periodic chains, has 7 figures, and gives an applied motivation through the voter model; [arXiv:1502.02883](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02883)), Rossetto's asymmetric persistent random walk ([arXiv:1712.06447](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.06447)), Artuso et al.'s lattice persistent walk in the Fürth-Taylor form ([arXiv:1805.09889](https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.09889)), and Mallmin, Blythe and Evans, who solve lattice run-and-tumble particles exactly and illustrate the exact solution in figures with no simulations at all ([arXiv:1810.00813](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.00813)). Two conclusions follow. First, Paper A can keep its theorems, which JSTAT has accepted before, but each main result needs a sentence or two of physical reading right after it, and the long uniform-bound arguments are better placed in appendices. Second, the paper should show its main result as a picture. The comparable papers all do, and Table 3 is only half of that: one figure of the crossing coupling against log N, with the exact values, the free and periodic asymptotics, and Stepanyan et al.'s two lines on one plot, would make the paper's contribution visible at a glance.

## Tao, Pak and Halmos agree on stating results first and claiming exactly what is proved

The writing advice that best matches this situation comes from three sources. Tao says the introduction should state the main results, compare them with the literature, and "demonstrate why the paper's results and techniques are new, interesting, and/or surprising given this context". He also says a paper "should neither understate nor overstate its main results" ([Tao, introduction](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/advice-on-writing-papers/use-the-introduction-to-%E2%80%9Csell%E2%80%9D-the-key-points-of-your-paper/); [Tao, describe results accurately](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/advice-on-writing-papers/describe-the-results-accurately/)). Pak wants the first theorem "on the first page, or at worst on the second page". When the main theorem is too technical to state that early, he recommends an easy-to-state corollary as "an advertisement of your paper". He also recommends a history paragraph of the form "[A] asked ..., [B] showed ..., in this paper we ...", with secondary history moved to Final Remarks ([Pak 2018](https://www.math.ucla.edu/~pak/papers/how-to-write1.pdf)). Pak's title rule is that the title should be "the first approximation to contents of your paper" (same source). Halmos: state theorems first, mention the trivial cases explicitly, and "Complete honesty makes for greatest clarity" ([Halmos 1970](https://faculty.washington.edu/heagerty/Courses/b572/public/HalmosHowToWrite.pdf)).

Applied to Paper A, Corollary 5.4 is Pak's advertising corollary. In Ising language it says that the free-chain crossing sits at βJ = (1/2)(log N − log log N) + ..., which is N/ξ = 2 log N − log log N + log(π/8), and that the periodic chain's crossing differs by a computable shift. That sentence, without formulas in the abstract but displayed in the introduction, should appear on page 1 or 2. Tao's honesty rule points to the Antal-Droz-Rácz issue discussed below. Halmos's rule on parallel statements ("use the same words in the same order ... then emphasize the difference") fits the free-versus-periodic comparison. The present title, "Uniform Bessel bounds for magnetization crossings in finite Ising chains", names the method first. A title that names the result, such as "Exact location of the order-disorder crossing in finite Ising chains", would follow Pak's rule better and is what physicists would search for. This change is optional, but if the author makes it, it has to be made before arXiv v2.

## Antal-Droz-Rácz already give the crossing equation heuristically, so the paper must say so first

The positioning has three parts. **Bruce (1981) and Antal, Droz and Rácz (2004) gave the exact finite-size scaling form of the magnetization distribution of the zero-field chain for free and periodic boundaries.** It consists of delta peaks at m = ±1 plus a continuum built from the Bessel functions I0 and I1, in the limit N → ∞ with ζ = N(1 − tanh K)/2 held fixed ([arXiv:cond-mat/0308442](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0308442); [Bruce, DOI 10.1088/0022-3719/14/25/012](https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3719/14/25/012)). Paper A's z_N = N(1 − p) is exactly ADR's ζ, which is half the mean number of domain walls (N/(2ξ)). Stepanyan et al. then defined the crossing P(m = ±N) = P(m = 0) for periodic chains and gave βJ ≈ (2 ln N − 1)/5 ([arXiv:2307.15479](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.15479); [DOI 10.1088/1742-5468/ad2679](https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-5468/ad2679)).

The risk is the step a knowledgeable referee will take next. **Substituting ζ ~ log N into the ADR free-boundary law reproduces z + (1/2) log z = log N + (1/2) log(π/8) + 1/(8z), the paper's crossing equation without its remainder term** (checked in this review). A referee who does this calculation will ask what Paper A adds. The answer is substantial, but the paper has to give it before the referee asks. The crossing lies at ζ ~ log N → ∞, outside the fixed-ζ window where the ADR scaling function is derived, so the scaling function alone does not justify using it there. Paper A's uniform, non-asymptotic Bessel bounds do justify it. They also give a bracket for the crossing at every N, prove that the crossing is unique, and give the exact free-versus-periodic comparison, including the boundary shift (log 2)/(2 log N) in the coupling and the constants log(π/8) and log(π/2) in N/ξ. This is the Mailler-Uribe Bravo pattern: the physics heuristic stated openly, followed by the rigorous result. A short paragraph in the introduction, plus a remark that carries out the ADR substitution in a few lines, turns the referee's strongest objection into evidence that the author knows the field.

The comparison with Stepanyan et al. is the paper's clearest physical payoff, and it should be stated without accusing them of anything. The exact asymptotics have slope 1/2 in ln N, with a −(1/2) ln ln N correction. The secant slopes of the exact couplings in Table 3 are 0.385, 0.417 and 0.450 (checked in this review). They climb slowly toward 1/2, so both Stepanyan et al.'s analytic 2/5 and their fitted 0.3864 describe the finite range accurately but give the wrong asymptotic slope, and the log log term explains why. Their Hamiltonian convention (H = −J Σ s_i s_{i+1}, periodic, βJ) matches the patch's use of it, so the comparison is like for like (checked in this review).

Beyond these three papers, a JSTAT referee will expect the persistent-walk and telegraph lineage: Goldstein 1951 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/qjmam/4.2.129)), Kac 1974 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1216/RMJ-1974-4-3-497)), Renshaw and Henderson 1981 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/3213286)), Weiss 2002 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0378-4371(02)00805-1)) and Masoliver and Lindenberg 2017 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1140/epjb/e2017-80123-7)). The referee will also expect the JSTAT run-and-tumble work, Malakar et al. 2018 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1088/1742-5468/aab84f)), and the two papers Stepanyan et al. themselves build on, Xu and del Campo 2019 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.122.160602)) and Yilmaz and Zimmermann 2005 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.71.026127)). One paragraph can tie these together. The ADR law is the telegraph propagator with γt ↔ ζ and x/(vt) ↔ m, and domain walls correspond to tumbles. In that language, the crossing is the time at which a lattice run-and-tumble particle's ballistic peak stops dominating its central bin. No run-and-tumble or active-matter paper defining that crossover was found, so the claim can be made with appropriate care.

## The revised package fixes the framing but misstates Stepanyan et al. three times

The patch applies cleanly and builds. Corollary 5.4's proof was checked by hand. Table 3 is reproduced both by the new script and by a brute-force sum over all spin configurations at N = 10 (free 0.823, periodic 0.687) and at N = 12, and the author has rechecked the corollary and the table independently (all checked in this review). The changes are well chosen. The paper now opens with Stepanyan et al., Kagey's problem has moved to the second paragraph, the other four questions of Problem 131 have moved to a remark, and all statement numbers are unchanged, so citations to v1 still resolve.

The three wording errors (checked in this review) are exactly the kind that would cost the paper with a referee who knows the Stepanyan paper, and quite possibly that referee is one of its authors. First, the abstract says the crossing was "approximated from numerical data". In fact Stepanyan et al.'s Eq. (16) is an analytic approximation, and only the 0.3864 ln N − 0.21055 line in their Fig. 1(b) is a fit. Better wording: "previously approximated analytically and by a numerical fit". In the abstract it should not name them at all, because JSTAT asks for no references in the abstract. Second, the text says the fitted line is accurate "on the range 10 ≤ N ≤ 100 where it was fitted", but their text does not state the fit range. Replace this with something like "accurate for the moderate N shown in their Fig. 1(b)", or report only where Table 3 shows agreement. Third, the related-work text says Stepanyan et al. "solve exactly" the one-flipped-spin crossing. The condition N e^{−4βJ} = 1 is indeed exact for the periodic chain, but they present it as a leading-order result. Better wording: "their first transition temperature, which they give to leading order, is in fact exact: ...". This credits them and the precision at once.

Three structural points remain. First, Kagey's problem in the second paragraph still puts the JAP editor's objection on page 1. A single sentence at the end of the introduction ("The free-boundary question was also posed as Problem 131 of Kagey's list [ref]") keeps the credit while letting the Ising question carry the motivation. Second, the paper needs the ADR-heuristic paragraph from the previous section, at least one figure, and a "Model and main results" section (or a clearly signposted equivalent) that states Corollary 5.4 in Ising variables early. Third, the computer-assisted parts need a short Methods-type section ("Computational verification") separate from the AI disclosure. IOP wants research-process AI use described in Methods and a separate AI statement in the Acknowledgements naming the tool and version, what it did, and how its output was verified ([IOP Generative AI tools](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/questions/generative-ai-tools/)). The disclosure should be factual and in proportion: it should not lead the paper, and it must not be vague.

The series is the policy risk most likely to be overlooked. JSTAT says "Serial publication should be avoided" ([JSTAT About](https://jstat.sissa.it/jstat/help/helpLoader.jsp?pgType=about)), and IOP requires authors to flag in the cover letter any material that has been published elsewhere ([IOP ethical policy](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/ethical-policy-journals/)). Paper A should be self-contained. It should cite Paper B (under review at J. Stat. Phys.) for what Paper B actually proves, and not present itself as part of a numbered six-paper programme. The cover letter should state in one sentence that a companion paper on different questions is under review elsewhere and that the two do not overlap. JAP is not an IOP journal, so IOP's rule on disclosing earlier rejections does not apply ([IOP Publishing Support](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/?p=2603)). Mentioning the JAP rejection is optional and gains nothing. What matters is that the paper JSTAT receives is substantively different, and it is.

## Conclusion

The JAP rejection was useful. It showed that this result is judged by where it sits, not by how hard it was to prove. In its own setting, the finite Ising chain, the question was already open in JSTAT, already answered heuristically by a classic J. Phys. A paper, and answered with the wrong asymptotic slope in the recent JSTAT paper. Paper A's real contribution is that the heuristic is correct beyond the window where anyone had justified it, together with an explanation, through the log log N correction, of why finite-N fits miss the asymptotic slope of 1/2. Put that way, the paper is a modest but genuine statistical-physics result rather than an undergraduate project, and every change it still needs follows from presenting it that way.

There is a practical consequence. Since "Not suitable" cannot be appealed and arXiv v2 must match the submission, the author has one shot, and the work should be done before v2 is posted. If JSTAT still declines on relevance, J. Phys. A explicitly covers the mathematical structures of physical processes ([IOP, J. Phys. A](https://publishingsupport.iopscience.iop.org/journals/journal-of-physics-a-mathematical-and-theoretical/)) and is the natural next venue. A transfer within IOP would then require disclosing the JSTAT decision.
