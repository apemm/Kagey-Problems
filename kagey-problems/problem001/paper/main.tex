\begin{abstract}
We study Problem 001 of Kagey's Open Problem Collection: black out as many points of an
$n\times m$ grid as possible so that every possible rectangle drawn with its corners on grid points remains
uniquely identifiable from its visible corners. We reduce the problem to a minimum
hitting set of a difference hypergraph, solve the two-row case completely (the Strip
Theorem: maximum blackout $m+1$ with exactly $m\cdot 2^{m-1}$ optima for $m\ge 3$),
prove that the maximum blackout is $m+2$ on three rows for $m\ge3$ and $m+4$ on
four rows for $m\ge6$, and obtain the general upper bound
$m+\lfloor n^2/4\rfloor$ for $m\ge3$. The structural step characterizes
axis-aligned identification by the absence of four- and six-cycles in the black
row--column incidence graph, and a separate geometric argument handles tilted rectangles.
On the $5\times5$ grid the same characterization gives the value $10$ by hand,
since a bipartite graph on $10$ vertices with no four- or six-cycle has at most $10$ edges.
We also report the computations, the uniqueness of the $5\times5$ optimum up to
symmetry (by computer), and the remaining conjecture for larger grids.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The problem is posed with some alterations chosen for clarity as the following: Suppose you, the player, are shown an $n \times m$ grid of vertices. Each vertex has two states, 'white' or 'blacked out'. A vertex that is blacked out is unable to convey any information. The game master asks us to 'black out', or turn off, as many of the vertices as we like. Once we choose the 'blackout', which is simply the set of all vertices that are blacked out, the game master draws a rectangle with its corners on grid vertices. We cannot see the rectangle itself. We are only told which of its corners are white, so a corner on a blacked-out vertex tells us nothing. The rectangle's sides are not necessarily parallel to the grid axes. We must be able to guess what the rectangle's corners are from this information with complete confidence. The question posed by Kagey \cite{kagey} is how many vertices can be blacked out while every rectangle the game master might draw remains distinctly identifiable. 

The order of play is important here. Because the blackout is chosen by us
before the rectangle is chosen, a blackout is valid only if \emph{no} two
rectangles are confusable under it. Writing $C(R)$ for the corner set of a rectangle
and $S$ for the blackout, what the player sees is $C(R) \setminus S$; the requirement
is that this assignment be injective on the set of rectangles. Section
\ref{sec:conventions} makes this precise, and the rest of the paper studies the
resulting extremal quantity.

The Strip Theorem (Theorem~\ref{thm:strip}) settles two rows, including the
classification and number of optima. Theorems~\ref{thm:threerow}
and~\ref{thm:fourrow} settle the values on three rows and on four rows of
width at least 6. The four-row grids of width 4 and 5 are settled by an
exhaustive computer check (Corollary~\ref{cor:fourboundary}). The general row
bound and the short-cycle characterization are Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} and
Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}.
The $5\times5$ value is $10$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fivefive}); the
proof is a short counting argument on the same graph, plus a check of one
construction. Optimum counts beyond strips and the $5\times5$ uniqueness statement remain
computational. The higher-dimensional Excess-1 formula remains conjectural
(Conjecture~\ref{conj:excess}). The old guess that the kept set always has size
$(n-1)(m-1)$ was first refuted at $5\times5$ and also fails on $4\times m$
for every $m\ge6$.

Every prediction was written in a ledger before the computation that tested it. The
values were computed with an ILP formulation, a branch-and-bound oracle, and an
exhaustive check straight from the definition, and Table 1 says which of these agree
for each grid. The three- and four-row results have their own verifier, which
enumerates the rectangles by their diagonals.

The paper is organized as follows. Section \ref{sec:conventions} fixes conventions and
defines the presentation map. Section \ref{sec:reduction} proves the reduction and its
consequences. Section \ref{sec:preprocessing} describes the pruning of the difference
hypergraph. Section \ref{sec:strip} proves the Strip Theorem and Section~\ref{sec:narrow}
proves the row bound and the results for three and four rows. Section
\ref{sec:computations} reports the computations and the verification. Section
\ref{sec:uniqueness} proves the value at $5 \times 5$ and describes its unique optimum. Section \ref{sec:conjectures}
records the refuted and live conjectures and states open questions.

\section{Conventions and the presentation map}\label{sec:conventions}
Vertices of the $n\times m$ grid are denoted as pairs $(i,j)$ with $1\le i\le m$ (column) and
$1\le j\le n$ (row). The set of all vertices on the grid is $V$. The rectangle class consists of all rectangles with four corners on
grid points, tilted rectangles included and degenerate (zero-area) configurations
excluded; $C(R)$ denotes the corner set of a rectangle $R$. For a blackout $S\subseteq V$
the \emph{presentation map} sends each rectangle to its visible corners,
$\pres(R)=C(R)\setminus S$, and $S$ is \emph{valid} when $\pres$ is injective over all
rectangles. The empty presentation is an ordinary and accepted value of the map, that at most one
rectangle may be fully hidden is a corollary of injectivity, not an axiom
(Remark~\ref{rem:empty}).

\begin{remark}\label{rem:empty}
Suppose 2 distinct rectangles $R_1 \neq R_2$ with corner sets $C_1$ and $C_2$ are both fully hidden, that is, $C_1 \subseteq S$ and $C_2 \subseteq S$. Then $\pres(R_1) = \emptyset = \pres(R_2)$, and since $\emptyset$ is an ordinary value of the presentation map, injectivity gives $R_1 = R_2$, contradicting our supposition. Hence, at most 1 rectangle may be fully hidden under any valid blackout, with no separate convention required.
\end{remark}

\section{The reduction}\label{sec:reduction}
\begin{lemma}[difference-set characterization]\label{lem:reduction}
For any sets $A,B,S$: \ $A\setminus S=B\setminus S$ if and only if
$A\,\triangle\,B\subseteq S$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}

($\Rightarrow$) Assume $A\setminus S=B\setminus S$, and let $x\in A\,\triangle\,B$ be
arbitrary. Exchanging $A$ and $B$ makes no difference since $\triangle$ is commutative and symmetric about $A$ and $B$, so we may assume $x\in A\setminus B$. Suppose $x\notin S$. Then
$x\in A\setminus S$, and since $A\setminus S=B\setminus S$ we get $x\in B\setminus S$,
hence $x\in B$, contradicting $x\in A\setminus B$. Therefore $x\in S$. As $x$ was
arbitrary, $A\,\triangle\,B\subseteq S$.

\smallskip

($\Leftarrow$) Assume $A \,\triangle\, B \subseteq S$. Both the hypothesis and the
conclusion are unchanged when $A$ and $B$ are exchanged, so it suffices to prove
$A \setminus S \subseteq B \setminus S$. Let $x \in A \setminus S$ be arbitrary, so
$x \in A$ and $x \notin S$. Suppose $x \notin B$. Then $x \in A \setminus B \subseteq
A \,\triangle\, B \subseteq S$, contradicting $x \notin S$. Hence $x \in B$, and with
$x \notin S$ we get $x \in B \setminus S$. As $x$ was arbitrary,
$A \setminus S \subseteq B \setminus S$; the reverse inclusion follows by symmetry, so
$A \setminus S = B \setminus S$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
$S$ is valid if and only if the kept set $K=V\setminus S$ intersects the difference set
$C(R_1)\,\triangle\,C(R_2)$ for every pair of distinct rectangles. Hence a maximum
blackout is the complement of a minimum hitting set of the \emph{difference hypergraph}.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}
By definition, $S$ is valid if and only if $\pres(R_1) \neq \pres(R_2)$, i.e. $C(R_1)\setminus S \neq C(R_2)\setminus S$, for every pair of distinct rectangles. From Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}, this holds if and only if $C(R_1)\,\triangle\,C(R_2) \not\subseteq S$, which is exactly the statement that at least 1 point of the difference set lies in $K$.
\end{proof}

Two simple facts about rectangles are used throughout.

\begin{lemma}[three corners suffice]\label{lem:threecorners}
Three corners of a rectangle determine the rectangle. Consequently, if two
rectangles have the same presentation under some blackout and that presentation
has at least $3$ points, the two rectangles are equal.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Let $p,q,r$ be 3 corners of a rectangle. Exactly one of them, say $q$, is
adjacent to the other 2, and the angle at $q$ in the triangle $pqr$ is a right
angle. A triangle has at most 1 right angle, so $q$ is determined by the 3
points alone, and then the fourth corner is $p+r-q$. For the second claim, a
presentation with at least $3$ points contains $3$ corners of each rectangle,
and these are the same $3$ points, so the corner sets agree.
\end{proof}

In particular, two rectangles can only be confused if they have the same
number of visible corners, and that number is at most $2$. So when we check a
blackout, only rectangles with at least $2$ black corners need attention.

\section{Preprocessing}\label{sec:preprocessing}
Before any computation we shrink the difference hypergraph. Think of it as a
table with one line for each pair of distinct rectangles, listing their
difference set; a kept set $K$ is valid when it meets every line. To avoid a
clash with the rows of the grid, we call these lines \emph{constraints}.
The first step is deduplication: identical constraints say the same thing, so
we keep one copy. The second step is a containment rule: if $D_1\subsetneq D_2$
are constraints, any $K$ meeting $D_1$ automatically meets $D_2$, so $D_2$ can be
deleted without changing which $K$ are valid. Finally, no constraint is empty:
$C_1\triangle C_2=\emptyset$ would mean $C_1=C_2$, and distinct rectangles have
distinct corner sets.

After these steps the hypergraph is replaced by the antichain of its minimal
constraints, and a set $K$ meets every original constraint if and only if it
meets every minimal one. All of the following computations in Section~\ref{sec:computations} run on the pruned version of the hypergraph.

\section{The Strip Theorem}\label{sec:strip}
\begin{theorem}[Strip Theorem]\label{thm:strip}
For a $2\times m$ grid with $m\ge 3$, every maximum valid blackout has size $m+1$,
there are exactly $m\cdot 2^{m-1}$ of them, and they are characterized as the complements
of kept sets containing one survivor per column and missing exactly one column. For
$m=2$ the condition is vacuous and every blackout is valid.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Define $K$ as the visible set, where $V \setminus S = K$, and let $K_0$ denote the
minimum possible size of $K$ over valid blackouts. For a $2\times m$ grid, define the
set $T$ to be the set of all columns with at least 1 of their 2 points in $K$. For
$m=2$ there is exactly one rectangle, so injectivity is vacuous and every blackout is
valid; assume $m\ge 3$. Suppose $S$ is a maximum valid blackout.

On a $2\times m$ strip every rectangle is axis-aligned, since a tilted rectangle's
corners would occupy at least 3 distinct rows and the strip has only 2. We can represent any
possible rectangle $R$ as $R_{i,j}$, where $i$ is the column containing the left
corners of $R$ and $j$ is the column containing the right corners, $1\leq i < j \leq m$.


If two distinct rectangles share exactly one column, say $R_{z,p}$ and $R_{z,q}$, the
shared column's corners overlap, and $C(R_{z,p}) \,\triangle\, C(R_{z,q})$ is exactly
the 4 points of columns $p$ and $q$; we call this the pair constraint on $\{p,q\}$. If they
share no column, the difference set is all 8 points of 4 distinct columns, which
contains a pair constraint and is deleted in preprocessing
(Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}). Every pair $\{p,q\}$ arises this way: two
rectangles sharing a column span at least 3 distinct columns between them, and
$m\geq 3$ guarantees a third column $z$ from which to build $R_{z,p}$ and $R_{z,q}$.
Hence, the pruned hypergraph consists of exactly the $\binom{m}{2}$ pair constraints.

Then, from Lemma~\ref{lem:reduction}, a valid visible set $K$ is one that intersects
every pair constraint. Conditioning on $T$, this constrains $T$ to contain every column
except at most one: if two distinct columns $p$ and $q$ are excluded, the pair constraint on
$\{p,q\}$ contains no point of $K$, and $K$ would not intersect every pair constraint. Each
column of $T$ contributes at least 1 point, so $\vert K\vert \geq \vert T\vert \geq
m-1$, and hence $\vert S\vert \leq 2m-(m-1)=m+1$. (The construction below shows
that $m-1$ is attained, so $K_0=m-1$.) If no column is
fully blacked out, $T$ would contain every column and $K$ would need $m$ points, so
$\vert S\vert \leq m$. If any column of $T$ kept both of its points, $\vert K \vert
\geq m$ as well. Hence, at the maximum, exactly one column is excluded from $T$, and
in the other $m-1$ columns, exactly 1 of the 2 points in each column is blacked out.


Every such construction is valid: any pair of columns includes at least one column of
$T$, whose surviving point meets the pair constraint. There are $m$ ways to choose which
column to exclude from $T$ and $2^{m-1}$ ways to choose which 1 of the 2 points to
black out in the remaining $m-1$ columns. Hence, $m\cdot 2^{m-1}$ such distinct
maximum blackouts exist, characterized as in the statement.
\end{proof}

\section{The $5\times 5$ grid}\label{sec:uniqueness}
\input{fig_fivefive.tex}
The $5\times5$ grid is the first square where the old guess of $n+m-1$ black
points fails, and it fails by exactly 1. It is also the smallest grid where
both dimensions are at least $5$, the case Conjecture~\ref{conj:excess} is
about. The row bound (Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}) only gives $5+\lfloor25/4\rfloor=11$
here. In this section we show that the true value is $10$, and we describe the
optimum.

The idea is to look at the blackout as a graph, as in Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}:
one vertex for each row, one for each column, and an edge for each black point.
On a $5\times5$ grid this graph has only $10$ vertices. Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}
forbids cycles of length $4$ and $6$, and the graph is bipartite, so every
cycle has length at least $8$. On $10$ vertices there is room for 1 such cycle
but not for 2 that overlap, and 2 that do not overlap need at least $16$
vertices. So the graph has at most as many edges as vertices. The next lemma
makes this precise.

\begin{lemma}[few edges without short cycles]\label{lem:tenvertices}
Let $G$ be a bipartite graph with $v\le 10$ vertices and no cycle of length $4$
or $6$. Then $G$ has at most $v$ edges. If it has exactly $v$ edges, then every
connected component of $G$ contains exactly 1 cycle, so $G$ is connected and its
only cycle has length $8$ or $10$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Since $G$ is bipartite, all of its cycles have even length, so every cycle has
length at least $8$.

\emph{Step 1: no component has more edges than vertices.} Suppose some
component $G'$ does. Delete vertices of degree $1$ from $G'$ one at a time,
each together with its edge, until none is left. Each deletion removes 1 vertex
and 1 edge, so the result $H$ still has more edges than vertices, and $H$ is
nonempty with every degree at least $2$. If every degree in $H$ were exactly
$2$, $H$ would have as many edges as vertices. So some vertex $x$ of $H$ has
degree at least $3$.

Let $D_i$ be the set of vertices of $H$ at distance $i$ from $x$. Two
different paths from $x$ to the same vertex, each of length at most $3$, would
together contain a cycle of length at most $6$, which is impossible. So for
$i=1,2,3$ every vertex of $D_i$ has exactly 1 neighbour in $D_{i-1}$, its
\emph{parent}. It has no neighbour in $D_i$, because $D_i$ lies on one side of
the bipartition. Since its degree is at least $2$, it therefore has a
neighbour in $D_{i+1}$. Two different vertices of $D_i$ ($i\le2$) cannot share
such a neighbour, since that neighbour would then have 2 parents. Hence
$|D_1|\ge3$, $|D_2|\ge|D_1|\ge3$, $|D_3|\ge|D_2|\ge3$ and $|D_4|\ge1$, and $H$
has at least $1+3+3+3+1=11$ vertices, more than $v\le10$. This
contradiction proves Step 1.

\emph{Step 2: the equality case.} By Step 1, every component has at most as
many edges as vertices, so the total number of edges is at most $v$. If it
equals $v$, every component has exactly as many edges as vertices, and a
connected graph with as many edges as vertices contains exactly 1 cycle. Two
components would then hold 2 vertex-disjoint cycles of length at least $8$,
which needs $16>10$ vertices. So $G$ is connected, and its cycle is even with
length between $8$ and $v\le10$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the $5\times5$ grid]\label{thm:fivefive}
The maximum blackout on the $5\times5$ grid is $10$. For every maximum
blackout $S$, the graph $G_S$ is connected and contains exactly 1 cycle, of
length $8$ or $10$. By computer, there are exactly $8$ maximum blackouts. They
form a single orbit of the symmetry group $D_4$ of the square, with trivial
stabilizer, and each of their graphs is an $8$-cycle with 2 pendant edges
(Figure~\ref{fig:fivefive}).
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
\emph{Upper bound.} Let $S$ be a valid blackout. By Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}
(which applies since $m=5\ge3$), the graph $G_S$ has no cycle of length $4$ or $6$. It
is bipartite with $5+5=10$ vertices and $|S|$ edges, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:tenvertices} gives $|S|\le10$, and if $|S|=10$ it gives the
structure stated.

\emph{Lower bound.} The blackout of Figure~\ref{fig:fivefive}(a) has $10$
points. Its graph is an $8$-cycle with 2 pendant edges, so by
Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} no 2 axis-aligned rectangles are confused. The remaining
$130-100=30$ rectangles are tilted, and \texttt{verify\_narrow.py} checks all
$130$ presentations directly and finds them distinct.

\emph{The count.} By the upper bound, a maximum blackout has a graph with
exactly $10$ edges and no $4$- or $6$-cycle. The script lists all $44{,}640$
such sets of $10$ points (a search that only adds a point when no short cycle
appears) and checks each against all $130$ rectangles. Exactly $8$ are valid, and each
has degrees $3,3$ at 2 vertices, $1,1$ at 2 others and $2$ elsewhere, so its graph is an
$8$-cycle with 2 pendant edges.
Applying the $8$ symmetries of the square to the blackout in
Figure~\ref{fig:fivefive}(a) gives $8$ different blackouts, and these are the $8$
found. The count of $8$ was found earlier, independently, by the ILP engine and
by an exhaustive check of all blackouts from the definition (Table~1).
\end{proof}

In words, the extremal blackout is a staircase of $9$ points running from the
top right corner to the bottom left, the old $n+m-1$ pattern, plus the
corner $(5,5)$. The staircase alone has a graph that is a tree. The
extra corner closes a cycle of length $8$, the shortest the lemma allows. Of the $1{,}440$ sets of $10$ points
whose graph is a single $10$-cycle (2 black points in every row and column),
none is valid; there the tilted rectangles are what goes wrong. The kept set is
2 blocks of $5$ and $10$ points on either side of the staircase, and the
extremal blackout is unique up to symmetry while having no symmetry of its own.


\section{Refuted and live conjectures}\label{sec:conjectures}

The Three-Row conjecture, which we checked for $3\le m\le10$ before proving it, is
now Theorem~\ref{thm:threerow}. The four-row case of Excess-1 is
Theorem~\ref{thm:fourrow}, with the boundary cases in
Corollary~\ref{cor:fourboundary}. We have not classified or counted the optima
beyond two rows.

\begin{conjecture}[Excess-1]\label{conj:excess}
$\mathrm{blackout}(n,m)=n+m$ for $\min(n,m)\ge 4$, excepting $4\times4$ and
$4\times5$ up to transposition, where it is $n+m-1$.
\end{conjecture}
We checked this on seven cells ($4\times 6$ through $6\times 6$), including the
prediction of $12$ at $6\times 6$, which was written down before the
$1063$-second computation. The four-row cases are now proved, and so is
$5\times5$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:fivefive}). The other cases where both dimensions
are at least 5 are open. There the row bound does not imply Excess-1, and
neither does Lemma~\ref{lem:tenvertices}, which needs $n+m\le10$.

Beyond the remaining conjecture, three questions strike us as the natural next ones: why
the number of optima collapses at $4\times 6$ ($26$, against neighbors in the
hundreds and thousands); whether the excess ever grows past $1$, with $5\times 8$ and
$6\times 7$ registered at $13$ as the next kill attempts; and whether the
$5\times 5$ uniqueness admits a structural proof rather than a computational one.
Theorem~\ref{thm:fivefive} reduces that question to $10$-point blackouts whose graph is
connected with a single cycle of length $8$ or $10$.
