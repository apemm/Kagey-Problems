\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

You are shown a grid of points with $h$ rows and $m$ columns, and you black out some
of them. We call the set of blacked-out points the \emph{blackout}. Then the game master
draws a rectangle with its four corners on grid points, tilted or not. You are told which
of its corners are white, and you must say where all four corners are. Kagey asks how many
points you can black out so that no two rectangles ever look the same.

We write $(i,j)$ for the point in column $i$ and row $j$, counting columns from the left
and rows from the top. Figure~\ref{fig:game} plays one round on the $3\times4$ grid with
row $1$ and column $1$ blacked out. The game master draws the tilted square with corners
$(2,2)$, $(3,1)$, $(4,2)$, and $(3,3)$, and we are shown $(2,2)$, $(4,2)$, and $(3,3)$.
Three corners of a rectangle determine the fourth (Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners}), so the
fourth corner is $(3,1)$. This blackout of six points is good, and no good blackout of the
$3\times4$ grid is larger (Section~\ref{sec:three}).

Kagey poses the problem in his Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey001} and in his gift
for the fourteenth Gathering 4 Gardner~\cite{kageyG4G14}. Kagey's page for the problem
cites his earlier question~\cite{kageyMSE} about upright triangles in a triangular array,
where Lavrov gave a counting upper bound and an alteration lower bound. We write $f(h,m)$ for the
largest size of a good blackout of the $h\times m$ grid, and $a_h(m)$ for the number of
good blackouts of that size, counted with their coordinates. From Section~\ref{sec:conv} on, a
good blackout is called \emph{valid} (Definition~\ref{def:valid}). These are the tables
\seqnum{A397315} and \seqnum{A399580} of the OEIS~\cite{oeis}, which we contributed. The
first versions of these entries recorded the conjecture $f(3,m)=m+2$ and the guess that $f(h,m)=h+m$
whenever $\min(h,m)\ge4$, apart from $f(4,4)=7$ and $f(4,5)=8$. We show that the guess
fails. The three-row conjecture was proved in Lean by
The Omega Institute~\cite{trureturing2026}. It is also the case $h=3$ of our row bound,
and we do not claim it as new.

\paragraph{Results.} Two rectangles look the same exactly when every corner that belongs
to only one of them is black (Lemma~\ref{lem:diff}). Put $q_h=\lfloor h^2/4\rfloor$.
Computer checks are marked in each statement.

\begin{introthm}[narrow grids]\label{thm:A}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For all $h,m\ge1$ we have $f(h,m)\ge h+m-1$, and for $m\ge3$ we have
$f(h,m)\le m+q_h$.
\item For $m\ge3$ we have $f(2,m)=m+1$ and $f(3,m)=m+2$. Also $f(4,m)=m+4$ for every
$m\ge6$, and $f(5,5)=10$.
\item (Computer-assisted, the last claim checked in Lean.) We have $f(4,4)=7$, $f(4,5)=8$,
$f(5,6)=11$, and $f(5,7)=12$, while $f(5,m)=m+6$ for every $m\ge8$.
\item (Computer-assisted for $m\ge9$, with checked certificates for $m\le14$ and checked in Lean
for $m\ge15$.) For $8\le m\le14$ we have $f(6,m)\le m+8$, and $f(6,m)=m+9$ for every $m\ge15$.
\item (Computer-assisted, with checked certificates.) We have $f(6,6)=12$,
$f(6,7)=13$, $f(7,7)=14$, and $f(6,8)=15$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{introthm}

Part (a) is Lemma~\ref{lem:cross} and Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}. So for $h\le6$ the row
bound is attained at every large width, and the guess $f(h,m)=h+m$ first fails on the
$5\times8$ grid (Theorem~\ref{thm:refute}).

\begin{introthm}[three-row optima]\label{thm:B}
For $m\ge4$ we have $a_3(m)=4(m-1)(3m+2)\,3^{m-5}+E_m$, where $E_m>0$ counts the optima
with a column black in rows $1$ and $3$, and $E_m=O(m^2(1+\sqrt3)^m)$. So $a_3(m)\sim\frac4{81}m^23^m$, and a fraction
tending to $4/27$ of the $m^23^{m-1}$ axis-valid candidates is valid. The series
$\sum_{m\ge3}a_3(m)z^m$ is rational. (Computer-assisted.) Its reduced denominator is
$(1-3z)^3(1-2z-2z^2)^3(1+2z^2)^3$, and $E_m$ has a closed form with
$E_m\sim\frac{19+11\sqrt3}{432}m^2(1+\sqrt3)^m$.
\end{introthm}

These are Theorems~\ref{thm:threecount}, \ref{thm:threeclosed}, and~\ref{thm:threegf}, the
last finished in Section~\ref{app:threegf}.

\begin{introthm}[four rows]\label{thm:C}
Let $m\ge4$. No valid blackout of the $4\times m$ grid hides all four corners of a
rectangle. For $m\ge6$,
\[
a_4(m)\le3\,(m)_4\,4^{m-4}\Bigl(\frac{53}{64}\Bigr)^{\max(0,\lfloor m/4\rfloor-4)},
\qquad (m)_4=m(m-1)(m-2)(m-3).
\]
(Computer-assisted.) The series $\sum_{m\ge6}a_4(m)z^m$ is rational with reduced
denominator of degree $420$, and $a_4(m)\sim c\,m^4\rho_4^m$ with $c\approx8.1611\times10^{-5}$
and $\rho_4=3.35199\ldots$, an algebraic number of degree $20$.
\end{introthm}

The base $212^{1/4}=3.8157\ldots<4$ makes valid blackouts rare (Theorems~\ref{thm:hidden},
\ref{thm:foursurvival}, \ref{thm:fourgfexact}).

\begin{introthm}[finite collar]\label{thm:D}
Let $h\ge3$ and $W_h=1+\lfloor(h-1)^2/4\rfloor$. Pad a blackout of an $h\times m$ grid, on
one side or on both, by columns black only in the top row, or only in the bottom row.
Then every such padding is valid if and only if the padding by $W_h$ columns on each
chosen side is valid. (Computer-assisted, checked in Lean.) A blackout of the $5\times8$ grid and one of the
$6\times16$ grid pass this test with the top row on the right. Hence $f(5,m)=m+6$ for
$m\ge8$ and $f(6,m)=m+9$ for $m\ge16$, and a stored blackout of the $6\times15$ grid gives
$f(6,15)=24$.
\end{introthm}

See Theorem~\ref{thm:collar}, proved by hand and in Lean, and Section~\ref{sec:five}. The padding row must be a
boundary row (Remark~\ref{rem:interior}).

\begin{introthm}[every fixed height]\label{thm:E}
Fix $h\ge1$ and write a blackout of the $h\times m$ grid as the word $B_1\cdots B_m$,
where $B_i$ is the set of black rows of column $i$. The valid words form a regular
language. If $h\ge2$, the excess $f(h,m)-m$ is eventually periodic in $m$, and
$\sum_{m\ge0}a_h(m)z^m$ is rational.
\end{introthm}

These are Theorems~\ref{thm:regular} and~\ref{thm:periodic}, and they give no period and
no threshold. On square grids, Section~\ref{app:square} shows that
$f(n,n)\ge c\,n^{6/5}(1+\ln n)^{-2/5}$ for some $c>0$ and all large $n$.

\paragraph{Prior work.} In its hitting-set form (Corollary~\ref{cor:hitting}) the game
asks for a test cover~\cite{katona1966,moretshapiro1985,bellitto2018,foucaud2015}.
For any $n$ distinct subsets of an $n$-set, Bondy's theorem~\cite{bondy1972} finds a
point whose deletion keeps them distinct, but here the rectangles far outnumber the points.
A fully black axis-aligned rectangle is a four-cycle in a bipartite graph on the rows and
columns (Section~\ref{sec:graph}), so forbidding these alone is the problem of
Zarankiewicz~\cite{reiman1958}. The axis-aligned
rectangles alone lead to bipartite graphs without cycles of length four and six
(Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}). Such graphs have $O(n^{4/3})$ edges on $n+n$
vertices~\cite{bondysimonovits1974}. They appear in a question of Erd\H{o}s
(Problem~1080 of Bloom's list~\cite{erdos1080}), and de Caen and
Sz\'ekely~\cite{decaenszekely1992,decaenszekely1997} study the unbalanced case.
Neuwirth~\cite[Theorem~1(ii) and Proposition~17]{neuwirth2001} bounds such graphs on
$h+m$ vertices by $m+h^2/4$ when $4m\ge h^2$, and by a bound that is no larger for
smaller $m$. So the row bound is known in graph form. Our proof with Mantel's
theorem~\cite{mantel1907} also finds and counts the equality case. None of these
works sees tilted rectangles. Grids of fixed height are often
treated by column transfer~\cite{euler2005,mertens2024,alanko2011},
\cite[Proposition~V.6]{flajolet2009}. Klav\v{z}ar and \v{Z}erovnik~\cite{klavzar1996} compute domination numbers of
fixed-height grids with $(\min,+)$ transfer matrices, those of all grids are
known~\cite{goncalves2011}, and minimum identifying codes of circular and infinite grids of small
height were found by computer search~\cite{bouznif2019}. Jiang~\cite{jiang2018} proves that the least
density of an identifying code in an infinite strip is attained by a periodic code, and
Theorem~\ref{thm:E} is of the same kind. Given the regularity in Theorem~\ref{thm:regular}, repeated use of
Theorem~1(3) of Guilhot, Little, and Parkinson~\cite{glp2025} also implies the periodicity and the
rational series (Section~\ref{sec:height}). We prove them directly.
