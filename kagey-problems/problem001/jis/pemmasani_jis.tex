\begin{abstract}
Kagey's Problem 001 concerns a grid of points, some of them blacked out. A rectangle
with its corners on grid points, tilted or not, shows only its white corners. We ask how
many points can be blacked out while every rectangle stays determined by what it shows.
We study the largest such blackout and the number of blackouts of that size, the tables
A397315 and A399580 of the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences. A graph on the rows
and columns and Mantel's theorem bound the maximum by the width plus a quarter of the
squared height. We find the maximum on up to five rows at every width, and on six rows
from width 15 on. On five rows from width 8 on, and on the $6\times8$ grid, the maximum
exceeds the sum of the two sides, against a guess we recorded there. On three rows we
count the optima exactly, and on four rows we find their growth rate. For every fixed
number of rows the maximum minus the width is eventually periodic. Some values rest on
computer checks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

You are shown a grid of points with $h$ rows and $m$ columns, and you black out some
of them. We call the set of blacked-out points the \emph{blackout}. Then the game master
draws a rectangle with its four corners on grid points, tilted or not. You are told which
of its corners are white, and you must say where all four corners are. Kagey asks how many
points you can black out so that no two rectangles ever look the same.

We write $(i,j)$ for the point in column $i$ and row $j$, counting columns from the left
and rows from the top. Figure~\ref{fig:game} plays one round on the $3\times4$ grid with
row $1$ and column $1$ blacked out. The game master draws the tilted square with corners
$(2,2)$, $(3,1)$, $(4,2)$, and $(3,3)$, and we are shown $(2,2)$, $(4,2)$, and $(3,3)$.
Three corners of a rectangle determine the fourth (Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners}), so the
fourth corner is $(3,1)$. This blackout of six points is good, and no good blackout of the
$3\times4$ grid is larger (Section~\ref{sec:three}).

Kagey poses the problem in his Open Problem Collection~\cite{kagey001} and in his gift
for the fourteenth Gathering 4 Gardner~\cite{kageyG4G14}. We write $f(h,m)$ for the
largest size of a good blackout of the $h\times m$ grid, and $a_h(m)$ for the number of
good blackouts of that size, counted with their coordinates. From Section~\ref{sec:conv} on, a
good blackout is called \emph{valid} (Definition~\ref{def:valid}). These are the tables
\seqnum{A397315} and \seqnum{A399580} of the OEIS~\cite{oeis}, which we contributed. Our
comments there record the conjecture $f(3,m)=m+2$ and the guess that $f(h,m)=h+m$
whenever $\min(h,m)\ge4$, apart from $f(4,4)=7$ and $f(4,5)=8$. We show that the guess
fails. The three-row conjecture was proved in Lean by
The Omega Institute~\cite{trureturing2026}. It is also the case $h=3$ of our row bound,
and we do not claim it as new.

\paragraph{Results.} Two rectangles look the same exactly when every corner that belongs
to only one of them is black (Lemma~\ref{lem:diff}). Put $q_h=\lfloor h^2/4\rfloor$.
Computer checks are marked in each statement.

\begin{thmA}[narrow grids]
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For all $h,m\ge1$ we have $f(h,m)\ge h+m-1$, and for $m\ge3$ we have
$f(h,m)\le m+q_h$.
\item For $m\ge3$ we have $f(2,m)=m+1$ and $f(3,m)=m+2$. Also $f(4,m)=m+4$ for every
$m\ge6$, and $f(5,5)=10$.
\item (Computer-assisted.) We have $f(4,4)=7$, $f(4,5)=8$, $f(5,6)=11$, and $f(5,7)=12$, while
$f(5,m)=m+6$ for every $m\ge8$.
\item (Computer-assisted for $m\ge9$.) For $8\le m\le14$ we have $f(6,m)\le m+8$, and
$f(6,m)=m+9$ for every $m\ge15$.

\item (Computer-assisted.) We have $f(6,6)=12$, $f(6,7)=13$, $f(7,7)=14$, and $f(6,8)=15$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{thmA}

The lower bound in (a) is the boundary cross of Lemma~\ref{lem:cross}, and the upper
bound is the row bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}, proved with Mantel's theorem. So for
$h\le6$ the row bound is attained at every large width, and the guess $f(h,m)=h+m$ first
fails on the $5\times8$ grid (Theorem~\ref{thm:refute}).

\begin{thmB}[three-row optima]
For $m\ge4$ we have $a_3(m)=4(m-1)(3m+2)\,3^{m-5}+E_m$, where $E_m\ge0$ counts the optima
with a column black in rows $1$ and $3$. So $a_3(m)\sim\frac4{81}m^23^m$, and a fraction
tending to $4/27$ of the $m^23^{m-1}$ axis-valid candidates is valid. The series
$\sum_{m\ge3}a_3(m)z^m$ is rational. (Computer-assisted.) Its reduced denominator is
$(1-3z)^3(1-2z-2z^2)^3(1+2z^2)^3$, and $E_m$ has a closed form with
$E_m\sim\frac{19+11\sqrt3}{432}m^2(1+\sqrt3)^m$.
\end{thmB}

These are Theorems~\ref{thm:threecount}, \ref{thm:threeclosed}, and~\ref{thm:threegf},
with the last proof finished in Appendix~\ref{app:threegf}. The sequence starts
$53,224,892,3420,12704$ at $m=3$.

\begin{thmC}[four rows]
Let $m\ge4$. No valid blackout of the $4\times m$ grid hides all four corners of a
rectangle. For $m\ge6$,
\[
a_4(m)\le3\,(m)_4\,4^{m-4}\Bigl(\frac{53}{64}\Bigr)^{\max(0,\lfloor m/4\rfloor-4)},
\qquad (m)_4=m(m-1)(m-2)(m-3).
\]
(Computer-assisted.) The series $\sum_{m\ge6}a_4(m)z^m$ is rational with reduced
denominator of degree $420$, and $a_4(m)\sim c\,m^4\rho_4^m$ with $c=8.1611\times10^{-5}$
and $\rho_4=3.35199\ldots$, an algebraic number of degree $20$.
\end{thmC}

The bound has the base $212^{1/4}=3.8157\ldots<4$, so valid blackouts become rare
(Theorems~\ref{thm:hidden}, \ref{thm:foursurvival}, and~\ref{thm:fourgfexact}).

\begin{thmD}[finite collar]
Let $h\ge3$ and $W_h=1+\lfloor(h-1)^2/4\rfloor$. Pad a blackout of an $h\times m$ grid, on
one side or on both, by columns black only in the top row, or only in the bottom row.
Then every such padding is valid if and only if the padding by $W_h$ columns on each
chosen side is valid. (Computer-assisted.) A blackout of the $5\times8$ grid and one of the
$6\times16$ grid pass this test with the top row on the right. Hence $f(5,m)=m+6$ for
$m\ge8$ and $f(6,m)=m+9$ for $m\ge16$, and a stored blackout of the $6\times15$ grid gives
$f(6,15)=24$.
\end{thmD}

The collar theorem is Theorem~\ref{thm:collar}, and Section~\ref{sec:five} has the two
blackouts. The padding row must be a boundary row (Remark~\ref{rem:interior}).

\begin{thmE}[every fixed height]
Fix $h\ge1$ and write a blackout of the $h\times m$ grid as the word $B_1\cdots B_m$,
where $B_i$ is the set of black rows of column $i$. The valid words form a regular
language. If $h\ge2$, the excess $f(h,m)-m$ is eventually periodic in $m$, and
$\sum_{m\ge0}a_h(m)z^m$ is rational.
\end{thmE}

These are Theorems~\ref{thm:regular} and~\ref{thm:periodic}, and they give no period and
no threshold.

\paragraph{Prior work.} In its hitting-set form (Corollary~\ref{cor:hitting}) the game
asks for a test cover~\cite{katona1966,bellitto2018,foucaud2015}. The axis-aligned
rectangles alone lead to bipartite graphs without cycles of length four and six
(Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}), studied by Erd\H{o}s~\cite{erdos1080} and by de Caen and
Sz\'ekely~\cite{decaenszekely1997}. Neuwirth~\cite[Theorem~1 and
Proposition~17]{neuwirth2001} proves our row bound for them when $m\ge q_h$. Our proof
uses Mantel's theorem~\cite{mantel1907}, covers every $m\ge3$, and counts the equality
case. None of these works sees tilted rectangles. Grids of fixed height are often
treated by column transfer~\cite{euler2005,mertens2024,alanko2011},
\cite[Proposition~V.6]{flajolet2009}. Jiang~\cite{jiang2018} proves that optimal
identifying codes in strips can be taken periodic, and Theorem~E is of the same kind. It
is close to a theorem of Guilhot, Little, and Parkinson~\cite[Theorem~1]{glp2025}, but we
maximize separately at each width and prove it directly. The code and data are in the
repository~\cite{repo}.
\section{Conventions and the difference lemma}\label{sec:conv}

These conventions hold throughout the paper.
\begin{enumerate}[(1),leftmargin=*]
\item An $h\times m$ grid has $h$ rows and $m$ columns, and $V$ is its set of points.
A $2\times m$ grid is a \emph{strip}, and an $n\times n$ grid is a \emph{square}.
\item The point $(i,j)$ is in column $i$ and row $j$, and $(1,1)$ is the top-left
point. So $i$ grows to the right and $j$ grows downward, as in
Figure~\ref{fig:game}(a).
\item A \emph{rectangle} is a set of four grid points that are the corners of a
rectangle of positive area, and tilted rectangles count. We identify a rectangle $R$ with
its corner set $C(R)$. It is \emph{axis-aligned} if its sides are parallel to the rows and columns, and
\emph{tilted} otherwise. Four distinct points form a rectangle if and only if they
split into two segments with the same midpoint and the same length.
\item A \emph{blackout} is a set $S\subseteq V$, and $K=V\setminus S$ is the
\emph{kept set}. The \emph{presentation} of a rectangle $R$ is the set
$\pres(R)=C(R)\setminus S$ of its white corners.
\item The \emph{black rows} of column $i$ form the set $B_i$ of rows $j$ with
$(i,j)\in S$. Column $i$ is \emph{empty}, a \emph{singleton}, a \emph{double}, or a
\emph{triple} when $|B_i|$ is $0$, $1$, $2$, or $3$. It is \emph{full} when $|B_i|=h$.
\item The \emph{pair set} $F$ is the set of row pairs contained in some $B_i$. We put
$q_h=\lfloor h^2/4\rfloor$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[valid blackout]\label{def:valid}
A blackout $S$ is \emph{valid} if different rectangles always have different
presentations, that is, if $\pres(R_1)\ne\pres(R_2)$ whenever $R_1\ne R_2$. The
\emph{maximum blackout} $f(h,m)$ is the largest size of a valid blackout of the
$h\times m$ grid. We write $a_h(m)$ for the number of valid blackouts of size $f(h,m)$.
They are counted with their coordinates, not up to symmetry.
\end{definition}

Swapping rows and columns gives $f(h,m)=f(m,h)$ and $a_h(m)=a_m(h)$. The tables
$f(h,m)$ and $a_h(m)$ are \seqnum{A397315} and \seqnum{A399580}, read by antidiagonals
with both indices starting at $2$. Section~\ref{sec:height} also uses width $0$ and height $1$, with no
rectangles, where $f(h,0)=0$, $a_h(0)=1$, $f(1,m)=m$, and $a_1(m)=1$.

The empty presentation is allowed. It means that every corner is black, so a valid
blackout hides at most one rectangle.

\begin{lemma}[difference sets]\label{lem:diff}
For all sets $A$, $B$, and $S$ we have $A\setminus S=B\setminus S$ if and only if
$A\triangle B\subseteq S$, where $\triangle$ is the symmetric difference.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Both conditions say that no point outside $S$ lies in exactly one of $A$ and $B$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[hitting sets]\label{cor:hitting}
A blackout $S$ is valid if and only if the kept set $K$ meets
$C(R_1)\triangle C(R_2)$ for every pair of distinct rectangles $R_1$, $R_2$. So
$hm-f(h,m)$ is the least size of a set meeting every such difference set.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:diff} to $C(R_1)$, $C(R_2)$, and $S$ for every pair.
\end{proof}

A larger kept set meets more sets, so every subset of a valid blackout is valid. Hence a
search that finds no valid blackout of exactly $k$ points proves $f(h,m)<k$.

\begin{lemma}[three corners suffice]\label{lem:threecorners}
Three corners of a rectangle determine the rectangle. So two distinct rectangles with
the same presentation both show at most two corners.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Three corners $p$, $q$, and $r$ of a rectangle form a right triangle, with the right
angle at the corner adjacent to both others, say $q$. A triangle has only one right
angle, so the three points determine $q$ and the fourth corner $p+r-q$.
\end{proof}
\section{Strips}\label{sec:strip}

On two rows every rectangle is axis-aligned. Indeed, a tilted rectangle has no
horizontal side, so both sides at a lowest corner go strictly up, and the opposite
corner lies in a third row.

\begin{theorem}[Strip Theorem]\label{thm:strip}
Let $m\ge3$.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item A blackout of the $2\times m$ strip is valid if and only if at most one of its
columns is full.
\item We have $f(2,m)=m+1$, and the maximum blackouts are those with one full column
and one black point in every other column.
\item There are $a_2(m)=m\cdot2^{m-1}$ maximum blackouts.
\end{enumerate}
For $m=2$ there is only one rectangle, so every blackout is valid, $f(2,2)=4$, and
$a_2(2)=1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Two distinct rectangles share at most one column. If they
share one, it cancels, and the difference set is the four points of the other two
columns $p$ and $q$, the \emph{pair constraint} on $\{p,q\}$. If they share
no column, the difference set is the eight points of four columns, and it contains a
pair constraint. Every pair constraint occurs, since $m\ge3$ gives a third column $z$,
and the rectangles on $\{z,p\}$ and $\{z,q\}$ have exactly this difference set. By
Corollary~\ref{cor:hitting}, $S$ is valid if and only if $K$ meets every two columns,
that is, if and only if at most one column has no kept point.

(b) By (a) a valid $S$ has $|K|\ge m-1$, so $|S|\le m+1$. Equality holds exactly when
one column is full and every other column has one kept point, hence one black point.
Such blackouts are valid by (a).

(c) There are $m$ choices for the full column and two in each other column.
\end{proof}

So row $2$ of \seqnum{A399580} is \seqnum{A001787}$(m)=m2^{m-1}$ from $m=3$ on.

\begin{remark}[orbits]\label{rem:kings}
Up to the row reflection and the column reversal, there are
$o_m=m\,2^{m-3}+[m\text{ odd}]\,2^{(m-3)/2}$ maximum blackouts of the $2\times m$ strip for
$m\ge3$, by Burnside's lemma (proof omitted). So $o_3,\ldots,o_8$ are $4,8,22,48,116,256$.
These six values agree with \seqnum{A322284}$(m-1)$, which counts nonattacking kings on a
$2\times(2m-2)$ board up to symmetry.
\end{remark}
\section{The row-column graph and general bounds}\label{sec:graph}

This section proves $h+m-1\le f(h,m)\le m+q_h$ and lists the tilted rectangles of narrow grids. A blackout $S$ of the $h\times m$ grid is \emph{axis-valid} if no two axis-aligned rectangles have the same presentation. A rectangle that shows at most two corners is \emph{weak}, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners} only weak rectangles can be confused.

The \emph{row-column graph} $G_S$ has a vertex $r_j$ for each row $j$, a vertex $c_i$ for each column $i$, and an edge $r_jc_i$ for each black point $(i,j)$. A fully black axis-aligned rectangle on rows $j,j'$ and columns $i,i'$ is the cycle $r_j\,c_i\,r_{j'}\,c_{i'}$.

\begin{lemma}[axis-aligned rectangles and short cycles]\label{lem:girth}
If $G_S$ has no four-cycle and no six-cycle, then $S$ is axis-valid. If $m\ge3$ and $S$ is axis-valid, then $G_S$ has no four-cycle and no six-cycle.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $m\ge3$. A four-cycle $r_j\,c_i\,r_{j'}\,c_{i'}$ and a third column $w$ give the rectangles on rows $\{j,j'\}$ and columns $\{i,w\}$ or $\{i',w\}$, which both show the white points among $(w,j)$ and $(w,j')$. For a six-cycle $r_a\,c_x\,r_b\,c_y\,r_c\,c_z$ the different rectangles on rows $\{a,b\}$, columns $\{x,y\}$ and on rows $\{a,c\}$, columns $\{y,z\}$ are black except possibly at $(y,a)$.

Conversely, let $R_1\ne R_2$ be axis-aligned with the same presentation, so $C(R_1)\triangle C(R_2)\subseteq S$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:diff}. Opposite corners determine an axis-aligned rectangle, so the common corners lie in one row or one column. Two common corners $(x,a)$ and $(y,a)$ leave rows $b\ne c$ and the black four-cycle $r_b\,c_x\,r_c\,c_y$, and a common column is similar. If $(x,a)$ is the only common corner, with $R_1$ on rows $\{a,b\}$ and columns $\{x,y\}$ and $R_2$ on rows $\{a,c\}$ and columns $\{x,z\}$, then $r_a\,c_y\,r_b\,c_x\,r_c\,c_z$ is a black six-cycle. With no common corner, $R_1$ is a black four-cycle. The all-black $2\times2$ grid shows that $m\ge3$ is needed.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[row bound]\label{thm:rowbound}
Let $m\ge3$. Every axis-valid blackout of the $h\times m$ grid has at most $m+q_h$ points, and at most $h+q_m$ points if also $h\ge3$. Hence $f(h,m)\le m+q_h$ for $m\ge3$.
\end{theorem}

For each nonempty column $i$ choose a tree $T_i$ with vertex set $B_i$, and let $H$ be the graph on the rows whose edges are those of all the trees $T_i$.

\begin{lemma}[row graph]\label{lem:H}
Let $m\ge3$ and let $S$ be axis-valid, with arbitrary trees $T_i$. Then $H$ has no repeated edge and no triangle, and $e(H)=|S|-t$, where $t$ is the number of nonempty columns.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}, $G_S$ has no four-cycle and no six-cycle. If columns $i\ne i'$ both gave the edge $r_jr_{j'}$, then $r_j\,c_i\,r_{j'}\,c_{i'}$ would be a four-cycle. So $e(H)=\sum(|B_i|-1)=|S|-t$. A triangle $r_ar_br_c$ does not lie in one tree. If two of its edges come from column $i$ and one from column $i'$, then two of $a,b,c\in B_i$ lie in $B_{i'}$, a four-cycle. If its edges come from columns $i,i',i''$, then $r_a\,c_i\,r_b\,c_{i'}\,r_c\,c_{i''}$ is a six-cycle.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Mantel \cite{mantel1907}]\label{lem:mantel}
Let $H$ be a simple triangle-free graph on $h$ vertices, with degrees $d(\cdot)$. Let $v$ have maximum degree $\Delta$, let $A$ be its set of neighbors, and let $B$ be the set of the other $h-\Delta$ vertices, with $e(B)$ edges inside $B$. Then
\[
e(H)=\sum_{b\in B}d(b)-e(B)\le(h-\Delta)\Delta-e(B)\le q_h.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
No two vertices of $A$ are adjacent, so every edge has an end in $B$, and the degree sum over $B$ counts the edges inside $B$ twice. Every degree is at most $\Delta$, $e(B)\ge0$, and $(h-\Delta)\Delta$ is an integer at most $h^2/4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}]
Lemmas~\ref{lem:H} and~\ref{lem:mantel} give $|S|=t+e(H)\le m+q_h$. Only Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} uses a third column, so for $h\ge3$ the exchange of rows and columns gives $|S|\le h+q_m$.
\end{proof}

This removes the hypothesis $m\ge q_h$ of Neuwirth's graph form~\cite[Theorem~1(ii)]{neuwirth2001}. His extremal graphs~\cite[Proposition~17]{neuwirth2001} are the blackouts of the next theorem, which adds the converse and the count.

\begin{theorem}[equality structure]\label{thm:equality}
Let $h\ge4$ and $m\ge3$, and let $S$ be a blackout of the $h\times m$ grid with $m+q_h$ points. Then $S$ is axis-valid if and only if
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item every column is a singleton or a double,
\item exactly $q_h$ columns are doubles, with pairwise distinct row pairs, and
\item these row pairs are the edges of a complete bipartite graph on the rows whose parts differ in size by at most one.
\end{enumerate}
There are $p_h\,(m)_{q_h}\,h^{m-q_h}$ such blackouts, where $(m)_q=m(m-1)\cdots(m-q+1)$ and $p_h$, the number of such unordered partitions of the rows, is $\frac12\binom h{h/2}$ for even $h$ and $\binom h{\lfloor h/2\rfloor}$ for odd $h$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1. Equality in Mantel's theorem.} Let $S$ be axis-valid and build $H$ from arbitrary trees. Since $|S|=m+q_h$, Lemmas~\ref{lem:H} and~\ref{lem:mantel} force $t=m$ and equality throughout Lemma~\ref{lem:mantel}. So every column is nonempty, $e(B)=0$, and every $b\in B$ has its $\Delta=|A|$ neighbors in $A$. Hence $H$ is complete bipartite with parts $A$ and $B$, whose sizes differ by at most one since $(h-\Delta)\Delta=q_h$.

\emph{Step 2. No triple.} Suppose $|B_i|\ge3$, so $T_i$ has a vertex $v$ with two neighbors $u,w$, both in the part opposite to $v$. Since $h\ge4$ the part of $v$ has a vertex $z\ne v$. Replacing the edge $vw$ of $T_i$ by $uw$ gives a tree on $B_i$, and the new graph $H'$ keeps $uz$ and $wz$ since $z\ne v$. The triangle $uwz$ contradicts Lemma~\ref{lem:H} and proves (a).

\emph{Step 3. The doubles.} By (a) the edges of $H$ are the row pairs of the $e(H)=q_h$ doubles. Lemma~\ref{lem:H} gives (b) and Step~1 gives (c).

\emph{Step 4. The converse.} Under (a) and (b) no two columns share two black rows, and a six-cycle $r_a\,c_x\,r_b\,c_y\,r_c\,c_z$ would need doubles $\{a,b\}$, $\{b,c\}$, $\{a,c\}$, a triangle in the graph of (c). So Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} applies.

\emph{Step 5. The count.} Choose a partition, an injective assignment of its $q_h$ edges to columns, and a row for each other column. The doubles determine these choices.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[small widths]\label{cor:small-m}
Let $h\ge4$ and $3\le m<q_h$. Then $f(h,m)\le m+q_h-1$. In particular $f(5,5)\le10$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Use Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}. A valid blackout of $m+q_h$ points is axis-valid, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:equality}(b) it has $q_h\le m$ doubles. Note that $q_5=6$.
\end{proof}

For tilted rectangles put $W_h=1+\lfloor(h-1)^2/4\rfloor$, so $W_3=2$, $W_4=3$, and $W_5=5$.

\begin{lemma}[leftmost corner]\label{lem:leftmost}
A tilted rectangle $R$ has exactly one corner of smallest column and exactly one of smallest row. If $\ell=(x,r)$ is the leftmost corner, the sides from $\ell$ are $(a,-b)$ and $(c,d)$ with positive integers $a,b,c,d$ and $ac=bd$, and the corners are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tilt-param}
(x,r),\qquad(x+a,\,r-b),\qquad(x+c,\,r+d),\qquad(x+a+c,\,r-b+d).
\end{equation}
So $R$ occupies the rows $r-b$ to $r+d$, its topmost corner is $(x+a,r-b)$, and its column span is $a+c$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
No side is vertical, so two corners in the smallest column $x$ are opposite and the center is in column $x$. Then the other two corners, with columns at least $x$ and mean $x$, also lie in column $x$, which is impossible. Rows are similar. So both sides from $\ell$ go strictly to the right, and since they are perpendicular, one goes up and one goes down, with $ac=bd$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[span bound]\label{lem:span}
Let $h\ge3$. Every tilted rectangle of the $h\times m$ grid has span $a+c\le W_h$, so it meets at most $W_h+1$ columns. Grids with one or two rows have no tilted rectangles.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Here $b+d\le h-1$, and $(a-1)(c-1)\ge0$ gives $a+c\le bd+1\le W_h$, since $bd$ is an integer at most $(b+d)^2/4$. With at most two rows, $b+d\le1$ is impossible.
\end{proof}

For $1\le i\le m-2$ and $1\le j\le h-2$ the \emph{diamond} $D(i,j)$ is the square with corners $(i,j+1)$, $(i+1,j)$, $(i+2,j+1)$, and $(i+1,j+2)$. Four rows allow four more kinds, $\kA_i$, $\kB_i$, $\kC_i$, and $\kE_i$ for $1\le i\le m-3$. Each has one corner in each of the columns $i$ to $i+3$. Read from left to right, the rows of these corners are $3412$ for $\kA_i$, $3142$ for $\kB_i$, $2413$ for $\kC_i$, and $2143$ for $\kE_i$.

\begin{lemma}[tilted rectangles on narrow grids]\label{lem:tilted-narrow}
The tilted rectangles of the $3\times m$ grid are the diamonds $D(i,1)$ with $1\le i\le m-2$. Those of the $4\times m$ grid are the diamonds $D(i,1)$ and $D(i,2)$ with $1\le i\le m-2$ and the rectangles $\kA_i,\kB_i,\kC_i,\kE_i$ with $1\le i\le m-3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Each listed set is a rectangle. On three rows $b=d=1$, so $a=c=1$ and $\ell$ is in row $2$. On four rows either $b=d=1$, which gives the diamonds, or $\{b,d\}=\{1,2\}$, $\{a,c\}=\{1,2\}$, and $\ell$ is in row $1+b$. The sides $(2,-2),(1,1)$ give $\kA$, then $(1,-2),(2,1)$ give $\kB$, $(2,-1),(1,2)$ give $\kC$, and $(1,-1),(2,2)$ give $\kE$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[boundary cross]\label{lem:cross}
Let $h,m\ge1$. The first row together with the first column of the $h\times m$ grid is a valid blackout. Hence $f(h,m)\ge h+m-1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $h,m\ge2$. An axis-aligned rectangle on columns $x_1<x_2$ and rows $y_1<y_2$ shows $X\times Y$, where $X=\{x_1,x_2\}\setminus\{1\}$ and $Y=\{y_1,y_2\}\setminus\{1\}$ are nonempty. Since $|X|=1$ forces $x_1=1$ and $|Y|=1$ forces $y_1=1$, the set $X\times Y$ determines the rectangle. By Lemma~\ref{lem:leftmost} a tilted rectangle has at most two black corners, $\ell$ and the topmost one. If both are black, then $\ell=(1,1+b)$ and it shows $p=(1+c,1+b+d)$ and $q=(1+a+c,1+d)$, which determine $a,b,c,d$. Since $p$ and $q$ differ in both coordinates, no axis-aligned rectangle shows $\{p,q\}$. Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners} covers the presentations with three or more points.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[axis-aligned rivals]\label{lem:rival}
Suppose that no axis-aligned rectangle is fully black, as holds when $S$ is axis-valid and $m\ge3$. Call an axis-aligned rectangle that shows exactly a set $P$ of at most two white points a \emph{rival} of $P$. Then the empty set has no rival, and $P$ has a rival if and only if it is a singleton $\{(x,r)\}$ with $\{r,s\}\in F$ for some $s\in B_x$, a vertical pair $\{(x,r),(x,s)\}$ with $\{r,s\}\in F$, a horizontal pair $\{(x,r),(z,r)\}$ with $B_x\cap B_z\ne\emptyset$, or an oblique pair $\{(x,r),(z,s)\}$ with $x\ne z$, $r\ne s$, and $(x,s),(z,r)\in S$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A fully black axis-aligned rectangle is a four-cycle of $G_S$, so the clause after ``as holds when'' is Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}. A rival of a nonempty $P$ has rows $\{r,s\}$ and columns $\{x,z\}$ as in the statement, with its other corners black. This says $s\in B_x$ and $\{r,s\}\subseteq B_z$ for a singleton, $\{r,s\}\subseteq B_z$ for a vertical pair, $s\in B_x\cap B_z$ for a horizontal pair, and $(x,s),(z,r)\in S$ for an oblique pair. Conversely, as $r\notin B_x$, these conditions force $z\ne x$ and $s\ne r$, and the rectangle on rows $\{r,s\}$ and columns $\{x,z\}$ shows exactly $P$.
\end{proof}
\section{Three rows}\label{sec:three}

On three rows the row bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} is attained, and we count the
maximum blackouts as words. Throughout, $m\ge3$, the rows are $1,2,3$ from top to bottom, and the
tilted rectangles are the diamonds $D(i,1)=\{(i,2),(i+1,1),(i+2,2),(i+1,3)\}$ for
$1\le i\le m-2$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:tilted-narrow}).

\begin{theorem}[three rows]\label{thm:threevalue}
For every $m\ge3$, the maximum blackout of the $3\times m$ grid has $m+2$ points, that is,
$f(3,m)=m+2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The upper bound is the case $h=3$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}, since $q_3=2$. The blackout
of row $1$ and column $1$ has $m+2$ points. It is the boundary cross of Lemma~\ref{lem:cross}
for $h=3$, so it is valid.
\end{proof}

From now on every blackout has $m+2$ points. We name the columns $a=\{1\}$, $b=\{2\}$,
$c=\{3\}$, $A=\{1,2\}$, $B=\{1,3\}$, $C=\{2,3\}$ and $X=\{1,2,3\}$, and call $a$ and $c$
\emph{outer}. A blackout with no empty column is its \emph{word} $B_1B_2\cdots B_m$. We name each
pair of $F$ (Section~\ref{sec:conv}) by its double letter.

\begin{proposition}[the two types]\label{prop:threeclass}
Let $m\ge3$ and let $S$ be a blackout of the $3\times m$ grid with $m+2$ points.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item If $S$ is valid, its word has one letter $X$ and singletons elsewhere (type T), or two
different doubles and singletons elsewhere (type P).
\item Conversely, for every word of type T or P the graph $G_S$ is a tree, so no two
axis-aligned rectangles have the same presentation.
\item There are exactly $m^23^{m-1}$ words of type T or P.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) By Lemma~\ref{lem:H}, $|S|=t+e(H)$ with $t\le m$ nonempty columns and $e(H)\le2$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:mantel}). So every column is nonempty and $\sum_i(|B_i|-1)=2$. Two equal doubles form a four-cycle in $G_S$, which
Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} forbids.

(b) The column of $X$, or the two doubles, connect the three rows, and every other column hangs
on one row. So $G_S$ is a tree, and Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} applies. (c) Types T and P give $m3^{m-1}$ and $\binom m2\cdot6\cdot3^{m-2}=m(m-1)3^{m-1}$ words.
\end{proof}

We call the words of type T or P \emph{candidates}. They fall into the triple class $X$ and
the \emph{ordered classes} $AC$, $CA$, $AB$, $BA$, $BC$ and $CB$, named by the doubles from left
to right. The \emph{class} $\{A,C\}$ is $AC\cup CA$, and likewise for $\{A,B\}$ and
$\{B,C\}$. Let $AC_m$ be the set of valid words of width $m$ in $AC$, and likewise for the others.

\begin{lemma}[diamond test]\label{lem:diamond}
Let $S$ be a candidate with pair set $F$. Then $S$ is valid if and only if every diamond
$D(i,1)$ passes the test below, where $P=D(i,1)\setminus S$ is its presentation.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item If $|P|\ge3$ or $P$ is empty, the diamond passes.
\item If $P=\{(i+1,1),(i+1,3)\}$, the diamond fails if and only if $B\in F$.
\item If $P=\{(i,2),(i+2,2)\}$, it fails if and only if $B_i\cap B_{i+2}$ meets $\{1,3\}$.
\item If $P$ is an oblique pair $\{(x,r),(z,s)\}$, it fails if and only if $(x,s),(z,r)\in S$.
\item If $P=\{(x,r)\}$, it fails if and only if $\{r,s\}\in F$ for some $s\in B_x$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Axis-aligned rectangles are told apart (Proposition~\ref{prop:threeclass}(b)), and three points
fix a rectangle (Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners}). Tests (b) to (e) are Lemma~\ref{lem:rival} for a
diamond, since in (b) a rival needs $B\in F$ and in (e) it needs $\{r,s\}\in F$. An empty
diamond has no axis-aligned rival, since $G_S$ has no four-cycle. Two diamonds $D(i,1)$
and $D(j,1)$ with $i<j$ share at most one point, so a common presentation has at most one point.
Then columns $i+1$ and $j+1$ are both black in rows $1$ and $3$, which no candidate allows.
\end{proof}

A \emph{window} is a string of three consecutive letters, at columns $i$, $i+1$ and $i+2$. It
\emph{passes} or \emph{fails} with the diamond $D(i,1)$. We call $(i,2)$ and $(i+2,2)$ the
\emph{tips}. A tip is black exactly when its letter is $b$, $A$, $C$ or $X$.

\begin{lemma}[singleton dichotomy]\label{lem:dichotomy}
Let $S$ be a candidate and let $stu$ be a window of three singletons.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item If $B\notin F$, the window passes.
\item If $B\in F$, the window fails if and only if it is $bab$, $bbb$ or $bcb$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $s=u=b$, the diamond shows the vertical pair (for $t=b$) or one point of column $i+1$ (for
$t$ outer), and tests (b) and (e) fail it exactly when $B\in F$. With one black tip it shows
three corners or an oblique pair whose test (d) needs $(i+1,2)$, which is white. With no black
tip three corners show.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[six of nine in the class $\{A,C\}$]\label{lem:sixofnine}
Let $S$ be a candidate of class $\{A,C\}$, so $F=\{A,C\}$, and let $s,t$ be singletons.
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item A window $sAt$ fails if and only if $st$ is $bb$, $bc$ or $cb$.
\item A window $sCt$ fails if and only if $st$ is $bb$, $ba$ or $ab$.
\item A window with one double, at a tip, passes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) Here $(i+1,1)$ and $(i+1,2)$ are black and $(i+1,3)$ is white. For $s=t=b$ the diamond shows
$(i+1,3)$ alone, and test (e) fails it through $2\in B_{i+1}$ and $C\in F$. For $s=b\ne t$ it
shows $(i+2,2)$ and $(i+1,3)$, and test (d) asks whether $(i+2,3)$ is black, that is, whether
$t=c$. The case $t=b\ne s$ is the mirror image, and with no black tip three corners show.
Part (b) follows by the reflection in the middle row, which swaps $a,c$ and $A,C$.

(c) Let the window be $Ast$, so the tip $(i,2)$ is black. If $t=b$, the diamond shows the
vertical pair or one point of column $i+1$, and both pass by tests (b) and (e), since
$B\notin F$. If $t\ne b$, it shows three corners or an oblique pair whose test (d)
needs $(i+1,2)$ black, and that point is white. Reflection and reversal give the other windows.
\end{proof}

Heuristically, each double of the class $\{A,C\}$ passes for six of its nine neighbor pairs,
and this class holds about a third of the candidates. So about $\frac13\cdot(\frac69)^2=\frac4{27}$
of the candidates are valid (Corollary~\ref{cor:threeasymp}).

By Lemma~\ref{lem:diamond}, for $m\ge3$ the set $AC_m$ consists of the words $UAVCW$ of width
$m$, with $U$, $V$ and $W$ strings of singletons, whose windows all pass the test with
$F=\{A,C\}$. We use the same description for $m=2$, put $G_{AC}(z)=\sum_{m\ge2}|AC_m|z^m$, and define $G_{AB}$ and $G_X$ in the same way
from the words $UAVBW$ and $UXW$, with $F=\{A,B\}$ and $F=\{A,B,C\}$. Put $R=1-3z$. The strings of $n\ge1$ singletons with a prescribed letter
next to a double have the generating function $z/R$.

\begin{proposition}[the class $\{A,C\}$]\label{prop:threeGAC}
The generating function of the ordered class $AC$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GAC}
G_{AC}(z)=\frac{z^2-4z^3+10z^4-8z^5-3z^6}{(1-3z)^3},
\end{equation}
so $|AC_2|=1$, $|AC_3|=5$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GACcoef}
|AC_m|=2(m-1)(3m+2)3^{m-5}\qquad(m\ge4).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Step 1. The windows with both doubles.} In a window $tAC$ the points $(i+1,1)$ and
$(i+2,2)$ are black and $(i+1,3)$ is white. For $t=b$ the diamond shows $(i+1,3)$ alone, and
test (e) fails it. For $t\ne b$ it shows $(i,2)$ and $(i+1,3)$, and test (d) fails it exactly
when $t=c$. By the half turn $ACt$ passes only for $t=c$, and $AtC$ passes by tests (b) and (e).

\emph{Step 2. Adjacent doubles.} In $UACW$ every window passes except possibly the two of
Step~1 (Lemmas~\ref{lem:dichotomy}(a) and~\ref{lem:sixofnine}(c)). So $U$ ends in $a$ and $W$
begins with $c$, if nonempty, and this case contributes $z^2(1+z/R)^2$.

\emph{Step 3. One singleton between.} In $UAtCW$ the window $AtC$ passes. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:sixofnine}(a), the last letter of $U$ has $p_t=3,1,2$ allowed values for
$t=a,b,c$, and by (b) the first letter of $W$ has $q_t=2,1,3$. So this case contributes
$z^3\sum_t(1+p_tz/R)(1+q_tz/R)=z^3(3+12z/R+13z^2/R^2)$, since $\sum p_t=\sum q_t=6$ and
$\sum p_tq_t=13$.

\emph{Step 4. At least two singletons between.} In $UAv_1\cdots v_kCW$ with $k\ge2$ only
$uAv_1$ and $v_kCw$ can fail. If $U$ is empty, $v_1$ is free, and otherwise six of the nine
pairs $(u,v_1)$ pass. So each end contributes $3+6z/R$, the free middle letters give $1/R$, and
this case contributes $z^4(3+6z/R)^2/R=9z^4(1-z)^2/R^3$.

\emph{Step 5. The coefficients.} The three cases sum to~\eqref{eq:GAC}. The coefficient of $z^m$ in $z^kR^{-3}$ is
$\binom{m-k+2}23^{m-k}$ for $m\ge k$ and $0$ otherwise. For $m\ge4$ the five terms with
$k=2,\dots,6$ sum to the polynomial $2(m-1)(3m+2)3^{m-5}$, and at $m=2,3$ they give $1$ and $5$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the number of three-row optima]\label{thm:threecount}
For $m\ge3$ let $E_m$ be the number of maximum blackouts of the $3\times m$ grid whose word is
outside $AC$ and $CA$. Then for every $m\ge4$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:threecount}
a_3(m)=4(m-1)(3m+2)3^{m-5}+E_m.
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Column reversal maps $AC$, $AB$ and $BC$ onto $CA$, $BA$ and $CB$, and the reflection in the
middle row maps $AB$ onto $CB$. Both preserve optima, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:classsum}
a_3(m)=2|AC_m|+4|AB_m|+|X_m|=2|AC_m|+E_m\qquad(m\ge3),
\end{equation}
and~\eqref{eq:GACcoef} gives~\eqref{eq:threecount}.
\end{proof}

A column black in rows $1$ and $3$ is $B$ or $X$, so $E_m$ is as in Theorem~B. At $m=3$,
$a_3(3)=53=2\cdot5+4\cdot7+15$ by Propositions~\ref{prop:GAB} and~\ref{prop:GX}.

\subsection{The generating function}

Put $L=1-2z-2z^2$, $N=1+2z^2$ and $D=LN$.

\begin{theorem}[three-row generating function, parts (b) and (c) computer-assisted]\label{thm:threegf}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item The series $\sum_{m\ge3}a_3(m)z^m$ equals $2G_{AC}+4G_{AB}+G_X-z-12z^2$. Here $G_{AC}$
is~\eqref{eq:GAC}, and $G_{AB}$ and $G_X$ are the rational functions of
Propositions~\ref{prop:GAB} and~\ref{prop:GX}, with denominators dividing $D^3$ and $D^2$.
\item The reduced denominator of the series is exactly $Q=R^3L^3N^3$.
\item With
\begin{align*}
P(z)={}&z-3z^2-34z^3+228z^4-641z^5+1307z^6-2186z^7\\
&+2056z^8-236z^9-1060z^{10}+2584z^{11}-4264z^{12}\\
&-1440z^{13}+704z^{14}-1024z^{15}+2080z^{16}+2176z^{17}+384z^{18},
\end{align*}
we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PQ}
\sum_{m\ge3}a_3(m)z^m=\frac{P(z)}{Q(z)}-z-12z^2.
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(a) Use~\eqref{eq:classsum}. The coefficients $1$ and $12$ of $z$ and $z^2$ count the word $X$,
the six words $sX$ and $Xs$, and the six words of two different doubles.

(b) This is Theorem~\ref{thm:denominator} in Appendix~\ref{app:threegf}. The orders at the
roots of $L$ and $N$ rest on the computer-assisted form~\eqref{eq:PB} of $G_{AB}$.

(c) Clearing denominators in (a) gives $P$, exactly in SymPy
(\nolinkurl{three_row_closed_form.py}). The script \nolinkurl{three_row_closed_form_check.py}
checks~\eqref{eq:PQ} against an independent transfer matrix with $350$ states, in exact integers
through width $100$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[asymptotics]\label{cor:threeasymp}
Let $\rho=1+\sqrt3$. As $m\to\infty$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:asymp1}
a_3(m)=\frac4{81}m^23^m-\frac4{243}(m+2)3^m+O(m^2\rho^m).
\end{equation}
So the valid fraction $a_3(m)/(m^23^{m-1})$ of the candidates is
$\frac4{27}-\frac4{81m}-\frac8{81m^2}+O\bigl((\rho/3)^m\bigr)$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The main term of~\eqref{eq:threecount} is
$4(3m^2-m-2)3^{m-5}=\frac4{81}m^23^m-\frac4{243}(m+2)3^m$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:threegf}(a)
and~\eqref{eq:classsum}, $E_m$ is the coefficient of $z^m$ in $4G_{AB}+G_X$, whose poles are roots of $D$ of order at
most three. The root of least modulus is $1/\rho$, so $E_m=O(m^2\rho^m)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[three-row closed form, computer-assisted exact algebra]\label{thm:threeclosed}
Let $\sigma=1-\sqrt3$. For every $m\ge3$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Eclosed}
E_m=p_+(m)\rho^m+p_-(m)\sigma^m+T_m,
\end{equation}
where $432\,p_\pm(m)=(19\pm11\sqrt3)m^2+(61\pm41\sqrt3)m+104\pm28\sqrt3$ and $T_m=(-2)^{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\,\tau(m)$ with $\tau(m)=(2m^2+41m-104)/216$ for even $m$ and
$\tau(m)=(7m^2-32m-175)/432$ for odd $m$. Hence $E_m\sim\frac{19+11\sqrt3}{432}m^2\rho^m$, and $E_m>0$ for every $m\ge4$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $c_m$ be the coefficient of $z^m$ in $4G_{AB}+G_X$, whose denominator divides $L^3N^3$.
By Theorem~\ref{thm:threegf}(a), $E_m=c_m$ for $m\ge3$. The roots $1/\rho$, $1/\sigma$
and $\pm1/(i\sqrt2)$ of $L^3N^3$ are triple, so beyond a polynomial part of degree one the
$c_m$ have the form~\eqref{eq:Eclosed}, and \nolinkurl{three_row_closed_form.py} finds the
twelve rational coefficients. Both sides satisfy the order-$12$ recurrence of $L^3N^3$
from $m=14$ on, so agreement at $m=2,\dots,13$ proves equality for every $m\ge2$. The asymptotic follows since $|\sigma|<1$
and $|T_m|=O(m^22^{m/2})$. Finally $E_m>0$, since the cross $Xaa\cdots a$ of
Theorem~\ref{thm:threevalue} lies in the triple class.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[optima up to symmetry, computer-assisted]\label{rem:threeorbits}
Up to reflection and reversal there are $15$, $58$, $227$, $864$, $3190$ and $11502$ optima at

\end{remark}
\section{Four rows}\label{sec:four}

On four rows the row bound of Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} is $m+4$, attained for every $m\ge6$. We write $(m)_k=m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)$ and use the tilted rectangles $D(i,1)$, $D(i,2)$, $\kA_i$, $\kB_i$, $\kC_i$, $\kE_i$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:tilted-narrow}.

\begin{theorem}[four rows]\label{thm:fourvalue}
For every $m\ge6$ we have $f(4,m)=m+4$.
\end{theorem}

The upper bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} with $h=4$. For the lower bound we take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourS}
S=\{(i,1):2\le i\le m-1\}\cup\{(m-1,2),(m,2),(1,3),(2,3),(1,4),(m,4)\},
\end{equation}
which has $m+4$ points. The double columns are column $1$ (rows $3,4$), column $2$ (rows $1,3$), column $m-1$ (rows $1,2$), and column $m$ (rows $2,4$). So $G_S$ is the eight-cycle $r_1\,c_2\,r_3\,c_1\,r_4\,c_m\,r_2\,c_{m-1}$ with the columns $c_3,\ldots,c_{m-2}$ hanging off $r_1$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:fourvalue}]
\emph{Step 1. Axis-aligned rectangles.} The graph $G_S$ has no cycle of length four or six. So by Lemma~\ref{lem:girth} the axis-aligned rectangles have distinct presentations, and none is hidden.

\emph{Step 2. The weak tilted rectangles.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:tilted-narrow}, a corner of a tilted rectangle in row $1$ is always black, and one in row $4$ is always white. The other black corners are $(i+2,2)$ for $i\ge m-3$ and $(i+1,3)$ for $i=1$ in $D(i,1)$, and $(i,3)$ for $i\le2$ and $(i+1,2)$ for $i=m-2$ in $D(i,2)$. In $\kA_i$ and $\kB_i$ they are $(i,3)$ for $i\le2$ and $(i+3,2)$ for $i\ge m-4$, and in $\kC_i$ and $\kE_i$ there are none. As $m\ge6$, the two conditions for $D(i,2)$ exclude each other. A rectangle with at most one black corner is safe by Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners}. So only the rectangles below need a check, with $s=1$ for $\kA_i$ and $s=2$ for $\kB_i$.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
rectangle & which $i$ & corners shown\\
\midrule
$D(1,1)$ & & $(1,2),(3,2)$\\
$D(i,1)$ & $i\in\{m-3,m-2\}$ & $(i,2),(i+1,3)$\\
$\kA_i$ or $\kB_i$ & $i\in\{1,2\}$, $i\notin\{m-4,m-3\}$ & $(i+s,4),(i+3,2)$\\
$\kA_i$ or $\kB_i$ & $i\in\{m-4,m-3\}$, $i\notin\{1,2\}$ & $(i,3),(i+s,4)$\\
$\kA_i$ or $\kB_i$ & $i=2$, $m=6$ & $(i+s,4)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\emph{Step 3. Against each other.} The rows of the shown points are $2,2$ and $2,3$ and $4,2$ and $3,4$ in the first four lines, so different lines never agree. Within a line the point in row $2$ or $3$ determines $i$, and the point in row $4$ then determines $s$.

\emph{Step 4. Against axis-aligned rectangles.} A rival of $\{(1,2),(3,2)\}$ needs a row $r$ with $(1,r)$ and $(3,r)$ black, but there is none. In the next three lines the axis-aligned rectangle through the shown points has the white corner $(i,3)$, $(i+s,2)$, or $(i,4)$, respectively, since $i\ge3$, $i+s\le4<m-1$, and $3\le i\le m-3$ there. In the last line $m=6$, and the shown point is $(z,4)$ with $z=i+s\in\{3,4\}$. Column $z$ is black only in row $1$, so a rival needs a column black in rows $1$ and $4$, and there is none. By Steps~1 and~2 no rectangle is hidden, so $S$ is valid.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[equality in the row bound on four rows]\label{lem:fourequality}
Let $m\ge4$. A blackout of the $4\times m$ grid with $m+4$ points is axis-valid if and only if it has the form described next. Exactly four columns have two black points, their row pairs are the edges of a four-cycle on the rows, and every other column has one black point. There are $3\,(m)_4\,4^{m-4}$ such blackouts, which is $72$ and $1{,}440$ for $m=4$ and $5$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
This is Theorem~\ref{thm:equality} with $h=4$. There are three four-cycles on the rows, $(m)_4$ ways to place their labeled edges, and four rows for each other column.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[widths four and five, computer-assisted]\label{cor:fourboundary}
We have $f(4,4)=7$ and $f(4,5)=8$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The lower bounds are Lemma~\ref{lem:cross}. For the upper bounds, the script \texttt{verify\_narrow.py} finds a repeated presentation in each of the $72$ and $1{,}440$ candidates of Lemma~\ref{lem:fourequality}. The brute-force check of Section~\ref{sec:verify} confirms both values without the lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[no hidden rectangle]\label{thm:hidden}
Let $m\ge4$. A valid blackout of the $4\times m$ grid hides no rectangle.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:diff}, two rectangles have the same presentation exactly when the symmetric difference of their corner sets is black. So it suffices to write each corner set as $C(R_1)\triangle C(R_2)$ with $R_1\ne R_2$. For the axis-aligned rectangle on rows $r,s$ and columns $x,y$, take rows $r,s$ with columns $\{x,w\}$ and $\{y,w\}$ for a third column $w$. For $1\le i\le m-3$ let $\mathsf Q_i=\{(i,2),(i,3),(i+3,2),(i+3,3)\}$. Checking corners gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourxor}
\begin{gathered}
\kC_i=\kA_i\,\triangle\,\mathsf Q_i,\qquad \kE_i=\kB_i\,\triangle\,\mathsf Q_i,\\
D(i+1,1)=D(i,2)\,\triangle\,\kA_i,\qquad D(i+1,2)=D(i,1)\,\triangle\,\kE_i,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
and each identity can be solved for each of its three rectangles. The first line handles $\kA_i$, $\kB_i$, $\kC_i$, and $\kE_i$. A diamond $D(j,1)$ or $D(j,2)$ has $j\ge2$ or $j\le m-3$, since $m\ge4$, and the second line with $i=j-1$ or $i=j$ handles it.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis $m\ge4$ is needed, since the valid blackout $\{(1,2),\allowbreak(2,1),\allowbreak(3,2),\allowbreak(2,3)\}$ of the $4\times3$ grid hides $D(1,1)$ (checked by computer).

Let $m\ge6$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:fourequality} an optimum has four double columns, whose pairs form a four-cycle $F$ on the rows, and singletons elsewhere. We name $F$ by its parts, $\{1,2\}\mid\{3,4\}$, $\{1,3\}\mid\{2,4\}$, or $\{1,4\}\mid\{2,3\}$. A \emph{block} is a run of four consecutive singleton columns, and its \emph{word} lists their black rows. The \emph{template words} $2413$, $3412$, $2143$, and $3142$ are the corner rows of $\kC_x$, $\kA_x$, $\kE_x$, and $\kB_x$. A \emph{swap word} exchanges two letters of a template word, for example $4213$. An \emph{edit word for $F$} replaces one letter $r$ of a template word by a row $s$ with $\{r,s\}\in F$.

\begin{lemma}[forbidden blocks]\label{lem:blocks}
Let $S$ be a valid blackout of the $4\times m$ grid with $m+4$ points and pair set $F$, where $m\ge6$. No block of $S$ has a swap word or an edit word for $F$. There are $20$ swap words, and $24$, $24$, or $32$ edit words for the three pair sets, none of them a swap word. So at most $212$ of the $256$ words of a block are allowed.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let the block start in column $x$, and let $T$ be one of $\kC_x$, $\kA_x$, $\kE_x$, $\kB_x$, with template word $t_1t_2t_3t_4$. In both steps an axis-aligned rectangle has the same presentation as $T$.
\emph{Step 1. Swap words.} If the letters in positions $p\ne q$ are exchanged, $T$ shows exactly $(x+p-1,t_p)$ and $(x+q-1,t_q)$, and so does the axis-aligned rectangle on these two points. The template words are the permutations with both end letters in $\{2,3\}$. Exchanging two end letters, or two middle letters, gives a template word again, and exchanging an end letter with a middle letter gives every permutation with exactly one end letter in $\{2,3\}$. These are the $20$ swap words.

\emph{Step 2. Edit words.} If $t_p=r$ is replaced by $s$ with $\{r,s\}\in F$, then $T$ shows only $(x+p-1,r)$. So does the rectangle on rows $r,s$ and the columns $x+p-1$ and $d$, where $d$ is the double column black in rows $r,s$. Each template word has $8$ edit words.

Two template words share an edit word only if they differ in two positions, carrying letters $r,s$ in opposite orders, and then they share two if $\{r,s\}\in F$. This happens for $2413,3412$ and $2143,3142$ with $\{r,s\}=\{2,3\}$, and for $2413,2143$ and $3412,3142$ with $\{r,s\}=\{1,4\}$. So there are $32-4[\{2,3\}\in F]-4[\{1,4\}\in F]$ edit words. They repeat a letter, so they are not swap words.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[forbidden-block bound]\label{thm:foursurvival}
For every $m\ge6$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:survival}
a_4(m)\le3\,(m)_4\,4^{m-4}\,(53/64)^k=O\bigl(m^4\,212^{m/4}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $k=\max(0,\lfloor m/4\rfloor-4)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix $F$, the double columns, and their pairs, in one of $3\,(m)_4$ ways. Of the $\lfloor m/4\rfloor$ groups of columns $4k-3,\ldots,4k$, at most four contain a double column. So at least $k$ are blocks, each with at most $212=4^4\cdot53/64$ words by Lemma~\ref{lem:blocks}. Every other column has at most $4$ values, which gives \eqref{eq:survival}.
\end{proof}

So the valid fraction of the candidates tends to zero (compare Corollary~\ref{cor:threeasymp}).

\begin{remark}[six columns of memory, proof omitted]\label{rem:fourmemory}
Fix a pair set $F$. A finite automaton reads a blackout column by column, remembers its last six columns and the pairs of $F$ already used, and accepts exactly the optima with pair set $F$. So their generating function is rational (Flajolet and Sedgewick~\cite[Section~V.5]{flajolet2009}). The proof uses Lemma~\ref{lem:rival} and Theorem~\ref{thm:hidden}, and we omit it (see the repository notes~\cite{repo}).
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[the four-row generating function, computer-assisted]\label{thm:fourgfexact}
There are coprime integer polynomials $P_4=26z^6-826z^7+\cdots$ of degree $444$ and $Q_4=\sum_{j=0}^{420}q_jz^j$ of degree $420$ with $q_0=1$ and $\sum_{m\ge6}a_4(m)z^m=P_4/Q_4$. The recurrence $\sum_jq_j\,a_4(n-j)=0$ holds for every $n\ge445$ and fails at $n=444$. Over the integers
\[
Q_4=(1-z)^2(1+z)^2\,g_5^3\,\tilde g_5^3\,g_6^3\,g_{11}^5\,g_{17}^3\,g_{19}^3\,g_{20}^5\,g_{21}^5,
\]
where $g_d$ and $\tilde g_d$ are irreducible of degree $d$ with constant term $1$, and all of $g_{20}$ comes from the pair set $\{1,3\}\mid\{2,4\}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The Berlekamp--Massey algorithm modulo eight primes, run on the values $a_4(m)$ for $m\le1500$ from the automaton of Proposition~\ref{prop:fourvalues}, gives $P_4$ and $Q_4$. With a register of used pairs the automaton has a transfer matrix $T_F$ with $6{,}128$, $7{,}792$, or $4{,}912$ states. Its state vectors $v(n)=v(0)T_F^n$ satisfy $\sum_jq_jv(n_F-j)=0$ at $n_F=436$, $435$, and $445$. Multiplying by powers of $T_F$ gives the recurrence for every $n\ge445$, and $\deg P_4=444$ forces the failure at $n=444$. We factor $Q_4$ in SymPy, and \texttt{four\_row\_\allowbreak recurrence.json} lists the factors.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[growth, computer-assisted]\label{cor:fourgrowth}
Let $\rho_4=3.351996777310\ldots$ be the largest real root of the irreducible polynomial $p(x)=x^{20}g_{20}(1/x)=x^{20}-4x^{19}+2x^{18}+\cdots-26x^2-4x+8$. Then $a_4(m)\sim c\,m^4\rho_4^m$ with $c=8.1611\times10^{-5}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The script \texttt{four\_row\_root\_isolation.py} isolates the complex roots of every factor of $Q_4$ in rational rectangles, in exact arithmetic in SymPy. The only root of modulus below $0.341$ is the positive root $1/\rho_4=0.29832\ldots$ of $g_{20}$, which is simple in $g_{20}$ and so has order five in $Q_4$. It is not a root of $P_4$, since the two are coprime. So $c$ is the coefficient of $(1-\rho_4 z)^{-5}$ in the partial fraction divided by $4!$ (Flajolet and Sedgewick~\cite[Section~IV.5]{flajolet2009}), computed to high precision.
\end{proof}
\section{Five and six rows}\label{sec:five}

One finite padding test settles every large width on five and on six rows.

\subsection{A finite test for boundary padding}

For a blackout $S$ of the $h\times m$ grid and $L,R\ge0$, let $S_{L,R}$ add $L$ columns on
the left and $R$ on the right. They are the columns $1-L,\dots,0$ and $m+1,\dots,m+R$. Each
is black exactly in the \emph{padding row}, which is row $1$ or row $h$. As before, $W_h$ is
the span bound of Lemma~\ref{lem:span}.

\begin{theorem}[finite collar]\label{thm:collar}
Let $h\ge3$ and $m\ge1$, and let $S$ be a blackout of the $h\times m$ grid. Fix the padding
row and choose the left side, the right side, or both. Call $(L,R)$ \emph{admissible} if
$L=0$ when the left side is not chosen and $R=0$ when the right side is not chosen. Put
$L^*=W_h$ if the left side is chosen and $L^*=0$ otherwise, and define $R^*$ in the same
way. Then $S_{L,R}$ is valid for every admissible $(L,R)$ if and only if $S_{L^*,R^*}$ is
valid.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $S^*=S_{L^*,R^*}$. If every admissible padding is valid, then so is $S^*$.
Conversely, let $S^*$ be valid and
$U=S_{L,R}$ with $(L,R)$ admissible. By Lemma~\ref{lem:leftmost} the corners of a tilted
rectangle lie in the rows $j$, $j-b$, $j+d$, $j+d-b$ with $b,d\ge1$, so at most one lies in
row $1$ and at most one in row $h$. Hence a weak tilted rectangle of $U$ has a black corner in an
original column $e$, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:span} its columns lie in
$[e-W_h,e+W_h]\cap[1-L,m+R]\subseteq[1-L^*,m+R^*]$. Since $U$ and $S^*$ agree on their common
columns, it is a rectangle of $S^*$ with the same presentation. Suppose that two distinct
rectangles $R_1$ and $R_2$ of $U$ have the same presentation $P$.

\emph{Step 1. Both axis-aligned.} The graph $G_U$ is $G_S$ plus one vertex of degree one for
each added column, so its cycles are cycles of $G_{S^*}$. Since $S^*$ is valid with at least
three columns, neither graph has a cycle of length four or six, and $U$ is axis-valid
(Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}).

\emph{Step 2. Both tilted.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:threecorners}, $|P|\le2$, so both are weak and
are rectangles of $S^*$ with the same presentation, which is impossible.

\emph{Step 3. One of each.} Let $R_1$ be tilted, so it is a rectangle of $S^*$. A column of
$R_2$ with a point of $P$ is a column of $R_1$, and one without has two black corners and is
an original column. So $R_2$ is a rectangle of $S^*$ too, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[the padding row has to be a boundary row]\label{rem:interior}
Take $h=3$, $B_1=B_2=\{1\}$, $B_3=\{1,3\}$, and the interior padding row $2$. The added
columns $0$ and $-1$ keep the board valid, but $-2$ breaks it. The diamond $(-2,2)$,
$(-1,1)$, $(0,2)$, $(-1,3)$ and the rectangle on columns $-1$, $3$ and rows $1$, $3$ both
show $\{(-1,1),(-1,3)\}$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Five rows}

\begin{theorem}[five rows, computer-assisted]\label{thm:fiverow}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]

\item We have $f(5,m)=m+6$ for every $m\ge8$, attained by $S_8$ followed by $m-8$ columns black in

(b) The upper bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:collar} to $S_8$

(a) The lower bounds are $S_0$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:fivefive} below for $m=5$ and the stored

(c) Among the $10\,(8)_6\,5^2=5{,}040{,}000$ candidates of Theorem~\ref{thm:equality}, two

\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item For $8\le m\le14$ we have $f(6,m)\le m+8$.
\item For every $m\ge15$ we have $f(6,m)=m+9$.

partition. A column search for $m\le13$ and a third encoder for $m=13$ and $m=14$ give a third
check at every width.

(b) The upper bound is Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound}. A valid blackout of $24$ points on the

presentation among its $4{,}627$ rectangles.

\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[few edges without short cycles]\label{lem:tenvertices}
Let $G$ be a finite simple bipartite graph with $v$ vertices, $1\le v\le10$, and with no cycle
of length four or six. Then $G$ has at most $v$ edges. If it has exactly $v$ edges, then $G$ is
connected and has exactly one cycle, of length $8$ or $10$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Every cycle of $G$ has length at least $8$. Suppose a component has more edges than
vertices. Removing vertices of degree one with their edges until none is left gives a connected
$H$ with more edges than vertices and minimum degree two, so some vertex $x$ of $H$ has degree
at least three. Let $D_k$ be the set of vertices at distance $k$ from $x$. Two paths of length
at most $3$ from $x$ to one vertex would close a cycle of length at most $6$. So for $k=1,2,3$
every vertex of $D_k$ has exactly one neighbor in $D_{k-1}$ and none in $D_k$. Its degree is
at least two, so it also has a neighbor in $D_{k+1}$. For $k\le2$ no other vertex of $D_k$
shares that neighbor. Hence $|D_1|\ge3$, $|D_3|\ge|D_2|\ge|D_1|$ and $|D_4|\ge1$, at least
$11$ vertices in all. So $G$ has at most $v$ edges, and with exactly $v$ edges every
component has one cycle. Two disjoint cycles of length at least $8$ need $16$ vertices, so $G$ is connected.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the $5\times5$ grid]\label{thm:fivefive}
\begin{enumerate}[(a)]
\item We have $f(5,5)=10$, and $G_S$ is connected with exactly one cycle for every optimum $S$.
\item (Computer-assisted.) There are exactly $a_5(5)=8$ maximum blackouts, the eight distinct
images of $S_0$ below under the symmetries of the square.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Upper bound.} If $S$ is valid, $G_S$ has $10$ vertices, $|S|$ edges and no cycle of
length four or six (Lemma~\ref{lem:girth}), so Lemma~\ref{lem:tenvertices} applies.

\emph{Lower bound.} Let $S_0$ be the staircase of nine points plus the corner $(5,5)$ with
$B_1=\{4,5\}$, $B_2=\{3,4\}$, $B_3=\{1,2,3\}$, $B_4=\{1\}$, and $B_5=\{1,5\}$. Its graph is an $8$-cycle with two pendant
edges, so $S_0$ is axis-valid. By Lemma~\ref{lem:leftmost} the $30$ tilted rectangles among
the $130$ are the nine unit diamonds, the four kinds of Lemma~\ref{lem:tilted-narrow} in four
positions each, one large diamond, and four that fill the grid. Exactly fifteen are weak. The
table lists the two corners each one shows and, after a bar for an oblique pair, the white
corner of its axis-aligned rectangle.

\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}

$(2,2),(4,2)$ & $(1,2),(4,3)\mid(1,3)$ & $(4,4),(5,2)\mid(4,2)$ & $(3,5),(4,4)\mid(3,4)$ \\

\section{Every fixed height}\label{sec:height}

Fix $h$. A blackout of the $h\times m$ grid is a \emph{column word} $w=B_1B_2\cdots B_m$ over the subsets of $\{1,\dots,h\}$, with length $|w|$ and blackout $S(w)$. Let $\mathcal L_h$ be the set of words with a valid blackout, the empty word included. A \emph{factor} of $w$ is a word $B_iB_{i+1}\cdots B_j$.

\begin{proposition}[validity is not a window property]\label{prop:nonlocal}
Let $g\ge1$. The blackout $S=\{(1,2),(3,2),(g+3,1),(g+3,3)\}$ of the $3\times(g+3)$ grid is not valid, but every factor of at most $g$ columns is valid.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The diamond $D(1,1)$ and the rectangle on columns $2,g+3$ and rows $1,3$ both show $\{(2,1),(2,3)\}$, while a factor of at most $g$ columns has at most two black points and two different rectangles differ in at least four corners (Lemmas~\ref{lem:diff} and~\ref{lem:threecorners}).
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[regularity and rational series]\label{thm:regular}
For every $h\ge1$ the language $\mathcal L_h$ is regular, and $G_h(z,u)=\sum_{w\in\mathcal L_h}z^{|w|}u^{|S(w)|}$ is rational in $z$ and $u$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The \emph{trace} of a rectangle $R$ is the word whose $i$th letter is the set of rows of its corners in column $i$, and it determines $C(R)$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:diff}, $w\notin\mathcal L_h$ exactly when two different traces $Y,Y'$ of length $|w|$ have $Y_i\triangle Y'_i\subseteq B_i$ for every $i$. The traces form a regular language, $\emptyset^*\{r,s\}\emptyset^*\{r,s\}\emptyset^*$ for axis-aligned rectangles and, by Lemmas~\ref{lem:leftmost} and~\ref{lem:span}, $\emptyset^*Y_0Y_1\cdots Y_n\emptyset^*$ for finitely many templates \eqref{eq:tilt-param} of span $n\le W_h$. So a finite automaton that reads $w$ and two traces in parallel recognizes the complement of $\mathcal L_h$, and $\mathcal L_h$ is regular. With the weight $u^{|B|}$ on a letter $B$, the transfer matrix $M(u)$ of a deterministic automaton for $\mathcal L_h$ gives $G_h=\alpha(I-zM(u))^{-1}\beta$, rational by Cramer's rule \cite[Proposition~I.3]{flajolet2009}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[fixed height]\label{thm:periodic}
Let $h\ge2$. (a) There are integers $m_0\ge3$, $p\ge1$, and $k_0,\dots,k_{p-1}$ such that $f(h,m)=m+k_{m\bmod p}$ for every $m\ge m_0$. (b) The series $\sum_{m\ge0}a_h(m)z^m$ is rational, with the conventions of Section~\ref{sec:conv}, and the words of optimum blackouts form a regular language.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $E(w)=|S(w)|-|w|$. Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} and $|S(v)|\le h|v|$ bound the excess of every factor of a valid word by $C_h=\max(q_h,2h-2)$. So a counter for the excess of prefixes, kept in $[k-C_h,C_h]$, gives an automaton for the valid words $\mathcal L_{h,k}$ of excess $k$, and their lengths are eventually periodic. For $m\ge3$, Lemma~\ref{lem:cross} and Theorem~\ref{thm:rowbound} make $f(h,m)-m$ the largest $k$ from $h-1$ to $q_h$ with a word of length $m$ in $\mathcal L_{h,k}$, which gives (a). The optimum words are those of $\mathcal L_{h,k_r}$ of lengths $m\ge m_0$ with $m\equiv r\pmod p$, and finitely many shorter ones, so (b) follows as in Theorem~\ref{thm:regular}.
\end{proof}

Guilhot, Little, and Parkinson \cite[Theorem~1(3)]{glp2025} prove that the words of maximal weight in a regular language form a regular language for an additive weight bounded above. This does not give (b), since we maximize at each width separately. Longer proofs are in \nolinkurl{jis/removed/sec08_height.tex} of the repository~\cite{repo}.
\section{Computations and verification}\label{sec:verify}

The results that rest on a computation are labeled computer-assisted. Every other result in
Sections~\ref{sec:conv} to~\ref{sec:height} has a written proof, unless a remark says that
its proof is sketched or omitted. Only Remark~\ref{rem:fourmemory} of these is used later,
in the design of a count. Every computed value and count comes from at least two programs
written separately, which agree wherever both ran. Before most computations a prediction went
into the ledger \nolinkurl{kagey-problems/problem001/data/ledger.md}, and several were wrong,
among them
$f(5,5)=9$, $f(4,8)=13$, $f(5,8)=13$, and $f(6,8)=14$.

All programs are in Python~3.14.3. The ILP program \texttt{solver/engine.py} finds a smallest
hitting set for the difference sets of Lemma~\ref{lem:diff} with CBC through
PuLP~\cite{pulp}. It took $723$ seconds on the $5\times7$ grid and $1{,}063$ seconds on the
$6\times6$ grid. The brute-force check \texttt{solver/brute.py} uses no lemma and shares no
code with it. The verifier \texttt{verify\_narrow.py} checks the lemmas and constructions of
Sections~\ref{sec:graph} to~\ref{sec:four} and the $5\times5$ grid in $21$ seconds. The
blackouts of $h+m$ points on the grids $5\times6$, $5\times7$, $6\times6$, $6\times7$, and
$7\times7$ are stored in \nolinkurl{jis/data/small_cells_sat_*.json}. The SAT solvers are
CaDiCaL~1.5.3 and~1.9.5, Glucose~3, 4, and~4.2, MiniSat~2.2, MapleChrono, and Lingeling from
pysat~1.9.dev15, and the algebra uses SymPy~1.14.

A SAT instance asks for a valid blackout of at least $k$ points, so by the observation after
Corollary~\ref{cor:hitting} an unsatisfiable instance proves $f(h,m)<k$. Encoding A has a
variable for each point and a clause ``not all black'' for each inclusion-minimal symmetric
difference of two rectangles, with a sequential counter for the size. Encoding B has a clause
for each pair of rectangles that share a corner and an indicator for each hidden rectangle, at
most one of them true, with a totalizer for the size. The third encoding,
\texttt{cells\_sat\_check.py}, is of type A but written from scratch. The structured encoders of
Section~\ref{sec:five} add the column structure of Theorem~\ref{thm:equality} for one row
partition and keep only the clauses that involve a tilted rectangle. The collar test boards of
Section~\ref{sec:five} are checked by three separately written enumerators, which list every
rectangle of the board and compare the presentations. The column searches use no solver. They place columns from left to right and stop
when two rectangles inside the placed columns show the same points, which is sound since every
interval of columns of a valid blackout is valid. We did not produce DRAT proofs, and rely on
the agreement of independent encodings on different solvers.

The scripts in \nolinkurl{jis/scripts} form five groups, by the prefixes
\texttt{three\_row}, \texttt{four\_row}, \texttt{five\_row}, \texttt{six\_row}, and

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}

\begin{enumerate}[(1),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{Five-row counts.} The series $\sum_{m\ge0}a_5(m)z^m$ is rational by
Theorem~\ref{thm:periodic}. Find it, and the growth rate of $a_5(m)$ (Remark~\ref{rem:fivecounts}).
\item \emph{Six rows below width $15$.} By the stored witnesses
\nolinkurl{jis/data/small_cells_sat_6x9_b16.json} and \nolinkurl{small_cells_sat_6x10_b17.json},
the bound $f(h,m)\ge h+m-1$,
and Theorem~\ref{thm:sixrow}, $f(6,9)$ is $16$ or $17$, $f(6,10)$ is $17$ or $18$, and $m+5\le f(6,m)\le m+8$ for
$11\le m\le14$. What are these values?
\item \emph{Seven and more rows.} Is the row bound $m+q_h$ attained at every large width for
every $h\ge7$? By Theorem~\ref{thm:collar}, one valid $7\times m$ blackout of $m+12$ points
that passes the collar test would settle $h=7$.
\item \emph{Squares.} For some $c>0$ and all large $n$,
$c\,n^{6/5}(1+\ln n)^{-2/5}\le f(n,n)=O(n^{4/3})$ by an alteration
argument~\cite[Section~3.2]{alonspencer2016} (proof omitted, see
\nolinkurl{jis/removed/appC_square.tex}) and by Neuwirth~\cite[Corollary~16]{neuwirth2001}.
What is the true order, and the least $n$ with $f(n,n)>2n$?
\item \emph{Proofs by hand.} Is there a proof without a computer that the $5\times5$ optimum
is unique up to symmetry, or that the four-row generating function has a dominant pole of
order five?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Three-row generating functions}\label{app:threegf}

We finish the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:threegf}, with $D=LN=1-2z-4z^3-4z^4$. A
\emph{restricted run} is a string of singletons with no two letters $b$ at distance two.
Reversal and reflection carry the next lemma to $BA$, $BC$ and $CB$.

\begin{lemma}[forbidden windows in the classes containing $B$]\label{lem:threewindows}
Let $S$ be a candidate in the ordered class $AB$ or in the triple class. A window fails if
and only if the table lists it for its class, where $s$ is any singleton.
\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
class & windows & forbidden \\
\midrule
$AB$, $X$ & singletons only & $bab$, $bbb$, $bcb$ \\
$AB$ & one double & $bsA$, $Asb$, $bAb$, $bAc$, $cAb$, $aBa$, $aBb$, $bBa$, $cBc$ \\
 & two doubles & $bAB$, $ABa$ \\
$X$ & one triple & $bsX$, $Xsb$, $aXa$, $aXb$, $bXa$, $bXc$, $cXb$, $cXc$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The tests (a) to (e) are those of Lemma~\ref{lem:diamond}, and
Lemma~\ref{lem:dichotomy}(b) gives the singleton windows. A window $sAt$ behaves as in
Lemma~\ref{lem:sixofnine}(a), except that test (e) also fails $bAb$, by $1\in B_{i+1}$ and
$B\in F$. In $sBt$ and $sXt$ column $i+1$ is black. The diamond is empty if $s=t=b$. If
$s=b\ne t$ it shows $(i+2,2)$ alone and fails by test (e) exactly when $\{2,t\}\in F$, and
symmetrically for $t=b\ne s$. If $s$ and $t$ are outer it shows the horizontal pair and fails
by test (c) exactly when $s=t$. In $uAB$ the diamond fails by test (d) for $u=b$, since
$(i+2,3)$ and $(i+1,2)$ are black, and shows three corners otherwise. In $ABw$ it is empty for
$w=b$ and otherwise fails by test (e) exactly for $w=a$. In $Asb$ and $Xsb$ it shows the
vertical pair or one point of column $i+1$, and tests (b) and (e) fail it as $B\in F$. Every
other window with a double or $X$ at the left tip shows three corners or an oblique pair whose
test (d) needs the white point $(i+1,2)$, so it passes. Reversal gives the right tip.
\end{proof}

We count restricted runs by a transfer on the last two bits. An outer letter has the bit $0$
and the weight $2$, and $b$ has the bit $1$. Let $h_{ij}$ count, by $z^n$, the continuations of
length $n\ge0$ of a restricted run ending in the bits $i,j$. Since $b$ is forbidden after $1,j$, $h_{00}=1+z(2h_{00}+h_{01})$, $h_{01}=1+z(2h_{10}+h_{11})$, $h_{10}=1+2zh_{00}$ and
$h_{11}=1+2zh_{10}$, and eliminating gives $Dh_{00}=1+z+3z^2+2z^3$. Read runs $w_1\cdots w_n$
with $n\ge1$ outward from a double. Let $g_0$ count those with $w_1$ a prescribed outer letter and
$g_1$ those with $w_1=b$. Let $u$ and $v$ count the same runs with $w_2$, if present, outer. A fictitious outer letter before $w_1$ restricts nothing, so $g_0=zh_{00}$,
$g_1=zh_{01}$, $u=z+2z^2h_{00}=zh_{10}$ and $v=z+2z^2h_{10}=zh_{11}$. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runs}
Dg_0=z(1+z+3z^2+2z^3),\ \ Dg_1=z(1+z),\ \ Du=z(1+2z^2+2z^3),\ \ Dv=z.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[the triple class]\label{prop:GX}
In the notation of~\eqref{eq:runs}, $G_X(z)=z\bigl(1+4u+2v+2u^2+v^2\bigr)$ has a denominator
dividing $D^2$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In $UXW$ the runs are restricted, and $bsX$ and $Xsb$ make the second letters from $X$ outer
(Lemma~\ref{lem:threewindows}). So one nonempty run gives $2u+v$ on either side. With
two, the window at $X$ allows only the neighbor pairs $(a,c)$, $(b,b)$ and $(c,a)$, which give $2u^2+v^2$.
\end{proof}

A word of $AB$ is $UAVBW$ with restricted runs $U$, $V$ and $W$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:threewindows}, a nonempty $U$ read from $A$ has an outer second letter and is
counted by $u$ or $v$, and a nonempty $W$ read from $B$ is counted by $g_0$ or $g_1$. Let
$\ell_t$ and $r_t$ count the allowed $U$ and $W$, the empty one included, when a singleton $t$
stands right of $A$ or left of $B$. The windows with the
double in the middle give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lr}
(\ell_a,\ell_b,\ell_c)=(1+2u+v,1+u,1+2u),\ (r_a,r_b,r_c)=(1+g_0,1+g_0+g_1,1+g_0+g_1).
\end{equation}
For $s\in\{00,10\}$ and a bit $\beta$ let $J_{s\beta}$ count, as for $h$, the continuations
from $s$ whose last letter has the bit $\beta$. Solving as for $h$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:J}
J=\frac1D\begin{pmatrix}1+2z^2+2z^3&z+z^2\\1&2z^2+2z^3\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[the class $\{A,B\}$, polynomial form computer-assisted]\label{prop:GAB}
In the notation of~\eqref{eq:runs} to~\eqref{eq:J},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GAB}
\begin{aligned}
G_{AB}(z)={}&z^2(1+2u)(1+g_0+g_1)+z^3(\ell_ar_a+\ell_br_b+\ell_cr_c)\\
&+z^4\,(\ell_a+\ell_c,\ \ell_b)\,J\,(r_a+r_c,\ 2r_b)^{T}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Moreover $G_{AB}=P_B/D^3$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:PB}
P_B(z)=z^2(1+z)(1+z^2-3z^3+4z^6+4z^7).
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The runs cover the windows inside them, and we split by the length $k$ of $V$. For $k=0$ the windows $uAB$ and $ABw$ require $u\ne b$ and
$w\ne a$, which gives the first term. For $k=1$ the window $AtB$ passes, and $uAt$ and $tBw$
give $\ell_t$ and $r_t$. For $k\ge2$ the window $Av_1v_2$ makes $v_2$ outer, $v_{k-1}v_kB$ passes,
and the weight is $\ell_{v_1}r_{v_k}$. After $v_2$ the bits are $00$ or $10$ as $v_1$ is outer or
$b$, and the rest is a continuation in which $v_2$ keeps its factor $2$ for $k\ge3$. Summing
over the last letter gives $J_{s0}(r_a+r_c)+J_{s1}\,2r_b$, and $k=2$ is the constant term of $J$.

The script \nolinkurl{three_row_closed_form.py} finds~\eqref{eq:PB} in SymPy. Its first coefficients $7$, $31$, $120$, $449$ and $1622$ match
\nolinkurl{three_row_definition_check.json}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[the exact denominator, computer-assisted]\label{thm:denominator}
The reduced denominator of $\sum_{m\ge3}a_3(m)z^m$ is $Q=R^3L^3N^3$, with five triple poles.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{thm:threegf}(a) the denominator divides $R^3D^3=Q$. The factors $R$, $L$ and
$N$ are coprime, since $L-N=-2z-4z^2$ vanishes only at $0$ and $-\frac12$, where $N\ne0$, and
$L$ and $N$ are positive at $\frac13$. The numerator in~\eqref{eq:GAC} is $\frac4{81}$ at
$z=\frac13$, where $G_{AB}$ and $G_X$ are analytic. Division gives $P_B\bmod L=\frac z2$ and
$P_B\bmod N=\frac14-\frac z2$, so $P_B$ vanishes at no root of $L$ or $N$. At these roots $G_{AB}$ has a
pole of order three, $G_X$ one of order at most two, and $G_{AC}$ none.
\end{proof}
